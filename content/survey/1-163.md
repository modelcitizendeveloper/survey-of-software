**What the research found**

- **chardet 7.7.0 read back 94.9% of 4,086 legacy-encoding samples, against 79.6% for charset-normalizer 3.5.2, 62.6% for cchardet 2.4.0 and 60.8% for chardet 5.2.0 on the same samples; the order held on both Wikipedia and Tatoeba text (measured 2026-11-05; harness/1-163-character-encoding/results/summary.json)** — The rule 'charset-normalizer for accuracy, cchardet for speed' no longer describes the field. Pin chardet 7 and re-check on upgrade; speed was not measured.
- **A detector's returned name is not a Python codec name: cchardet, faust-cchardet, uchardet and ICU scored 0.0% on Hong Kong Cantonese and code page 932 extension samples when their BIG5 and SHIFT_JIS answers were decoded by Python's codecs; read the way browsers read the labels, every detector rises (cchardet 62.6% to 77.1%, ICU 55.4% to 67.9%, chardetng 78.5% to 96.7%, chardet 7 94.9% to 95.0%) (measured 2026-11-05)** — Translate a detector's answer to a codec that can read the extensions, or try the widest sibling (big5hkscs, cp932, gb18030, cp949) before accepting a failure.
- **Short input separates the detectors: at 5 characters chardet 7.7.0 read 87.5% correctly, charset-normalizer 47.4%, cchardet 29.6% and ICU 11.5%; chardetng read as WHATWG names 89.1% (measured 2026-11-05)** — Detect per source rather than per string, on pooled text, wherever the input is a filename, a subject line or a database field.
- **Input that ends inside a character removed charset-normalizer's answer and turned chardetng's into windows-1252 on all 58 buffers cut inside a character (16 articles, four two-byte codecs, cuts of up to three bytes); chardet 7, chardet 5.2.0, cchardet, faust-cchardet, uchardet and ICU gave the same answer as on whole characters (measured 2026-11-05)** — Trim incomplete trailing bytes before detecting on a prefix of a file.
- **Python's gb18030 codec does not follow GB 18030-2022 (in force since 2023-08-01): eleven probed sequences decode to private-use characters or swapped targets where glibc iconv gives the 2022 characters, identically on Python 3.12.3, 3.13.15 and 3.14.7 (measured 2026-11-05)** — A producer on the 2022 edition decodes without error to wrong characters in Python; transcode with glibc iconv or another implementation of the 2022 mapping.


**What the research recommends**

**Default** — Convert to UTF-8 once at the boundary and keep the source bytes. When the producer is known, decode with its codec under strict and widen the codec on failure (big5, cp950, big5hkscs; shift_jis, cp932; gb2312, gbk, gb18030). When the encoding is unknown, use chardet 7 pinned to an exact version, pool strings from one source before detecting, trim incomplete trailing bytes, and verify the answer with a strict decode.

| Persona | Pick |
|---|---|
| known producer migration | The producer's codec under strict, widened on failure; keep source bytes and the codec name; search the output for U+FFFD and private-use characters. |
| unknown uploads | UTF-8 check, then chardet 7, then strict decode with the widest sibling, then a user-visible preview. |
| web pages | The declared charset through the WHATWG label table, then chardetng (allow_utf8, labels as WHATWG) or chardet 7 when it is wrong. |
| brief inputs | Detect per source on pooled text; do not detect per string. |
| repairing mojibake | ftfy.fix_encoding for UTF-8 read as a single-byte encoding; reverse the known pair with the codecs for everything else; never fix_text on CJK. |
| gb18030 interchange | gb18030 under strict plus glibc iconv for the 2022 mapping, compared on a partner file containing FE59 and A6D9. |
| hong kong content | big5hkscs first; chardet 7 or charset-normalizer if detecting. |
| two scripts | OpenCC s2twp for phrase conventions or zhconv-rs for Wikipedia's; store the original script. |

# Character Encoding (Chinese, Japanese and Korean Focus)

## What problem does this solve?

An encoding is the agreement between bytes (how a computer stores text) and characters (what a person reads).
Chinese, Japanese and Korean (CJK) text was stored for decades in encodings that each cover one language or region, and a file
carries no reliable record of which one it uses. Four separate jobs follow from that:

1. **Transcoding** - the encoding is known; turn the bytes into text and back.
2. **Detection** - the encoding is unknown; guess it from the bytes.
3. **Repair** - the text was already decoded with the wrong encoding; undo the damage.
4. **Variant conversion** - Traditional and Simplified Chinese are different character sets; convert between them. This looks
   like an encoding problem and is not one.

Most failures in this area come from using a tool for one job on another: running a detector on a file whose encoding is
printed in its header, running a repair tool on text that was never damaged, or treating a Big5 file as "Traditional
Chinese" and a GB file as "Simplified Chinese" as if the encoding decided the script.

## Why this matters

Modern software works in Unicode internally and UTF-8 on the wire. W3Techs reports UTF-8 on 99.1% of websites in November
2026 (https://w3techs.com/technologies/overview/character_encoding, accessed 2026-11-05), and each legacy East Asian
encoding appears on fewer than 0.1%. The old encodings have not gone away; they sit in files, databases, mail archives,
exports from older systems and interchange formats. The pattern is to decode bytes at the boundary, work with Unicode
strings inside, and encode on the way out.

Situations where the boundary is hard:

- A bank, a registry or a government office exports files in an encoding that its documentation names loosely ("Big5",
  "GB 2312") but that is really a vendor extension.
- A user uploads a file that claims one encoding and uses another.
- A web page declares a charset in a header or a meta tag and the body disagrees.
- A database connection decodes with one encoding and the table stores another.

## The CJK encoding landscape

### Big5 (Traditional Chinese, Taiwan and Hong Kong)

A 1984 industry encoding of about 13,000 Traditional characters. It was never one standard: vendors and governments extended
it in incompatible ways. Microsoft's code page 950 differs from plain Big5; Big5-HKSCS adds the Hong Kong Supplementary
Character Set, including the Cantonese characters used in everyday Hong Kong writing; other extensions add rarely used
characters and symbols. A file labeled "Big5" may need any of these to decode fully. A few Big5-HKSCS sequences decode to two
Unicode characters rather than one, so character counts and byte counts do not line up one to one.

### GB 2312, GBK and GB 18030 (Simplified Chinese, Mainland China)

Three government standards, each a superset of the one before.

- **GB 2312** (1980): about 7,000 characters.
- **GBK** (1995): about 21,000 characters, backward compatible with GB 2312.
- **GB 18030**: one to four bytes per character, covers every Unicode character. Support for its mandatory subset has been
  required for software sold in China since 2006 (English Wikipedia, GB 18030 article, read 2026-11-05).
  Editions appeared in 2000, 2005 and 2022; GB 18030-2022 has been in force since 1 August 2023 and changed 55 code
  positions from the 2005 edition (English Wikipedia, GB 18030 article, read 2026-11-05). Python's `gb18030` codec does not follow
  the 2022 edition; glibc's does (both measured in S2).

Much text labeled "GB 2312" is really GBK, because the label is older than the content. A strict GB 2312 decoder rejects such
text, and a lenient one hides the mismatch.

### Japanese and Korean

Japanese has three common legacy forms: Shift-JIS (and Microsoft's code page 932 extension, which disagrees with it on a
handful of symbols), EUC-JP, and ISO-2022-JP (a 7-bit form used in email that switches character sets with escape
sequences). Korean text is in EUC-KR, which covers 2,350 Hangul syllables, or in Microsoft's code page 949, which covers all
11,172.

### What the web does

Browsers do not follow each encoding's own table by label. The Web Hypertext Application Technology Working Group (WHATWG) Encoding Standard sends the label `gb2312` to its GBK
decoder, `big5-hkscs` to its Big5 decoder, and `windows-31j` to its Shift_JIS decoder. A page can therefore look right in a
browser and fail a strict decode in a programming language that follows the older tables.

### Unicode CJK

Unicode merges characters that Chinese, Japanese and Korean writing share into one code point ("Han unification"), so the same
code point can look different depending on the font and language tag. Rare and historical characters live in extension
blocks, and legacy encodings contain characters that Unicode kept only as compatibility duplicates so that round trips work.
Choosing UTF-8 does not remove the need to know these blocks when searching or matching text.

## Common pain points

### Mojibake (文字化け, "character transformation")

Garbled text from reading bytes with the wrong encoding. The usual causes:

1. Decoding with the wrong encoding, for example reading Big5 bytes as UTF-8.
2. Encoding with a repertoire too small for the text, then substituting question marks.
3. Double encoding: decoding UTF-8 bytes as Windows-1252, then encoding the result as UTF-8 again.
4. A database column whose collation or connection encoding differs from what was stored.

A concrete case: the two characters 中文 stored as UTF-8 and read as Windows-1252 appear as ä¸­æ–‡. That damage lost
nothing, so it can be reversed when the pair is known.

### Round-trip conversion loss

Text that goes from encoding A to Unicode and back to A does not always come back byte for byte. Legacy encodings contain
vendor extensions, duplicate code points and private-use characters. Some characters are simply absent from the target. A
Hong Kong character stored in Big5-HKSCS (byte pair 87 40, which decodes to U+43F0) decodes fine and cannot be encoded back
to plain Big5.

### Detection is a guess

No method detects an encoding reliably from bytes, for these reasons:

- Many encodings are valid readings of the same bytes, and the GB and Big5 byte ranges overlap.
- Short text carries too little signal. A five-character string is often consistent with several encodings.
- Mixed content, such as an email with several parts, has no single answer.

Detectors work by statistics over byte pairs (chardet, cchardet, uchardet, and the detector in International Components for Unicode (ICU)) or by decoding with each candidate and scoring
how plausible the result reads (charset-normalizer). Each answers with a name and sometimes a confidence; S2 measures how
often the name is right.

### Traditional and Simplified are not encodings

Big5 files are usually Traditional and GB files usually Simplified, but the encodings do not force it. Converting between the
two scripts is a vocabulary problem as well as a character problem: one Simplified character often stands for several
Traditional ones (Simplified 发 for both Traditional 發 and 髮), and Taiwan, Hong Kong and Mainland writing use different
words for the same thing (Mainland 软件, Taiwan 軟體). Conversion tools carry dictionaries for this; byte-level tools do not.

## What this survey evaluates

Tools for the four jobs, mostly reachable from Python: the standard library's codecs, glibc iconv and ICU as the transcoding
baselines; chardet, charset-normalizer, cchardet, faust-cchardet, uchardet, ICU's detector and chardetng for detection; ftfy
for repair; OpenCC, zhconv and zhconv-rs for variant conversion. The measures are accuracy of the answer, fidelity of the
conversion, behavior on bad input, install weight and maintenance.

## Out of scope

- **Font rendering**: how glyphs are drawn.
- **Input methods**: how people type CJK characters.
- **Text recognition from images**: survey 1.166.
- **Translation and segmentation**: the 1.170s and 1.033.2 and 1.033.3.

## What the field looks like after the changes of 2026

- Detection is no longer a two-horse choice. chardet was rewritten and relicensed, cchardet was revived, and the community
  fork of cchardet now trails it. Rules inherited from the earlier state of the field ("cchardet for speed, charset-normalizer for
  accuracy") need to be measured again, not carried over.
- The system libraries move too. glibc follows GB 18030-2022 on the sequences probed, and ICU 73.2 and later do according to the English Wikipedia
  article on GB 18030; Python's `gb18030` codec holds the pre-2022 assignments, and for one pair the 2000 edition's.
- ftfy and zhconv are the two tools in the sample that have not been released in years, for different reasons: ftfy's last
  release is from 2024, zhconv's from 2021.

# Repair and Variant Conversion

Two jobs sit beside detection and are often mistaken for it.

## Repair: text that was already decoded wrongly

Mojibake is not an unknown-encoding problem. The bytes were read with the wrong codec and the result was stored as text,
so the original bytes are no longer in hand. Detection cannot help; the only repair is to guess which wrong decoding
produced the garble and reverse it, which works when the wrong decoding lost nothing.

| Tool | Version, date | License | State on 2026-11-05 | Read or ran |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ftfy | 6.3.1, 2024-10-26 | Apache-2.0 | No release in more than two years; last commit 2024-10-30; 27 open issues and pull requests | Ran |

ftfy is the only general-purpose repair tool found. S2 measures whether it reaches East Asian multibyte garbling, and how often it alters text that was
never garbled.

The last release is more than two years old, and the stall test used by this survey's library check reads `stalled` on
2026-11-05: pull requests arrive, none are merged and no commits land. S4 weighs what that means for a pure-Python tool.

## Variant conversion: Traditional and Simplified Chinese

Converting between the two scripts is a character-set and vocabulary problem. It is not an encoding problem: Traditional and
Simplified text are both valid Unicode, and a Big5 file converted to UTF-8 is still Traditional. The tools differ in whether
they convert character by character or by phrase, and in whether they also change regional vocabulary (Taiwan "軟體" for
Mainland "软件").

The MediaWiki tables that zhconv and zhconv-rs use include regional phrase conversion (Taiwan, Hong Kong, Mainland), so
the tools are not split into "character-level" and "phrase-level" camps. S2 runs the comparison like for like.

## Starting points

- Garbled Latin-looking text from UTF-8 read as a single-byte encoding: try ftfy, then confirm the output reads correctly.
- Garbled East Asian text: if the wrong decoder and the right one are both known, reverse them with the codecs; do not
  expect a repair tool to find a multibyte pair.
- Traditional to Simplified for search or matching: any of the three; for text readers will see, check the result with a
  reader of the target region.

# Transcoding: When the Encoding Is Known

When the encoding is known, there is no detection problem and nothing to choose between libraries. The choice is between
codec implementations, and they disagree at the edges. Three implementations are in reach of a Python program.

| Implementation | What it is | Where the CJK tables come from | Read or ran |
|---|---|---|---|
| Python's built-in codecs | C modules in the standard library (`_codecs_cn`, `_codecs_tw`, `_codecs_hk`, `_codecs_jp`, `_codecs_kr`, `_codecs_iso2022`) | CPython's own tables, one per codec name | Ran on Python 3.12.3, 3.13.15 and 3.14.7 |
| glibc iconv | The C library's converter, 2.39 on the test machine (Ubuntu 24.04) | glibc's gconv modules | Ran, through `ctypes`, against the same inputs |
| ICU converters | ICU4C, 74.2 on the test machine; current release is 79.1 | ICU's conversion tables | Read (its charset detector was run; see `detectors.md`) |

## The codec names that matter, and the trap in each

| Name in Python | Covers | The trap |
|---|---|---|
| `gb2312` | The 1980 standard, about 7,000 characters | Much text labeled "GB2312" is really GBK; a strict decoder rejects it |
| `gbk` / `cp936` | GBK, Microsoft's code page 936 | Python's `gbk` and `cp936` are the same codec, and it lacks the euro sign at byte 0x80 that Microsoft's code page 936 table has |
| `gb18030` | Every Unicode scalar value, one to four bytes | Python's table does not follow the 2022 edition. GB 18030-2022, in force since 2023-08-01, changed 55 code positions; glibc follows it on the sequences S2 probed, and ICU 73.2 or later does according to the English Wikipedia article on GB 18030 |
| `big5` | Plain Big5 | Rejects Hong Kong characters and the common vendor extensions |
| `cp950` | Microsoft's code page 950 | Decodes 42 sequences that `big5` rejects and decodes 11 others differently |
| `big5hkscs` | Big5 with the Hong Kong Supplementary Character Set | A few sequences decode to two Unicode characters |
| `shift_jis` / `cp932` | JIS X 0208 in Shift-JIS form; Microsoft's extended code page 932 | `shift_jis` and `cp932` decode the same bytes 8160 and 8161 to different characters (wave dash against fullwidth tilde, double vertical line against parallel-to), and glibc reads bytes 0x5C and 0x7E as yen and overline where Python reads backslash and tilde |
| `euc_jp`, `iso2022_jp` | EUC-form and 7-bit escape-sequence Japanese | Switches character sets with escape sequences, which every detector tested found easily |
| `euc_kr` / `cp949` | KS X 1001 only; Microsoft's extended Korean | `euc_kr` writes the syllables outside the 2,350 in KS X 1001 as filler plus jamo sequences, which glibc's EUC-KR will not |

`gb18030` appears in Python's `gb18030` codec, glibc, and ICU; the three do not all follow the same edition. S2 measures
which does.

## What the web does instead

Browsers do not follow any of these tables by label. The WHATWG Encoding Standard maps the label `gb2312` to its GBK
decoder, `big5-hkscs` to its Big5 decoder, `shift_jis`, `windows-31j` and `ms932` to one Shift_JIS decoder, and
`windows-949` to its EUC-KR decoder; `hz-gb-2312`, `iso-2022-kr` and `iso-2022-cn` decode to an error on purpose. A
page labeled "Big5" therefore decodes in a browser to more characters than `big5` in Python accepts. The standard is
the reason that detectors built for browsers (chardetng) report WHATWG names whose meaning differs from the Python codec
of the same name. Read from encoding.spec.whatwg.org on 2026-11-05; not run, because no browser was driven.

## Starting points

- Known encoding, Python: the standard library. Pick the codec by what the producing system actually emits (a Taiwanese
  bank's `big5` file may need `cp950` or `big5hkscs`), not by what its header says.
- Round trip must be byte-exact: decode with the codec that matches the producer, compare `encode(decode(x))` to `x`,
  and keep the original bytes when they differ. S2 measures how many two-byte sequences do not round-trip, per codec.
- Mainland government or banking data after 2023: check which GB 18030 edition the producer implements before choosing a
  codec; Python's will not produce the 2022 mappings.

## The detection inputs

- **Wikipedia articles** (CC BY-SA 4.0) in Mainland and Taiwan Chinese, Hong Kong Cantonese, Japanese, Korean and English, ten
  articles per language, fetched by revision and pinned by SHA-256.
- **Tatoeba sentences** (CC BY 2.0 FR) in Mandarin, Japanese, Korean and Cantonese: short conversational sentences, a second
  corpus that no detector can have seen as encyclopedia text. Mandarin was split into Simplified and Traditional by what each
  sentence can be encoded in.
- Each sample is a whole number of sentences cut to 5, 10, 30, 100, 300 or 1,000 characters, encoded strictly in the known codec,
  40 per cell, seeded. Samples above 30 characters from Tatoeba join unrelated sentences.
- Classes whose name ends in "ext" must contain a character the narrower sibling codec cannot encode, so the sample cannot
  be read by it: GBK beyond GB 2312, GB 18030 beyond GBK (in the Wikipedia samples mostly the no-break space and the hyphenation point, which only GB 18030 can
  encode), Big5-HKSCS beyond Big5, code page 932 beyond Shift_JIS. A code page 949 class could not be built:
  Python's `euc_kr` codec writes every Hangul syllable, so no sample is outside it (see `codec-fidelity.md`).
- Controls: UTF-8 in each language and ASCII English.

## The scoring rule

A detector's answer is **correct** when `bytes.decode` with the Python codec that the returned name resolves to gives the
original text exactly. Otherwise it is **silent wrong** (decodes to different text), **raises** (decode fails), **unknown name**
(no Python codec of that name) or **no answer**. The same rule scores every detector. Two extra readings apply to all of them:
**correct after the superset** (the label's usual superset is tried too: GB 2312 then GBK then GB 18030, Big5 then Big5-HKSCS,
Shift_JIS then code page 932, EUC-KR then code page 949, ASCII then UTF-8), and exact-name match, which the harness records and
this survey does not use because names differ by convention. A second reading applies to every detector alike: the returned name is looked up in the WHATWG Encoding Standard's label table
(GBK and GB 2312 as `gb18030`, Big5 as `big5hkscs`, Shift_JIS as `cp932`, EUC-KR as `cp949`, and so on), because browsers decode
those labels that way and chardetng defines its labels that way. Both figures are reported for all eight detectors; a low literal
figure for a narrow label is a gap between label meanings, not necessarily a missing capability.

Samples whose bytes are pure ASCII were left out of the legacy classes (65 of 6,311), because every ASCII-compatible encoding
reads them the same way and no detector can be right or wrong about them.

## Limits

- Two corpora are not the web. No HTML, no markup hints, no byte-order marks and no mixed encodings were tested.
- chardet 7's repository documents CulturaX, MADLAD-400 and Wikipedia as training sources (read from `scripts/data_sources.py`).
  The Wikipedia articles here may overlap its training text; the Tatoeba corpus is the check on that, and chardet 7 scores
  higher on it, not lower.
- Every detector ran with default arguments. Confidence thresholds, candidate lists and encoding filters were not tuned for
  any of them, and chardet 7 offers such filters.
- **Timing is not reported.** The machine carried other jobs during every run. The one-minute load average recorded beside the
  final speed measurements ran from 2.9 to 4.4, straddling the limit of 4 above which this survey publishes no timing, and
  differed between detectors because they ran one after another. Speed is unmeasured. Peak memory, which load does not affect,
  is reported.

# Codec Fidelity: Python Against glibc and the Vendor Tables

When the encoding is known, the codec is the whole question. Python's built-in codecs were compared with glibc's iconv (called
through `ctypes` on the same machine) and with five Unicode vendor mapping tables. All of this ran on 2026-11-05 on arm64 Linux
(WSL2, Ubuntu 24.04.3 userland, glibc 2.39), Python 3.12.3 for the codec checks (`harness/1-163-character-encoding/results/codecs.json`).
The GB 18030 mapping was also checked on Python 3.13.15 and 3.14.7 with identical results.

"Valid sequences" below are the one-byte and two-byte sequences a decoder accepts, out of 24,322 tried (all 256 single bytes and
every lead byte from 0x81 to 0xFE with every trail byte from 0x40 to 0xFE). A pair counts as valid when both bytes decode, so a
half-width katakana byte followed by an ASCII byte is valid in the Shift_JIS family. Four-byte GB 18030 forms and the
three-byte EUC-JP forms are not in this inventory.

## Decoding: where Python and glibc agree

(measured 2026-11-05; Python 3.12.3, glibc 2.39; one-byte and two-byte sequences)

| Codec | Valid in Python | Valid in glibc | Decoded differently by the two | Valid only in Python | Valid only in glibc | Sequences that do not round-trip in Python |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| gb2312 | 7,573 | 7,573 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| gbk | 21,919 | 21,920 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| gb18030 | 24,068 | 24,068 | 19 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| big5 | 13,838 | 14,040 | 260 | 0 | 202 | 4 |
| big5hkscs | 18,530 | 18,592 | 0 | 7 | 69 | 12 |
| cp950 | 13,880 | 14,040 | 249 | 0 | 160 | 10 |
| shift_jis | 15,071 | 15,071 | 128 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| cp932 | 18,446 | 17,796 | 0 | 650 | 0 | 398 |
| euc_jp | 7,070 | 7,100 | 0 | 0 | 30 | 0 |
| euc_kr | 8,353 | 8,387 | 0 | 0 | 34 | 0 |
| cp949 | 17,176 | 17,176 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

A sequence "does not round-trip" when it shares its decoded character with another byte sequence, so encoding the result cannot
return both of the originals. A pair of glibc-only counts of 30 and 34 for EUC-JP and EUC-KR are mostly the bytes 0x80 to 0x9F, which
glibc passes through as C1 control characters and Python rejects.

What stands out:

- **The GB family agrees almost entirely.** GB 2312 and CP949 are identical in both. The exceptions are GBK, where glibc has
  one more sequence (the single byte 0x80, the euro sign in Microsoft's code page), and GB 18030 (next section).
- **Big5 is not one table.** Python's `big5` and glibc's `BIG5` decode 260 of the same sequences to different characters: A145
  to U+2022 against U+2027, A14E to U+FF64 against U+FE51, A1C2 to U+203E against U+00AF. Python's `big5` and `cp950` differ from
  each other on 11 sequences, and 42 sequences are valid in `cp950` and not in `big5`. A file labeled "Big5" has no single
  correct reading without knowing its producer.
- **Shift_JIS disagrees on two bytes.** All 128 sequences Python and glibc decode differently contain the byte 0x5C or 0x7E.
  Python reads 0x5C as the backslash U+005C and 0x7E as the tilde U+007E, as ASCII does; glibc's `SHIFT_JIS` reads them as the
  yen sign U+00A5 and the overline U+203E, as the Japanese national variant of ASCII does. The same bytes in a path or a
  regular expression decode to different characters.
- **Code page 932 has duplicates.** 398 of its sequences share a character with another sequence, in the lead-byte ranges of the
  NEC and IBM extension rows (0x87, 0xED to 0xEE, 0xFA to 0xFC) and 0x81, so decode-then-encode returns a different byte
  sequence for them. Python also decodes the single bytes 0x80, 0xA0, 0xFD, 0xFE and 0xFF (to U+0080 and to the private-use
  U+F8F0 to U+F8F3) where glibc rejects them; the 650 sequences only Python accepts begin with one of those bytes.
- **Big5-HKSCS** decodes identically in Python and glibc wherever both accept a sequence (18,523 of 18,523); glibc accepts 69
  more and Python 7 more. Twelve Python sequences do not round-trip. A few sequences decode to two Unicode characters, such as
  the Hong Kong 8862 to U+00CA U+0304, so character counts and byte counts do not line up one to one.

## GB 18030: Python does not follow the 2022 edition

GB 18030-2022 has been enforced since 2023-08-01 and moved 55 code positions to new characters (English Wikipedia, GB 18030
article, read 2026-11-05). Eleven byte sequences whose targets changed were decoded by Python and glibc.

(measured 2026-11-05; the same Python result on 3.12.3, 3.13.15 and 3.14.7)

| Bytes | Python | glibc iconv |
|---|---|---|
| FE59 | U+E81E (private use) | U+9FB4 |
| FE61 | U+E826 (private use) | U+9FB5 |
| FE66, FE67, FE6D, FE7E, FE90, FEA0 | U+E82B, U+E82C, U+E832, U+E843, U+E854, U+E864 (private use) | U+9FB6 to U+9FBB |
| A6D9 | U+E78D (private use) | U+FE10 |
| A8BC | U+E7C7 (private use) | U+1E3F |
| 8135F437 | U+1E3F | U+E7C7 |

Python maps the probed Appendix E sequences to the private use area, as the 2005 edition did, and maps A8BC and 8135F437 as the
2000 edition did: the 2005 edition swapped those two (English Wikipedia, GB 18030 article), and glibc has the swapped form. So
the codec holds neither the 2005 swap nor any 2022 change. A file written by software that follows the 2022 edition decodes without error in Python, to private-use
characters in place of the characters the producer meant. No open issue about this was found in CPython's tracker
(searched `GB18030` in python/cpython on 2026-11-05). ICU 73.2 and later follow the 2022 edition (read from the same Wikipedia
article; not run).

- **Silent changes on a round trip.** Encoding the yen sign to `shift_jis` or `euc_jp` succeeds and writes 0x5C, which decodes
  to a backslash. In `cp932`, the wave dash U+301C encodes to 8160, which decodes to U+FF5E, a different character. In `cp950`,
  U+00A5 encodes to A244 and decodes to the fullwidth yen U+FFE5.
- **The wave dash and its lookalike.** `shift_jis` writes U+301C as 8160 and `cp932` writes U+FF5E there. A Japanese string that

  used throughout.
- **Cantonese characters need Hong Kong's extension.** `big5` and `cp950` cannot encode 嘅, 咗 or 啲; `big5hkscs` can. They are
  also in GBK's extension area, so the same characters have a GBK code (86FE for 嘅).
- **Only GB 18030 writes everything.** The supplementary-plane character 𠮷 (U+20BB7), the emoji U+1F600 and U+00A0 encode in
  no other codec in the table. In the Wikipedia samples used in `detection-accuracy.md`, the characters that put a sample in the GB 18030 class were
  mostly U+00A0 and U+2027.

## EUC-KR in Python is wider than the standard

Python's `euc_kr` encodes all 11,172 Hangul syllables, although KS X 1001 contains only 2,350. It writes the other 8,822 as a
filler character followed by jamo: 갂 becomes the eight bytes A4D4 A4A1 A4BF A4A2, against two bytes for a syllable in the
2,350. Python's decoder reads that form back, so the round trip is exact for all 11,172 syllables
(measured 2026-11-05, `results/codecs.json`, part F). glibc's EUC-KR encoder writes only the 2,350. Two consequences follow: a
file written with Python's `euc_kr` can contain long forms where glibc's writer would have refused the character, and a code
page 949 test class cannot be built by encoding Korean text with `euc_kr`, since `euc_kr` never rejects a syllable.

## What this means

- Which codec name to choose is a data question, not a language question. For Big5 files, test `big5`, `cp950` and `big5hkscs`
  against the producer's own sample before trusting a label.
- For Shift_JIS, decide whether 0x5C means backslash or yen before decoding; Python and glibc answer differently.
- For GB 18030 files from a producer that follows the 2022 edition, transcode with glibc iconv or another implementation of
  the 2022 mapping rather than Python.
- Compare `encode(decode(x))` with `x` on the data you have; for code page 932 and the Big5 family, some sequences cannot come
  back byte for byte.

Eight detectors, one set of inputs, one scoring rule (see `approach.md`). Every figure here is from

codec name. In the second, the name is looked up in the WHATWG Encoding Standard's label table (GBK and GB 2312 both mean its

capability.

## How detectors fail

Counted over the 4,086 legacy samples (`harness/1-163-character-encoding/inspect_failures.py`):

- **Claiming UTF-16.** ICU answered `UTF-16BE` on 430 samples and charset-normalizer answered `utf_16_be` or `utf_16_le` on 181.
  UTF-16 decodes almost any even-length byte string, so such an answer decodes without error to unrelated text. No other
  detector answered UTF-16 on these inputs.
- **Claiming UTF-8 or a single-byte code page.** cchardet answered `UTF-8` on 167 legacy samples, which then fail to decode;
  uchardet gave `windows-1252` on 192 and ICU on 223.
- **Naming a narrower relative.** `big5` (cchardet 617 wrong answers), `shift_jis`, `GB2312` (chardet 5.2.0 522 wrong answers):
  names that are right for ordinary text and wrong for the extensions.
- **No answer.** uchardet 516, charset-normalizer 77, cchardet 207, chardet 5.2.0 435 (each count includes all classes).

## Input that ends inside a character

A common way to detect an encoding is to read the first few kilobytes of a file. Such a cut often lands inside a multibyte
character. Sixteen pinned Wikipedia articles (four each for Big5, GBK, Shift_JIS and EUC-KR) were cut at 2,048 and 8,192 bytes on a
character boundary, giving 32 buffers per detector. Each buffer was also shortened by 1, 2 and 3 bytes. Of the 96 shortened
variants, 58 end inside a character (strict decoding fails) and 38 end on a boundary (for two-byte codecs, removing two bytes drops
a whole character).

(measured 2026-11-05; 16 articles, 2 sizes, 4 codecs; `harness/1-163-character-encoding/results/cut/`; the test covers 2-byte
codecs only, the cuts remove at most three bytes, and the texts are encyclopedia prose)

| Detector | 32 whole-character buffers: answered | 38 cuts on a boundary: same answer as whole | 58 cuts inside a character: answered | 58 cuts inside a character: same answer as whole |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| chardet 7.7.0, chardet 5.2.0, cchardet, faust-cchardet, uchardet, ICU | 32 | 38 | 58 | 58 |
| charset-normalizer 3.5.2 | 32 | 38 | **0** | **0** |
| chardetng 0.3.5 | 32 | 38 | 58 (all `windows-1252`) | **0** |

On this test two detectors that did well on whole-character input lose their answer when the input ends inside a character:
charset-normalizer returns nothing on all 58, and chardetng returns `windows-1252` on all 58. The result holds for these 16 texts,

# Error Handling: Unmappable, Malformed and Cut Input

Everything here ran on Python 3.12.3 on 2026-11-05 (`harness/1-163-character-encoding/results/codecs.json`, part D). Python's
CJK codecs share one implementation of the error handlers, so most results are the same across codecs.

## Encoding a character the codec cannot write

The text `abc𠮷def` (U+20BB7 in the middle) was encoded in GB 2312, GBK, Big5, Shift_JIS, EUC-JP and EUC-KR, none of which has
that character, under each handler.

(measured 2026-11-05; identical in all six codecs)

| Handler | Result |
|---|---|
| `strict` | Raises `UnicodeEncodeError` at position 3 |
| `ignore` | `abcdef`: the character disappears |
| `replace` | `abc?def` |
| `xmlcharrefreplace` | `abc&#134071;def` |
| `backslashreplace` | `abc\U00020bb7def` |
| `namereplace` | `abc\N{CJK UNIFIED IDEOGRAPH-20BB7}def` |

`ignore` and `replace` lose information without saying so, and `replace` writes an ASCII question mark that later reads as a real
one. `xmlcharrefreplace` is the only handler whose output a later reader can turn back into the original character, and only
if the text is HTML or XML. The pattern for a legacy-encoded output is to use `strict`, catch the error, and decide per
character, or to write GB 18030, which can encode every character.

## Decoding malformed input

Five bytes with one invalid byte in the middle (for example Big5 `A4 A4 FF A4 A4`, which is 中 then an invalid byte then 中).

(measured 2026-11-05)

| Codec | `strict` | `replace` | `ignore` |
|---|---|---|---|
| big5, gbk, shift_jis, euc_kr, utf-8 | Raises, "illegal multibyte sequence" or "invalid start byte", pointing at the bad byte | One U+FFFD between the two good characters | The good characters, nothing marking the gap |
| gb18030 (a broken four-byte form, `D6 D0 81 30 CE C4`) | Raises, "illegal multibyte sequence" | 中, U+FFFD, **the digit 0**, 文 | 中, **the digit 0**, 文 |

Python's decoder consumes the leading two bytes of the broken four-byte form (81 30) and then reads the remaining byte, 0x30, as
the ASCII digit zero. A damaged GB 18030 stream can therefore produce a stray digit that was never in the text. Under `strict`
the error is raised and nothing is lost.

## Input that ends inside a character

Cutting a byte string after the first byte of a two-byte character (or after three bytes of a four-byte GB 18030 character):

(measured 2026-11-05)

| Codec | `strict` | `replace` |
|---|---|---|
| big5, gbk, shift_jis, euc_jp, euc_kr | Raises, "incomplete multibyte sequence", at the last byte | The good text, then one U+FFFD |
| gb18030, a four-byte character cut after three bytes | Raises, "incomplete multibyte sequence", at the start of the character | One U+FFFD for the whole character |

## Reading in chunks

A file read in pieces breaks multibyte characters at piece boundaries. Four Wikipedia-derived texts of about 20,000 characters
were encoded in Big5, GBK, Shift_JIS and EUC-KR and decoded in chunks of 1, 3 and 4,096 bytes, once with the incremental decoder
Python provides and once by decoding each chunk separately.

(measured 2026-11-05; Python 3.12.3)

| Codec | Bytes | Chunk size | Incremental decoder matches the full decode | Chunks that a separate `decode` call rejects |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| big5 | 39,134 | 1 | Yes | 29,455 of 39,134 |
| big5 | 39,134 | 3 | Yes | 8,299 of 13,045 |
| big5 | 39,134 | 4,096 | Yes | 4 of 10 |
| gbk | 37,947 | 4,096 | Yes | 8 of 10 |
| shift_jis | 25,764 | 4,096 | Yes | 1 of 7 |
| euc_kr | 33,759 | 4,096 | Yes | 6 of 9 |

At 4,096 bytes, the usual buffer size, a plain per-chunk `decode` fails on most chunks of CJK text. The incremental decoder
matched the full decode in every one of the 12 combinations (four codecs, three chunk sizes). Using `codecs.getincrementaldecoder`,
or opening the file with an `encoding` argument, which uses it, is the fix; decoding each chunk yourself is not.

## An open bug in incremental decoding

CPython issue 160307, opened 2026-10-22, reports that the incremental decoders of codecs with sequences longer than two bytes
(`euc_jp`, `euc_jis_2004`, `euc_jisx0213`, `gb18030`) drop input after an incomplete trailing sequence when the error handler is
`surrogateescape`. A fix is proposed in a pull request that was open on 2026-11-05 (read from the issue). The harness reproduced
the report on Python 3.12.3.

(measured 2026-11-05; `errors='surrogateescape'`, `final=True`)

| Codec and bytes | One-shot `decode` | Incremental decoder | Same |
|---|---|---|---|
| euc_jp `8F 00` | U+DC8F then NUL | U+DC8F only | No |
| euc_jp `A8 8F 6C` | U+DCA8, U+DC8F, `l` | U+DCA8, U+DC8F | No |
| gb18030 `80 36` | U+DC80 then `6` | U+DC80 only | No |
| big5 `A4`, gbk `D6`, shift_jis `93`, euc_kr `C7` | One surrogate | One surrogate | Yes |

The lost bytes are plain ASCII (the NUL, the `l`, the `6`). Reading a file through `open(..., errors="surrogateescape")` goes
through the incremental decoder, so the loss is reachable by ordinary file reading. The two-byte codecs are not affected, because
one pending byte leaves nothing further to drop.

## What this means

- Prefer `strict`, then decide. The lenient handlers each hide a different loss.
- Never decode chunks separately; use the incremental interface or let `open` do it.
- For GB 18030 and EUC-JP input that may contain damaged bytes, do not use `surrogateescape` on Python builds that have not
  taken the fix for issue 160307.

## The rules the measurements overturned or sharpened

- **"charset-normalizer for accuracy, cchardet for speed" no longer describes the field.** On these inputs chardet 7.7.0 is 15
  points more accurate than charset-normalizer, and cchardet 2.4.0 is 17 points less accurate. Speed was not measured, so the
  speed half of the rule is untested here.
- **A detector's name is not a Python codec name.** The C-family detectors and ICU answer `BIG5`, `SHIFT_JIS` and `UHC` for text
  that needs `big5hkscs`, `cp932` or `cp949`, and score 0.0% on Hong Kong and code page 932 extension samples when the name is
  decoded by Python. Read the way browsers read the labels, the same answers score 16.5% to 81.5% (Hong Kong) and 59.2% to 66.3%
  (code page 932). chardet 7 returns the extended names by default, so the two readings agree for it.
- **"No answer" and "wrong answer" are different failures, and the second is the dangerous one.** charset-normalizer
  (18.5% of answers silently decode to the wrong text) and ICU (21.7%) never signal a problem when they are wrong.
- **Input that ends inside a character can erase a detector's answer.** On 58 buffers cut inside a character (16 articles in four
  two-byte codecs, cuts of up to three bytes), charset-normalizer returned no answer on all 58 and chardetng returned
  `windows-1252` on all 58; the other six detectors kept their answers.
- **ftfy repairs one family of damage, not CJK encodings.** It recovers UTF-8 read as Latin-1 or Windows-1252, in Chinese,
  Japanese and Korean text alike. It recovers nothing from multibyte pairs, and it changes text without repairing it in 665 of
  1,200 cases where CJK bytes were read as Latin-1 or Windows-1252.
- **Python's `gb18030` is behind the standard.** Eleven probed sequences decode differently in Python and glibc, and glibc has
  the 2022 mapping.

## What was not settled

- **Speed.** Not measured. The machine was shared and the load straddled the limit. A project that detects over gigabytes of
  data should time the candidates itself.
- **Real-world files.** The inputs are Wikipedia and Tatoeba text re-encoded by the harness. Files that real systems produced
  (with their own extensions, mixed encodings, or markup) were not available.
- **Tuned configurations.** Defaults only. chardet 7's era filter and candidate lists, and charset-normalizer's `cp_isolation`,
  may help on a known domain.
- **Other ecosystems.** JavaScript and Rust detectors were read at registry level and not run.

## How the walk-throughs use the evidence

- A figure from S2 carries the corpus and the length it was measured at. A figure measured on 300-character Wikipedia text
  does not describe a 10-character filename, and the short-string walk-through says so.
- "Read" means the claim comes from documentation or a registry and was not run. "Ran" means the harness produced it.
- Every walk-through ends with **Check before committing**: a test that takes the reader's own data and settles the question
  in an afternoon. The survey's numbers are a prior; the reader's data is the evidence.

# S3 Recommendation: Matching the Situation to the Tools

The same tools come out differently in different situations. This table reads the S2 evidence for each walk-through, and each
row applies to its own situation only.

| Situation | Reach for | Do not reach for | The check that decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| Migrating files from a known producer | The producer's codec, widened if strict decoding fails; keep source bytes | A detector | Strict decode of a sample from every year, then a search for U+FFFD and private-use characters |
| Accepting uploads of unknown encoding | UTF-8 check, then chardet 7.7.0, then strict decode with the widest sibling, then a preview the user can override | charset-normalizer or ICU as the only judge (silent wrong answers on 18.5% and 21.7%) | Fifty real files from your users |
| Reading web pages | The declared charset mapped through WHATWG's table; chardetng (`allow_utf8`, labels as WHATWG) or chardet 7.7.0 when it is wrong | Passing a browser-style label to Python's codec of the same name | Pages cut at the detection-prefix boundary |
| Short strings | Detect per source, not per string; chardet 7.7.0 or chardetng on the pooled text | ICU, cchardet, charset-normalizer on single strings | A hundred real strings against the pooled answer |
| Repairing garbled rows | `fix_encoding` for UTF-8 read as a single-byte encoding; the known pair reversed with codecs for everything else | `fix_text` defaults on CJK text; ftfy on multibyte-encoding garble | A reader of the language over a hundred repaired rows |
| GB 18030 interchange | `gb18030` strict, plus glibc `iconv` for the 2022 mapping | Trusting Python's table for a 2022-edition producer | A file with FE59 and A6D9 from the partner, decoded by two implementations |
| Hong Kong content | `big5hkscs` first; chardet 7.7.0 or charset-normalizer when detecting | cchardet, uchardet, ICU, and plain `big5` | Rows that fail `big5` and pass `big5hkscs` |
| Two scripts | OpenCC `s2twp` for phrase conventions or zhconv-rs for Wikipedia's; store the original | zhconv for new work | Reviewers in each region over a hundred articles |

## What the walk-throughs have in common

- **The strict decode is the cheapest test.** Nearly every situation is settled faster by decoding a sample under `strict` and
  looking at what fails than by trusting a label or a detector's confidence.
- **A detector answers a different question from the one the next step asks.** Its name may not be a Python codec name, its
  answer may be narrower than the data, and on short or cut input it may be wrong or empty.
- **Keep the originals.** Every path stores the source bytes or the author's original script beside the converted text.

# Exchanging Data Under GB 18030

## The situation

A system exchanges records with a Mainland partner whose contract or regulation names GB 18030 as the interchange encoding.
Names, addresses and notes in the records include rare characters, so GBK is not enough. Software sold in China has been
required since 2006 to support the mandatory subset of the standard (English Wikipedia, GB 18030 article, read 2026-11-05).

## What the measurements say

- **The codec covers every character.** Python's `gb18030` encoded and decoded back all 1,112,064 Unicode scalar values without
  error (`codec-fidelity.md`). Characters outside GBK, such as 𠮷 (U+20BB7), U+00A0 and the emoji U+1F600, encode in no other
  codec tested.
- **The edition matters, and Python is behind.** GB 18030-2022 has been enforced since 2023-08-01 and changed 55 code positions.
  Eleven probed byte sequences decode in Python to private-use characters (FE59 to U+E81E and similar) where glibc's iconv gives
  the standard characters (FE59 to U+9FB4). Python's A8BC and 8135F437 are swapped relative to glibc's. The same results came
  from Python 3.12.3, 3.13.15 and 3.14.7. A partner on the 2022 edition can send text that decodes without error and to the wrong
  characters.
- **Detection on GBK-range text that contains a few GB 18030-only characters splits the detectors.** The class is sentences with
  a few characters outside GBK, mostly the no-break space (204 of the 240 Wikipedia samples) and the hyphenation point; it does not test rare
  ideographs or the four-byte range generally. uchardet read 100.0%, chardetng 99.6%, chardet 7 90.0%, charset-normalizer 80.8%,
  ICU 41.2% and cchardet and faust-cchardet 5.0%, under both label readings (`detection-accuracy.md`). A detector in this position
  is not needed when the contract names the encoding, but a gateway that checks partner files for compliance will rely on one.
- **A damaged four-byte sequence leaves debris.** Python's `replace` handler on a broken four-byte form produced U+FFFD
  followed by a stray digit 0 from the sequence's remaining byte (`error-handling.md`).
- **Incremental decoding of `gb18030` is affected by an open CPython bug** under `surrogateescape` (issue 160307).

## A path

1. Decode with `gb18030` and `strict`; reject files that raise rather than replacing.
2. Scan the result for private-use characters (U+E000 to U+F8FF). Under the 2022 edition several of them should have been
   standard characters; their presence means either an old producer or Python's table.
3. If the partner follows the 2022 edition, transcode with glibc's `iconv` or a library that implements the 2022 mapping, and
   compare the two decoders on the 11 sequences in `codec-fidelity.md`.
4. Store UTF-8 internally and write GB 18030 only at the boundary.

## Check before committing

- Ask the partner which edition they implement, and get a file that contains FE59 and A6D9 to test against.
- Run the same file through Python and `iconv -f GB18030 -t UTF-8` and diff the output; any difference is an edition difference.
- If a detector is in the path, test it on files that contain GB 18030-only characters, including the rare ideographs and four-byte forms this survey did not sample.

# Content in Hong Kong Cantonese

## The situation

A publisher or archive holds Hong Kong text: news, forum posts, government documents. It is Traditional Chinese with Cantonese
characters such as 嘅, 咗 and 啲 that Taiwan and Mainland writing do not use. Older material is in Big5 with the Hong Kong
Supplementary Character Set (HKSCS), some in vendor variants, some in Unicode already.

## What the measurements say

- **Plain Big5 cannot hold this text.** Python's `big5` and `cp950` cannot encode 嘅, 咗 or 啲; `big5hkscs` can (9DEF, 9DF7 and
  9DF8). GBK also has codes for them (86FE for 嘅) (`codec-fidelity.md`). A file containing them read with `big5` fails strictly.
- **Most detectors answer the narrower name.** On Hong Kong Cantonese samples that need HKSCS, only chardet 7.7.0 (98.3% on
  both corpora) and charset-normalizer (78.5%) returned a name that Python decodes them with. cchardet, faust-cchardet, uchardet
  and ICU return `BIG5` or `Big5`, which raises on the Hong Kong characters when decoded by Python's `big5`: 0.0% literally.
  That is largely a label-meaning gap. WHATWG's Big5 includes the Hong Kong set, and read that way cchardet scores 81.5%,
  faust-cchardet 66.2%, ICU 65.6% and uchardet 16.5%; chardetng, whose labels are defined that way, scores 98.8%.
- **The distinguishing characters are few.** Every one of the 480 Cantonese samples contains at least one character outside Big5
  by construction. The most common were 嘅 (in 348 samples), 喺 (278), 咗 (208), 啲 (168), 哋 (150) and 嚟 (126). Real Cantonese
  text that happens to avoid them is valid Big5 as well, and no detector can tell the difference.
- **Where two readings differ, they differ on a few sequences.** Python's `big5hkscs` and glibc's `BIG5-HKSCS` agree on all
  18,523 sequences both accept; they differ in which extra ones each accepts (69 and 7). A few sequences decode to two Unicode
  characters (8862 to U+00CA U+0304), which matters when counting characters.

## A path

1. For Big5-family input from Hong Kong, decode with `big5hkscs` first, not `big5`.
2. If detecting, prefer chardet 7.7.0 or charset-normalizer, or translate any detector's `Big5` answer to `big5hkscs` before decoding.
3. Convert to UTF-8 once; Cantonese characters then survive every later step.
4. When producing legacy output for a Hong Kong system, check that the target actually reads HKSCS; plain Big5 consumers will
   reject those bytes.

## Check before committing

- Pull a sample from the archive and count rows that fail `big5` but pass `big5hkscs`; that count is the size of the problem.
- Check normalization: the two-character decodes mean `len()` of a decoded string differs from the count of source characters.
- Check that the Hong Kong forms of shared characters are not silently replaced by Taiwan or Mainland forms by a later
  variant converter; Hong Kong has its own configurations (`s2hk`, `hk2s` in OpenCC, `zh-hk` in zhconv), whose accuracy this
  survey did not measure.

# Migrating a Legacy Archive Whose Producer Is Known

## The situation

A team holds years of exports from one system: a bank's batch files, a registry's database dumps, a mail archive. The files are
Big5, GBK or Shift_JIS, the producer is known and documented, and the job is to convert everything to UTF-8 once and keep the
originals. Nothing needs guessing; the question is which codec the producer really wrote.

## What the measurements say

- Detection is the wrong tool. A detector adds a guess where the producer's documentation already has the answer, and the best
  one tested was wrong on 5.1% of samples (chardet 7.7.0), the others on 20% to 45% (`detection-accuracy.md`).
- The codec name decides the result. For Taiwanese data, `big5`, `cp950` and `big5hkscs` decode 13,838, 13,880 and 18,530
  sequences respectively, and `big5` and `cp950` decode 11 sequences differently (`codec-fidelity.md`). For Japanese data,
  `shift_jis` and `cp932` decode two byte pairs (8160 and 8161) to different characters, and 398 `cp932` sequences share a
  character with another, so some originals cannot come back byte for byte.
- A producer that follows GB 18030-2022 will decode in Python to private-use characters at the positions that changed. glibc's
  `iconv` has the 2022 mapping.
- `strict` decoding finds the problems: a malformed byte raises with its position, and nothing is lost silently
  (`error-handling.md`).

## A path

1. Decode a few thousand files with `strict`, using the codec the producer's documentation names, and count failures.
2. For each failure class, try the next wider codec (`big5` then `cp950` then `big5hkscs`; `shift_jis` then `cp932`; `gb2312`
   then `gbk` then `gb18030`) and see whether the failures vanish.
3. Convert with the widest codec that reads everything, write UTF-8, and keep the source bytes and the codec name in a manifest.
4. Re-encode a sample back to the source codec and compare bytes; the survivors of that comparison are the sequences that are
   safe to round-trip.

## Check before committing

- Run the migration on a sample that includes the oldest and the newest files; producers change their extensions over time.
- Search the output for U+FFFD, U+E000 to U+F8FF (private use), and unexpected backslash or yen characters in Japanese text.
- If the producer is a GB 18030 system dated after August 2023, compare a few of the changed positions (FE59 is one) against
  glibc's output before trusting Python's.

# Repairing Text That Is Already Garbled in a Database

## The situation

A table holds customer names or messages that were stored through the wrong connection encoding years ago. Some rows read
correctly; others show strings like ä¸­æ–‡ or ÖÐÎÄ. The original bytes are gone from the application, but the garbled text is
still in the database, and the question is whether it can be reversed.

## What the measurements say

Method and the full table are in `repair-and-conversion-results.md`; the points that matter here:

- **Two families of damage behave very differently.** UTF-8 text that was read as Latin-1 or Windows-1252 (the "ä¸­æ–‡" kind)
  is repaired by ftfy in 792 of 800 cases across Chinese, Japanese and Korean. Text whose bytes were GBK, Big5, Shift_JIS or
  EUC-KR read as Latin-1 or Windows-1252 (the "ÖÐÎÄ" kind) is repaired in 0 of 1,200 cases.
- **ftfy can make the second family worse.** In those 1,200 cases it altered 665 strings into a different wrong string (all 200
  Shift_JIS ones, 322 of 400 GBK, 64 of 400 Big5, 79 of 200 EUC-KR). Never overwrite a row with ftfy's output without
  keeping the original.
- **When the damage lost nothing, knowing the pair beats any tool.** Reversing the known encode and decode pair recovered every
  case in all 18 garbling pairs tested, including all those ftfy could not touch. The wrong decode must have been lossless:
  Latin-1 is, Windows-1252 is when no byte in the data is one of its five undefined values, and a strict decode that
  replaced bytes with U+FFFD is not.
- **Do not run `ftfy.fix_text` over a whole column.** With default settings it rewrote 106 of 200 Simplified Chinese sentences,
  107 of 200 Traditional and 31 of 200 Japanese that were never garbled, replacing fullwidth punctuation and digits with ASCII.
  `fix_encoding` changed none of 800.

## A path

1. Group rows by pattern. Garble with lowercase accented letters and symbols (ä, ¸, æ) looks like the UTF-8 family; garble with
   capital accented letters (Ö, Ð, Î) looks like the legacy multibyte family. These are rules of thumb from the examples
   tested; the survey did not test a classifier, so read a sample of each group.
2. For the UTF-8 family, apply `fix_encoding` per cell, compare with the original, and apply only when the result differs and
   contains CJK characters.
3. For the legacy family, work out the pair from the source system's history: which encoding the application wrote and which
   one the connection read. Then reverse it: encode the garbled text with the codec it was wrongly decoded with, decode those bytes with the
   codec the application originally wrote, and check that the result re-garbles to the stored text.
4. Keep the damaged text in a side column until the repaired text has been reviewed.

## Check before committing

- Repair a hundred rows by the proposed rule and have a reader of the language check them. Round-trip tests catch loss; they
  do not catch a wrong but plausible repair.
- Check for rows that were garbled twice (UTF-8 read as Latin-1, twice); `fix_encoding` can handle some of these and the
  survey did not test them.
- Check for rows that contain U+FFFD: that character means the bytes were already lost, and no repair can recover them.

# Short Strings: Filenames, Email Subjects, Database Fields

## The situation

The input is a single string, not a file: the name of an entry inside a ZIP archive, an email subject line, a customer name in a
database column, an ID3 tag. There are a handful of bytes, often 10 to 30, and the source system's encoding is unrecorded.

## What the measurements say

Short input is where the detectors separate most (`detection-accuracy.md`, both corpora, share decoding to the original text):

| Length in characters | chardet 7.7.0 | chardetng | charset-normalizer | cchardet | ICU |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 87.5% / 87.5% | 73.9% / 89.1% | 47.4% / 48.4% | 29.6% / 35.6% | 11.5% / 11.5% |
| 10 | 93.3% / 93.5% | 77.1% / 96.3% | 60.8% / 62.3% | 55.0% / 67.6% | 11.7% / 11.7% |
| 30 | 97.3% / 97.3% | 79.6% / 99.0% | 89.1% / 89.4% | 68.4% / 84.8% | 71.7% / 90.3% |

Each cell is "label used as a Python codec name / label read as a WHATWG name", the same two readings for every detector.

(measured 2026-11-05; 641, 673 and 678 samples per row)

- At five characters the two best readings are wrong about one time in eight (chardet 7.7.0) and one in nine (chardetng read as
  WHATWG names, which is the reading its documentation defines), and charset-normalizer and the C family are wrong half the time or more. Five
  characters is a surname and a given name.
- **ICU is unusable below about 30 characters here**: 11.5% and 11.7%; `UTF-16BE` was its most common wrong answer across all lengths.
- **The short samples that are pure ASCII were removed** from these figures; no detector can be right or wrong about them, and
  they decode the same in every ASCII-compatible encoding.
- Some short strings are ambiguous in principle. Five bytes can be a valid reading in two or three encodings, and the
  detector's confidence will not say so. The harness counted only whether the answer read the bytes back correctly, not whether
  the question had one answer.

## A path

1. Do not detect per string. Detect per source: pool all the strings from one archive, one mailbox, one column or one
   producer, detect on the pool, and apply the result to each string.
2. For ZIP entries, check the archive's UTF-8 flag first; names without it are in the producing machine's code page, and one
   archive usually has one code page.
3. If the source is truly one string at a time, narrow the candidates before detecting: restrict the detector to the two or
   three encodings plausible for the source's country (chardet 7 accepts a candidate list; not tested here).
4. Keep the raw bytes; store the decoded text and the encoding you chose beside them.

## Check before committing

- Take a hundred real strings from the source, decode them by hand with the suspected encoding, and compare with the pooled
  detection. Disagreements show whether pooling works for that source.
- Include names with Hong Kong characters, or Japanese with the fullwidth tilde, if the source may contain them: the
  C-family detectors scored 0.0% on both classes.

# Serving Traditional and Simplified Readers

## The situation

A publisher keeps one body of Chinese content and serves it to readers in Taiwan, Hong Kong and the Mainland. Authors write in
one script; each readership wants its own. The content is stored as UTF-8. The question is which converter to run and what to
check, since this is a vocabulary problem as well as a character problem.

## What the measurements say

Details and method are in `repair-and-conversion-results.md`. The reference throughout is MediaWiki's own rendering of the same
two Wikipedia articles in both variants, so the MediaWiki-derived tools (zhconv, zhconv-rs) are favored by construction.

- **One Simplified character often stands for several Traditional ones.** A round trip from Taiwan text to Simplified and back
  left 393 of 988 lines unchanged with OpenCC, 390 with zhconv and 676 with zhconv-rs. The loss is in the languages, and no
  converter can remove it; store the author's original script.
- **Against Wikipedia's convention, the MediaWiki tools are closer.** Mainland to Taiwan with regional vocabulary: 0.60% of
  characters differing for zhconv-rs, 0.77% for zhconv and 1.61% for OpenCC `s2tw`. This measures agreement with Wikipedia, not
  correctness.
- **The Taiwan vocabulary conventions differ.** On ten Mainland terms with Taiwan forms chosen in advance, OpenCC `s2twp` got
  10, zhconv and zhconv-rs 6; the four they left alone were 内存, 网络, 默认 and 信息. Which convention is right depends on the
  readers: on this list, Wikipedia's Taiwan rendering is more conservative than OpenCC's phrase configuration.
- **Ambiguous Traditional characters converted correctly in all three tools** on the ten terms tried (hair, heaven and earth,
  dry and so on), at 10 of 10 each.
- **The oldest tool trails.** zhconv (no release since 2021) differs from zhconv-rs on Mainland-to-Traditional conversion by
  1.67% against 0.69% of characters differing from MediaWiki's rendering in character-level mode.
- **Installation differs.** zhconv has no wheel (source distribution only); OpenCC and zhconv-rs install from wheels
  (`install-and-maintenance.md`). Check the license of whichever you choose: the registry and repository disagree for zhconv
  and zhconv-rs.

## A path

1. Keep the author's script as the stored original.
2. Convert at publish time to the other scripts, using regional configurations (OpenCC `s2twp` and `s2hk`; zhconv-rs `zh-tw`, `zh-hk` and
   `zh-cn`).
3. Maintain a protected-terms list for names, brands and quotations; no converter knows them.
4. Have a native reader of each target region review a sample of each article type before launch.

## Check before committing

- Convert a hundred real articles and give them to reviewers in each region; count the sentences they would rewrite.
- Run the round trip on your own content and measure how many lines come back different.
- Decide whether to follow Wikipedia's conventions or a house style for regional terms, since the tools disagree.

# Accepting Uploaded or Imported Files of Unknown Encoding

## The situation

A service lets people upload CSV files, text exports or subtitles. Some arrive as UTF-8. Many arrive from Windows or older
systems in whatever the person's tools wrote: Big5, GBK, Shift_JIS, EUC-KR. The upload form claims nothing reliable. The service
must decide per file.

## What the measurements say

- Detection is needed here, and the choice matters. On 4,086 legacy-encoding samples, chardet 7.7.0 read back 94.9% correctly;
  charset-normalizer 79.6%, uchardet 69.3%, cchardet 2.4.0 62.6% (`detection-accuracy.md`).
- **Most failures were wrong answers, not empty ones.** A silently wrong decode is accepted by the next step. 18.5% of
  charset-normalizer's answers and 21.7% of ICU's decoded to the wrong text without an error, against 5.1% for chardet 7.7.0.
- **Short files are the weak point.** A 5-character file was read correctly 87.5% of the time by chardet 7.7.0 and 47.4% by
  charset-normalizer; a 10-character file 93.3% and 60.8%. Fields in a spreadsheet, a subtitle line and a name list are all this
  short.
- **UTF-16 claims.** charset-normalizer and ICU answered UTF-16 on legacy bytes (181 and 430 of 4,086), which decodes to
  unrelated text without error.
- **Extensions matter, and so does the label.** Hong Kong text was read correctly by chardet 7.7.0 (98.3%) and charset-normalizer
  (78.5%); the C-family detectors and ICU answer `BIG5`, which Python's `big5` cannot decode (0.0%), though read as a WHATWG
  label several of them reach 65% to 82%. Code page 932 text with the fullwidth tilde (the usual extension in the samples) was
  read correctly by chardet 7.7.0 (87.6%) and charset-normalizer (55.9%; 77.5% as WHATWG names); the others answer
  `Shift_JIS` (0.0% literally, 59.2% to 66.3% as WHATWG names).

## A path

1. Try a byte-order mark and strict UTF-8 first. Of 3,613 samples in the 8-bit legacy encodings, 6 were also valid UTF-8 (5
   at 5 characters, 1 at 10, none at 30 or more; measured 2026-11-05, `results/utf8_validity.json`). ISO-2022-JP, being 7-bit, always
   passes a UTF-8 check, so a file with escape sequences needs its own test.
2. If that fails, call a detector and take its answer as a hypothesis, not a decision.
3. Decode with `strict` under the hypothesis; if it raises, try the widest sibling (`big5hkscs`, `cp932`, `gb18030`, `cp949`).
4. Show the person a preview of the first lines in the chosen encoding and let them override it.
5. Store the encoding you chose beside the file.

## Check before committing

- Collect fifty real files from the intended users and decode them with your pipeline; count the wrong previews by eye. The
  survey's numbers are from encyclopedia and conversation text and may not match your users' files.
- Test files with a single line, a header only, and a trailing partial line: the shortest cases are where the measured
  detectors diverge most.
- If you pick chardetng, set `allow_utf8` and treat its labels as WHATWG names.

# Reading Web Pages Whose Declared Charset May Be Wrong

## The situation

A crawler or a link-preview service fetches pages in East Asian languages. Most declare UTF-8. Some declare a charset in a header
or meta tag that the body does not match, such as a page labeled `gb2312` that contains GBK characters, or a Big5 page with Hong
Kong extensions. The service reads the first few kilobytes, decides, and decodes the rest.

## What the measurements say

- **Pages are almost all UTF-8.** W3Techs reports UTF-8 on 99.1% of websites in November 2026, and each legacy East Asian
  encoding on fewer than 0.1% (accessed 2026-11-05). The legacy cases are a small, long tail.
- **Labels already mean something else in browsers.** The WHATWG Encoding Standard decodes the label `gb2312` as GBK, `big5-hkscs`
  as its Big5, and `windows-31j` and `ms932` as its Shift_JIS (read 2026-11-05). A service that mimics browsers should use the
  same mapping rather than Python's codec of the same name. In Python, `gbk` or `gb18030` is the safer decode for a page labeled
  `gb2312`, and `big5hkscs` for `big5`.
- **chardetng is built for this job, with conditions.** Read as WHATWG names its answers decoded the original text 96.7% of the
  time; by default it never answers UTF-8 and changes its answer to `windows-1252` when the buffer ends inside a character (58 of 58 cut
  buffers in four two-byte codecs) (`detection-accuracy.md`). Both matter when reading the first kilobytes of a page.
- **A cut buffer is the normal case.** A fixed-size prefix of CJK text, where most characters take two bytes or more, often ends
  inside a character. charset-normalizer
  gave no answer on all 58 cut buffers and chardetng answered windows-1252 on all 58; the other six detectors (chardet 7, chardet 5.2.0, cchardet, faust-cchardet, uchardet and ICU) gave the same answer as on whole characters.
- **Declared charset first, detection second.** The detectors tested were given no markup hints. chardet 7 documents a markup
  stage that reads declarations (read from its documentation); it was not tested here.

## A path

1. Honor a byte-order mark, then the HTTP `Content-Type` charset, then a `<meta>` declaration in the first 1,024 bytes, mapped
   through the WHATWG label table.
2. Decode strictly with the mapped codec; a failure is evidence the declaration is wrong.
3. On failure, trim any incomplete trailing bytes, then detect, then retry with the widest sibling codec.
4. Record which step decided, for the pages that needed step 3.

## Check before committing

- Sample a few thousand pages from your own crawl, label the legacy ones by hand, and score the pipeline with strict decoding
  of the whole page, not of the detection prefix.
- Test cuts at the prefix length your own pipeline uses; the published cut test covers 2,048- and 8,192-byte prefixes cut 1 to 3 bytes into a character.
- Check what your fallback does to pages that are valid UTF-8 with a wrong declaration; browsers follow the declaration, and
  matching them may be the wrong goal for an indexer.

S2 measured how the tools behave today. S4 asks about the next two years for each tool and for the standards under them.

| Question | Evidence | File |
|---|---|---|
| Who maintains each detector, and what could end that? | Registry release histories, repository activity, changelogs, license fields, read on 2026-11-05 | `viability-detectors.md` |

## The demand side

The strategic fact for this category is on the demand side: UTF-8 is on 99.1% of websites (W3Techs, November 2026), so the tools
that guess, convert and repair legacy East Asian encodings serve a shrinking population of files. They are not growth projects;
they are maintained for as long as someone is migrating an archive.

# S4 Recommendation: Choosing for Two Years

| Decision | Choose | Because | Hedge |

| Detector to depend on | chardet 7.x, pinned to an exact version | Permissive license (0BSD), no dependencies, 17 releases since February 2026, and the strongest measured results under Python's codec names (chardetng leads narrowly under the WHATWG reading) | Wrap it behind one function; re-run an accuracy check on every upgrade, since the 7.x line is a rewrite with bursts of change |
| A second detector for web content | chardetng (Rust crate, or chardetng-py with care) | Described by its crate as a detector for legacy web content; 1.0.0 since 2026-03-30 | The Python wrapper has had no release since 2025-07-27; label translation is yours to maintain |
| Detector to avoid starting on | faust-cchardet | The original cchardet shipped 2.2.0 to 2.4.0 after the fork's last release | If already in use, move to cchardet 2.4.0 or chardet 7 |
| Detector with licensing risk | cchardet, uchardet | Three-way MPL, GPL, LGPL license; choose the MPL or LGPL option and read the notice file | chardet 7 (0BSD) and charset-normalizer (MIT) avoid the question |
| Repair | `ftfy.fix_encoding`, pinned | No release since 2024-10-26 and the stall test reads `stalled`; pure Python with one small dependency | Pin it, and test on each new Python release; a vendored copy is the fallback |
| Variant conversion | OpenCC | Active, Apache-2.0, largest community | For Wikipedia's conventions, zhconv-rs after reading its license |
| Transcoding | Python's codecs, plus glibc iconv where the standard has moved | Standard library stability; but the codec tables lag the standards | Test against iconv on any producer that changes editions |

## What would change this advice

- A chardet release that changes results materially, or a stall in its release cadence. The measurements here are of 7.7.0.
- A CPython release that updates the GB 18030 tables (none found as of 2026-11-05) or fixes issue 160307.
- A charset-normalizer release that handles input cut inside a character; today it returns nothing.
- cchardet's maintainer going quiet again after the 2.2.0 to 2.4.0 sprint of September and October 2026.

## The position that does not depend on any tool

Convert to UTF-8 at the boundary once, name the producer's encoding in a record, keep the source bytes, and do not detect the
same file twice. The demand for detectors is shrinking, and an archive converted once stops needing one.

# Viability: Detectors

The question for each tool is whether it will still be maintained, licensed compatibly and installable in two years. The
facts are from the PyPI JSON API, the GitHub REST API, the projects' own changelogs and documentation, and W3Techs, all read on
2026-11-05.

## The demand curve is falling, and that is the strategic fact

W3Techs reports UTF-8 on 99.1% of websites in November 2026, with ISO-8859-1 at 0.8% and Windows-1252 at 0.2%
(source: https://w3techs.com/technologies/overview/character_encoding, accessed 2026-11-05). Shift JIS, EUC-KR, GB2312,
Big5, GBK, GB18030 and Big5-HKSCS each appear on fewer than 0.1% of websites (same page). The legacy East Asian encodings
survive in files, databases, interchange formats and archives rather than on the public web, which changes who needs
detection: migration projects, archive readers and ingest pipelines, not web crawlers.

A shrinking, specialist user base predicts slow maintenance, not abandonment, for the tools that already exist. It also
means a new detector is unlikely to appear; the maintained set is the set to choose from.

## Per tool

## What to do about it

- Treat detection as a replaceable component behind one function in your code, and keep the input bytes and the chosen
  encoding in the output record. Two years from now the best tool may differ, and old decisions should be re-runnable.
- Pin versions. In this survey's own measurements the answer for the same bytes changed between chardet 5.2.0 and 7.7.0
  (see S2 `detection-accuracy.md`).
- Where a project vendors a detector and licensing matters, chardet 7 (0BSD) and charset-normalizer (MIT) are the permissive
  options; cchardet and uchardet are copyleft-optioned tri-licenses.

## Standards and system libraries

| Item | State on 2026-11-05 | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| GB 18030-2022 | In force since 2023-08-01; 55 code positions changed from the 2005 edition (English Wikipedia, GB 18030 article, read 2026-11-05) | Python's codec follows the 2005 mapping on 3.12.3, 3.13.15 and 3.14.7 (measured); glibc 2.39's iconv follows the 2022 mapping (measured on the probed sequences), and ICU 73.2 and later do too (read). Data from a producer that adopted the 2022 edition can decode differently in Python than in a C program on the same machine |
| Python's CJK codecs | CPython's tracker has no open issue about GB 18030-2022 (searched `GB18030` in python/cpython on 2026-11-05); one open issue about incremental decoders dropping input under `surrogateescape` (gh-160307, opened 2026-10-22) | Do not wait for the standard library to catch up; transcode with glibc iconv or ICU where the 2022 mapping matters |
| WHATWG Encoding Standard | Repository pushed 2026-10-19 | Browsers decode by this standard, not by Python's tables; a page that looks right in a browser can fail strict Python decoding |
| UTF-8 share of the web | 99.1% (W3Techs, November 2026) | Legacy encodings are a data-migration problem, not a growth area |

## Strategic summary

- The detectors will not be replaced; they will be maintained at a slow pace by small teams. Choose by measured behavior on
  your data (S2) and by license (S4 detectors file), and wrap the choice so it can change.
- The standards underneath are moving faster than the codec tables in Python. The risk is silent: a GB 18030-2022 file decodes
  without error to different characters for the positions whose mapping moved, eleven of which S2 `codec-fidelity.md` probes.
- The long-term safe position is to convert to UTF-8 at the boundary once, with the producer's encoding named in a
  record, and never again detect.
