**What the research found**

- **On 400 horizontal rendered images (25 aligned sentences in each of Simplified Chinese, Traditional Chinese, Japanese and Korean; clean, serif, 18 px and degraded), PaddleOCR 3.7.0 with its default PP-OCRv6 models had a character error rate of 0.3% overall (0.2 to 0.5% by language), Tesseract 5.3.4 in single-block page mode 1.5% (0.6 to 2.2%), EasyOCR 1.7.2 6.7% (4.0 to 9.6%), and RapidOCR 3.10.0 9.8% overall, which is 0.8 to 3.8% on Chinese and Japanese and 32.7% on Korean (measured 2026-11-05; harness/1-166-ocr-cjk/results/scores.json).** — For horizontal printed text PaddleOCR was lowest in every language and condition; the ranking does not extend to photographs or handwriting, which were not measured.
- **Tesseract's default automatic page layout failed on about one horizontal image in six (62 of 400 with the `best` data had a CER over 25%, 46 of them Korean, 8 Korean images empty); its single-block page mode, with the same data, cut that to 5 of 400, and forcing 300 DPI in the default mode left Korean at 41.2% CER while Chinese fell to 2.2 to 2.4% (measured 2026-11-05; results/scores.json, results/hyp/).** — Tesseract's page mode is part of the product for CJK; test it on your own layouts before judging the data files.
- **On 45 vertical images (Chinese and Japanese columns, right to left) Tesseract with its vertical data files and page mode 5 had 0.5 to 7.6% CER; PaddleOCR and RapidOCR had 73 to 85% CER as emitted while recognizing 94% of the characters, because they emit the columns left to right; with the lines reversed in scoring (a scorer-only reading, not an engine run) PaddleOCR PP-OCRv6 had 8.7, 10.0 and 9.8% (Simplified, Traditional, Japanese), RapidOCR medium 8.2, 11.6 and 5.8%, PP-OCRv5 server 11.0 to 20.7% and RapidOCR small 9.2 to 21.2%; EasyOCR stayed at 98 to 99% and recognized 1.4% of the characters. Tesseract was given the vertical data and page mode 5; the others had no direction hint (measured 2026-11-05; results/scores.json, cer_reversed_lines).** — Tesseract reads vertical columns in order when told the direction; PaddleOCR and RapidOCR need a right-to-left column sort on their output, which was approximated in scoring and not built.
- **Reading the 200 horizontal Chinese images with the other Chinese script's model raised CER from 2.0 and 2.2% to 31.9 and 31.8% for Tesseract, and from 4.0 and 9.6% to 34.7 and 36.6% for EasyOCR, with no error raised; in PaddleOCR 3.7.0 the Simplified, Traditional and Japanese settings load the same models (read from its model-selection code) (measured 2026-11-05; results/scores.json).** — A pipeline that meets both Chinese scripts needs a script check or a single model for both; the language setting is silent.
- **PyPI's paddlepaddle 3.3.1 (2026-03-26) has no Linux ARM64 wheel (newest with one: 3.2.2); PaddleOCR's default of 10 CPU threads segfaulted on an 8-core ARM machine and worked with 8 (observed at setup; the terminal output was not saved); PyPI's Linux ARM64 PyTorch wheel made an EasyOCR environment 5.6 GB against 1.1 GB with the CPU wheel; RapidOCR saves its models inside the package directory (272 MB environment before, 537 MB after) (measured 2026-11-05; results/install.json, results/runs/).** — Installing on Linux ARM64 needs checks on the PaddlePaddle wheel, the PyTorch build and the thread count; x86-64 was not tested.


**What the research recommends**

**Default** — PaddleOCR with the model generation pinned, for horizontal printed Chinese, Japanese and Korean

| Use case | Pick |
|---|---|

# CJK OCR: Domain Explainer

## What this solves

You have text in images (scanned pages, photographs of signs, screenshots, forms) and you need it as characters that a
computer can search, translate or store. Doing that is optical character recognition (OCR). For Chinese, Japanese and
Korean the same task is harder than for English, for reasons that decide which tool works.

People meet this in several places: digitizing archives and books, reading screenshots and receipts, translating a
photographed menu or label, indexing scanned documents for search, and checking that a printed form matches a database.

## Why CJK is harder

- **Thousands of characters instead of dozens.** A recognizer chooses among several thousand common characters, and many
  differ by one stroke (土 and 士 differ only in the length of one horizontal line).
- **Chinese comes in two scripts.** Simplified and Traditional share some characters and differ in many. A model trained
  for one gives wrong output on the other, and the engine will not tell you.
- **Mixed content is normal.** Japanese runs kanji, kana, Latin letters and digits together; Korean pages often carry Chinese
  characters, Latin words and numbers.
- **Vertical writing exists.** Japanese books and Traditional Chinese signage run in columns from right to left. Engines built
  around horizontal lines do not read columns unless told to.
- **No spaces between words.** Gaps do not help find where one unit ends.

## How the tools work

Most engines work in two steps. A *detector* finds where text lines are in the picture. A *recognizer* reads each line crop and
outputs characters. Tesseract adds a classical layout analysis before its recognizer; PaddleOCR, EasyOCR and RapidOCR use a
neural detector. Newer vision-language models read the whole page in one step and write text or Markdown; they handle layout
well and can invent text that is not on the page.

An engine picks its recognizer by a *language setting*. That setting is part of the input: the right one for the page's
script has to be chosen by the person or by a separate step.

## Document text and scene text

A scan or screenshot has straight lines, even lighting and a plain background. A photograph of a sign or product has
perspective, glare, curved surfaces and busy backgrounds. Engines tuned for pages can fail on photographs. This survey's
harness uses rendered pages with controlled degradation; it did not photograph anything, so scene text is not measured.

## Handwriting

Handwriting is a separate problem with separate tools and is covered by the handwriting survey in this series (1.162). The
engines here are built and documented for print. Amazon's Textract documentation says handwriting is supported in English only.

## What a result looks like

Accuracy is usually reported as character error rate (CER): the edits needed to turn the output into the reference, divided
by the reference length. A CER of 0.02 means two wrong characters per hundred. Published percentages from other sources
depend on the images, the language, the normalization and the reader, and do not transfer. S2 reports CER from one set of
rendered images, with the same rules for every engine.

Two other things matter more than the average:

- **Whether the failure is loud or quiet.** A missing line is easy to spot; a swapped look-alike character is not.
- **Whether the output order is the reading order.** A correct set of characters in the wrong order is still wrong text.

## Build or buy

Hosted services (Amazon Textract, Google Cloud Vision) take an image and return text, with no model to install and a
per-image price that changes; the image leaves your control. Open engines run on your machine, cost nothing per image, and
come with install weight, model downloads and upgrades that can change output. The choice depends on volume, on whether
the images may leave the building, and on who maintains the pipeline. S4 treats it as a structure rather than a break-even
number, because the price lists were not checked (2026-11-05).

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing the language setting once.** A pipeline that reads Simplified Chinese with the Simplified setting and meets
  Traditional pages will degrade without an error.
- **Reading the engine's confidence as accuracy.** Confidence scores come from the same model that made the error.
- **Testing on clean pages and deploying on photographs.** In S2 the leading engines changed little between clean and
  degraded rendered pages (PaddleOCR 0.3% to 0.5%), but a rendered page that is blurred is not a photograph; only a sample of
  your own images shows how an engine behaves on yours.
- **Not pinning the model version.** PaddleOCR's default model changed generations between library versions.
- **Assuming the output is in reading order.** For vertical text it often is not.

# The Category: Reading Printed Chinese, Japanese and Korean From Images

Optical character recognition (OCR) turns a picture of text into text. For Chinese, Japanese and Korean the problem differs
from Latin script in four ways that decide which tools work:

- **Large character sets.** A recognizer has to tell apart thousands of characters, many of which differ by one stroke.
- **Two scripts for Chinese.** Simplified and Traditional share many characters and differ in many others; Japanese and
  Korean text also mixes in kana, hangul and Latin letters and digits.
- **Vertical writing.** Japanese and Traditional Chinese print vertical columns, read right to left; most engines were built
  for horizontal lines.
- **No spaces.** Word gaps do not help find line or character boundaries, and text-line cutting has to work from the glyphs.

The category splits by how the engine gets from pixels to characters, which also predicts its install weight and its failure
mode.

| Model | How it works | What it needs | Entries | File |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Classical engine with a small recurrent network | Layout analysis, line cutting, then a recurrent line recognizer per language | A C++ library and one data file per language | Tesseract | `tesseract.md` |
| Detector plus recognizer, trained for East Asian text | A text-box detector, then a line recognizer; one family of models for Chinese, Japanese, Traditional Chinese | An inference runtime, model downloads | PaddleOCR | `paddleocr.md` |
| Detector plus recognizer, general multilingual | CRAFT-style detector, per-language recognizer | PyTorch, model downloads | EasyOCR | `easyocr.md` |
| Repackaged runtimes and other engines | The same model families on ONNX Runtime, or other designs | Varies | RapidOCR, CnOCR, docTR, MMOCR, manga-ocr | `other-engines.md` |
| Vision-language models and parsers | A model that reads the whole page and writes text or Markdown | A GPU for comfort; large weights | PaddleOCR-VL, Surya, olmOCR, DeepSeek-OCR, MinerU | `other-engines.md` |
| Hosted services | The vendor runs the model | An account and a network call | Google Cloud Vision, Amazon Textract, Azure | `hosted-services.md` |

# EasyOCR

JaidedAI/EasyOCR (Apache-2.0), a PyTorch pipeline: a CRAFT text detector and a per-language recognizer. The last release is
1.7.2 on 2024-09-24; the repository's last push was 2025-12-05 (GitHub, 2026-11-05). Its README advertises "80+ supported
languages" and lists handwriting support under "What's coming next".

## What to know

- **Language combinations.** A reader is built from a list of languages. The README states "not all languages can be used
  together" and that English is compatible with every language. Simplified Chinese and Traditional Chinese are separate
  language codes, as are Japanese and Korean; the harness builds one reader per script.
- **Install.** It pulls PyTorch. On Linux ARM64 the PyPI torch wheel brings the CUDA libraries, so the default environment
  measured 5.6 GB; with the CPU wheel index it measured 1.1 GB (`S2-comprehensive/install-and-memory.md`).
- **Models** download on first use into a per-user directory.
- **Maintenance.** No release in over two years; see `S2-comprehensive/maintenance-and-licensing.md` for the measured state.
- **No documented vertical mode.** The README does not mention vertical text.

# PaddleOCR

Baidu's open-source OCR toolkit, PaddlePaddle/PaddleOCR (Apache-2.0). Version 3.7.0 was released 2026-06-11; the repository
was last pushed 2026-09-16 (GitHub, 2026-11-05). The README reads: "PP-OCRv6 supports 50 languages with a single unified model
(Chinese, English, Japanese, and 46 Latin-script languages)".

## Generations and routing

- **PP-OCRv6** is the library default for Chinese, Traditional Chinese (`chinese_cht`) and Japanese in 3.7.0, with a medium
  detector and medium recognizer. **Korean is not in the v6 set**; the library routes Korean to the PP-OCRv5 server detector and the Korean PP-OCRv5 mobile recognizer.
- **The default moved in one minor release.** In 3.6.0 the library default for Chinese was PP-OCRv5 server (read from that
  version's source on 2026-11-05); in 3.7.0 it is PP-OCRv6 medium.
- **PP-OCRv5** remains selectable: a server detector and server recognizer for Chinese, Traditional Chinese and Japanese, and a
  mobile Korean recognizer.
- The recognizer is chosen by a language argument, so a Traditional Chinese page read with the Simplified setting is a user
  error the library does not catch.
- **Document parsing.** PP-StructureV3 and the vision-language PaddleOCR-VL family (v3.3.0 on 2025-10-16, 109 languages; 1.5
  on 2026-01-29, 111 languages; 1.6 on 2026-05-28) sit in the same repository. They address page structure, tables and
  formulas, not only text lines, and are outside this survey's measured panel.

## Install behavior

- Core install is minimal since 3.2.0; optional features are extras.
- It needs the PaddlePaddle framework. On PyPI, `paddlepaddle` 3.3.1 (2026-03-26) ships wheels for Linux x86-64, Windows and
  macOS ARM only. The newest release with a Linux ARM64 wheel is 3.2.2. See `S2-comprehensive/install-and-memory.md`.
- Models download on first use from a model hub into a per-user cache.

## Where it fits

Chinese-first and Japanese work where accuracy on printed text matters and an inference runtime with a few hundred megabytes of
weights is acceptable. Korean is served by an older model generation than the other three languages.

# Tesseract

The long-running open-source engine, maintained at tesseract-ocr/tesseract (Apache-2.0). Version 5.5.4 was released on
2026-10-28 and the repository was last pushed 2026-11-02 (GitHub records, 2026-11-05). The 5.x line uses a recurrent network
(LSTM) line recognizer after a layout-analysis stage that cuts the page into blocks and lines.

## CJK in practice

- **Data files.** One file per language and script: Simplified Chinese, Traditional Chinese, Japanese and Korean, each with a
  separate vertical-text variant. Two sets exist: a "fast" set (integer-quantized, smaller) and a "best" set (floating point,
  larger). Both repositories are static: last pushes 2024-08-01 and 2024-03-09, no release since 2021-02-16. The models used
  today predate 2025.
- **Vertical text.** Handled by selecting a vertical data file and a single-block page mode; the horizontal files do not read
  vertical columns.
- **Install.** A system package or a source build, plus data files. The distribution package on the harness machine was 5.3.4
  with only English data installed, so every CJK file had to be fetched. Distribution packages lag the upstream release.
- **No GPU path.** CPU only; no framework dependency.
- **Python access.** Wrappers such as pytesseract 0.3.13 (PyPI, 2024-08) call the command-line program.

## Where it fits

Clean, horizontal, printed pages in a pipeline that already uses Tesseract for Latin text, or an environment where nothing
heavier than a C++ library can be installed. The S2 harness measures how far its accuracy falls when the page is small,
degraded or vertical.

# Accuracy on Rendered Printed Text

Measured 2026-11-05 on CPU with `harness/1-166-ocr-cjk/`: 445 images of 25 aligned sentences in Simplified Chinese,
Traditional Chinese, Japanese and Korean, rendered in clean, serif, small and degraded conditions, plus 45 vertical images
(Chinese and Japanese). Every number is character error rate (CER) in percent, lower is better: edits needed to turn the
output into the drawn text, divided by the drawn text's length, after Unicode NFKC normalization with whitespace removed.
Results are in `results/scores.json`; each configuration's run record has the engine versions and the machine state.

These are rendered pages, not photographs. The text is drawn in Noto fonts on a plain background, so 0.2 to 0.5% on clean rendered print is near a ceiling and does not rank the engines on real scans or
photographs. See `approach.md` for what else that leaves out.

## Horizontal print, by language (clean, serif, small and degraded images together, 400 images)

| Config | Simplified | Traditional | Japanese | Korean | All |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PaddleOCR PP-OCRv6 (library default) | 0.2 | 0.3 | 0.3 | 0.5 | 0.3 |
| PaddleOCR PP-OCRv5 server | 0.3 | 0.4 | 1.0 | 0.5 | 0.6 |
| Tesseract `best`, single-block page mode | 2.0 | 2.2 | 0.6 | 1.6 | 1.5 |
| Tesseract `fast`, single-block page mode | 2.1 | 1.7 | 0.9 | 1.5 | 1.5 |
| EasyOCR 1.7.2 | 4.0 | 9.6 | 5.5 | 7.9 | 6.7 |
| RapidOCR, PP-OCRv6 medium (Chinese and Japanese: 1.5 to 2.9%) | 1.5 | 1.8 | 2.9 | 29.6 | 9.1 |
| RapidOCR, PP-OCRv6 small, library default (Chinese and Japanese: 0.8 to 3.8%) | 0.8 | 0.9 | 3.8 | 32.7 | 9.8 |
| Tesseract `best`, default page mode | 5.4 | 4.7 | 1.1 | 34.7 | 11.3 |
| Tesseract `fast`, default page mode | 7.3 | 8.5 | 3.6 | 35.3 | 13.5 |

Notes that change how to read the table:

- **Tesseract's page mode decides its result.** With its default automatic layout, about one horizontal image in six had a
  CER over 25% (62 of 400 for `best`, 74 for `fast`), mostly Korean (46 of 100 for `best`), and 8 Korean images came back
  empty. With the single-block mode the count fell to 5 of 400. The data files are the same.
  The PNGs carry no resolution metadata. In the default mode Tesseract printed "Estimating resolution as N" (228 to 378 in
  the first lines logged); stderr was non-empty for all 400 horizontal images in that mode and empty in the single-block mode. Forcing 300 DPI with the
  default page mode (`tess_best_dpi300`, `best` data, the same 400 images) moved Simplified Chinese from 5.4 to 2.4%,
  Traditional from 4.7 to 2.2% and Japanese from 1.1 to 0.7%, and Korean from 34.7 to 41.2%, with 11 Korean pages empty against 8 in the default mode: the Korean failure does not
  depend on the resolution estimate, and the Chinese failures partly do. Whoever adopts Tesseract for CJK
  has to test the page mode on their own pages; a result from the default can look like a model failure.
- **PaddleOCR is lowest in every language and every horizontal condition**, in both model generations; PP-OCRv5 server is
  slightly worse than PP-OCRv6 on Japanese and on small and degraded images.
- **RapidOCR drops leading characters on Korean lines** (inspected outputs begin partway into the line or with wrong
  characters) and is weaker on Japanese. It uses the same model family as PaddleOCR, so the gap is in its pipeline or
  model conversion and not in the models' ability; the cause was not isolated. A larger model made little difference.
- **EasyOCR is worst on Traditional Chinese** (9.6 against 4.0 for Simplified), with three images over 25% CER.

# S2 Approach: What Was Run, What Was Read, What Was Not Done

## Run (harness `harness/1-166-ocr-cjk/`)

Twelve configurations over 445 rendered images with known text, on CPU, one at a time, each under a 3 GB memory cap:

| Config | What it is |
|---|---|
| `tess_fast`, `tess_best` | Tesseract 5.3.4 (the distribution package on the machine; upstream is at 5.5.4) with the `fast` and `best` data sets, default page mode |
| `tess_fast_psm6`, `tess_best_psm6` | The same, with the single-block page mode for horizontal images |
| `paddle_v6` | PaddleOCR 3.7.0 library defaults (PP-OCRv6 medium detector and recognizer for Chinese, Traditional Chinese, Japanese; Korean: PP-OCRv5 server detector and the Korean PP-OCRv5 mobile recognizer) |
| `paddle_v5` | PaddleOCR 3.7.0 with the PP-OCRv5 server detector and recognizer for Chinese, Traditional Chinese and Japanese (Korean: PP-OCRv5 server detector and the Korean PP-OCRv5 mobile recognizer, as in `paddle_v6`) |
| `rapid` | RapidOCR 3.10.0 on ONNX Runtime (PP-OCRv6 small for Chinese, Traditional Chinese, Japanese; Korean: PP-OCRv5 mobile detector (`ch_PP-OCRv5_det_mobile`) and the Korean PP-OCRv5 mobile recognizer) |
| `rapid_medium` | The same with PP-OCRv6 medium for Chinese, Traditional Chinese and Japanese, (Korean: PP-OCRv5 server detector and the Korean PP-OCRv5 mobile recognizer), run to separate model size from the engine |
| `easyocr` | EasyOCR 1.7.2 on CPU PyTorch |
| `tess_best_psm6_cross`, `easyocr_cross` | The 200 horizontal Chinese images read with the other Chinese script's model |

**Images.** Twenty-five aligned sentences from Flores-200 (CC BY-SA 4.0) in Simplified Chinese, Traditional Chinese, Japanese
and Korean, rendered with Noto Sans and Noto Serif CJK (SIL OFL) into five conditions: `clean` (32 px sans), `serif` (32 px),
`small` (18 px sans), `degraded` (28 px with blur, low contrast, noise, a 2 degree skew and JPEG quality 35), and `vertical`
(32 px columns read right to left, for Chinese and Japanese only; 15 sentences per language). The ground truth is the text that
was drawn, so there is no transcription error in the reference.

**Rules that were the same for every engine.** The correct language setting for each image's script; CPU; all cores; no
orientation or unwarping preprocessing; no direction hint for vertical images except that Tesseract was given its `_vert` data and page mode 5; the others got no hint (the harness did not look for one); each engine's own line order, with nothing re-sorted (vertical images are also scored with the lines reversed, a scorer-only reading shown beside the as-emitted figure); the same normalization before
comparing (Unicode NFKC, whitespace removed). The measure is character error rate (CER), micro-averaged. Where one setting
differs between engines because the engine's own routing demands it (Korean on PP-OCRv5 models in both Paddle and Rapid, with a server detector in Paddle and in `rapid_medium` and a mobile detector in `rapid`; the models each config loaded are in `results/models_loaded.json`), the
table says so.

**What a low number means.** The test text is rendered in Noto fonts on a plain background, so 0.2 to 0.5% on clean rendered print is near a ceiling and does not rank the engines on real scans or
photographs.

**Tesseract's page mode.** The first Tesseract run used its default automatic page layout (`--psm 3`). It returned empty or
scrambled output on some large-text images, so the single-block mode (`--psm 6`) was run as a second configuration, and both
are shown. The other engines were run with their library defaults; the one added variant, RapidOCR's medium models, tests
whether model size explains its gap to PaddleOCR. No engine was otherwise tuned. PaddleOCR's default of 10 CPU threads
segfaults on this 8-core ARM build, so it ran with 8.

## Read, not run

Feature claims in `feature-comparison.md` marked "read" come from each project's README or documentation, fetched 2026-11-05.
Hosted services were not called.

## Not done

- **Photographs, real scans and handwriting.** All images are rendered, so perspective, glare, paper texture and real
  scanner noise are absent. Scene text is not measured.
- **Timing.** The machine carried other work throughout (1-minute load average between 1 and 15). Per-image seconds were
  recorded in the harness but are not published.
- **Cloud services, vision-language models, Surya, CnOCR, docTR, MMOCR and manga-ocr.** Read only.
- **GPU.** None was available; GPU behavior is not measured.
- **x86-64.** The machine is aarch64 (ARM). Install behavior on x86-64 may differ; the PaddlePaddle ARM wheel gap is specific
  to this architecture.
- **Sample size.** 25 sentences per language per condition (15 vertical). A difference of a few tenths of a percent between
  two engines on one language is inside the noise; the bootstrap intervals are in `results/scores.json`.

## Behavior that decides installs

- **PaddlePaddle has no Linux ARM64 wheel for its latest release.** PyPI's `paddlepaddle` 3.3.1 (2026-03-26) lists wheels
  for Linux x86-64, Windows and macOS ARM only; the newest release with a Linux ARM64 wheel is 3.2.2. `uv` refused the
  3.3.1 pin ("doesn't have a source distribution or wheel for the current platform") and the harness uses 3.2.2.
  PaddleOCR 3.7.0 runs on it. On x86-64 this does not arise.
- **PaddleOCR's default CPU thread count crashes on an 8-core ARM machine.** The library default is 10 threads; with it the
  process died with a segmentation fault on the first image. Setting the thread count to the core count (8) worked, as did 1,
  2 and 4. These were observed once during setup on 2026-11-05 and the terminal output was not saved; the thread count was
  not retried afterwards, and other hardware was not tried.
- **PyPI's Linux ARM64 PyTorch wheel carries the CUDA libraries.** The default EasyOCR install on this machine was 5.6 GB
  (`du` read once at setup; the environment was then replaced, so the figure is a recorded constant),
  of which the `nvidia` packages were 3.3 GB and `triton` 0.9 GB, on a machine with no GPU. Installing the CPU wheel from
  PyTorch's CPU index brought it to 1.1 GB.
- **RapidOCR stores models inside the installed package** and downloads them on first use from a model-hosting site. A
  read-only install or a container image built without network access has to include them in advance.
- **Model sets differ by language inside one engine.** PaddleOCR routes Chinese, Traditional Chinese and Japanese to the
  PP-OCRv6 medium pair and Korean to the PP-OCRv5 server detector and the Korean PP-OCRv5 mobile recognizer, so a Korean-and-Chinese service loads four
  models. RapidOCR's small v6 pair has no Korean model; asking for one raises an error that names the supported list.
- **Tesseract has no framework**, and the harness ran it as a command-line program per image.

| Horizontal print, and no deep-learning framework or small memory is a hard limit | Tesseract in single-block page mode | 0.6 to 2.2% CER, 70 to 134 MB per process, no framework; needs the page mode set |
| Vertical Chinese or Japanese | Tesseract with the vertical data files and page mode 5 | 0.5 to 7.6% CER in order, with the direction supplied; PaddleOCR and RapidOCR recognize the characters but emit columns left to right (6 to 21% CER only after a scorer-side line reversal), EasyOCR fails |
| Chinese of unknown script (Simplified or Traditional) | PaddleOCR | One model serves both (read from the library code and seen in the run log; no wrong-script PaddleOCR run was made); Tesseract and EasyOCR lose about 30 points of CER when given the wrong script |
| An existing PyTorch stack that already loads EasyOCR | Staying with EasyOCR is workable on horizontal Chinese, Japanese and Korean | 4.0 to 9.6% CER, flat across conditions, but stalled upstream; no vertical text |
| An ONNX-only deployment or a small footprint | RapidOCR small, with a Korean check first | 836 MB peak; 0.8 to 3.8% CER on Chinese and Japanese, but 33% on Korean |

## What to check before relying on any row

1. **Run the candidate on 50 of your own pages**, with ground truth for at least some of them. These error rates come from
   25 sentences per language per condition and do not predict a document type with its own fonts.
2. **For Tesseract, test the page mode on your layout.** The default failed on one image in six here, mostly Korean.
3. **For PaddleOCR or RapidOCR on vertical text,** expect the columns in left-to-right order and plan a right-to-left sort on their box positions; the characters were recognized (94%), and the sort itself was not built here.
4. **For any service that sees both Chinese scripts,** feed it pages of each and compare; a wrong-script setting does not
   raise an error.
5. **Pin the model version** for PaddleOCR and RapidOCR; the default generation changed inside the 3.x line.
6. **On Linux ARM64,** check the PaddlePaddle wheel (3.3.1 has none), the PyTorch build (the PyPI default is 5.6 GB with
   CUDA) and, for PaddleOCR, the CPU thread count (the default crashed here).

## What these results do not support

A ranking for photographs of signs, labels or documents; a statement about handwriting; any speed claim; any statement about
hosted services or vision-language models, which were not run.

# Use Case: Digitizing Printed Books and Archives

## Who needs this

A library, archive, publisher or researcher scanning printed pages in Chinese, Japanese or Korean to build searchable text.
Some material is set in vertical columns (Japanese books, Traditional Chinese reprints).

## The pain

- A scanned page is an image; nothing can be searched or quoted.
- Vertical columns are common in the material that is most worth scanning, and most OCR engines read lines.
- Volume is in the tens of thousands of pages, so a hand check of every page is not available.

## Requirements that follow

1. Reads vertical columns in the correct order.
2. Handles both horizontal and vertical pages in one collection.
3. Low enough error that search works; a small residue is acceptable if it can be sampled.
4. Runs offline and in batch; speed matters less than correctness.

## What the measurements say

| Option | Horizontal print | Vertical print, as emitted / lines reversed in scoring |
|---|---|---|
| Tesseract with `_vert` data, page mode 5 (told the direction) | 0.6 to 2.2% CER (single-block mode) | 0.5% Japanese, 4.2 to 5.3% Chinese (`best`); in order, so reversal does not apply |
| PaddleOCR PP-OCRv6 (no direction hint) | 0.2 to 0.5% | 75 to 81% / 8.7 to 10.0% |
| RapidOCR medium (no direction hint) | 1.5 to 2.9% on Chinese and Japanese | 76 to 82% / 5.8 to 11.6% |
| EasyOCR | 4.0 to 9.6% | 98 to 99% / 98 to 99% |

Tesseract reads vertical columns in order when it is given its vertical data and page mode. PaddleOCR and RapidOCR recognized
about 94% of the characters on the vertical pages and emitted the columns left to right; with the lines reversed in scoring
(a stand-in for a right-to-left column sort, not an engine run) they reach 6 to 21% CER. A front end that detects page
direction, cuts columns and orders them could do that for real; it was not built here. The rendered pages are clean; real
scans add bleed-through, stains and variable type.

## The shape that works

Detect page direction first (a sample of lines is enough), then send horizontal pages to a recognizer chosen for accuracy and
vertical pages to Tesseract's vertical files (or to PaddleOCR with a right-to-left column sort in front of its output, once built and checked). Keep the engine and data-file versions with each output page so a later run can
be compared. Sample a few hundred output lines against the page images each time an engine or file changes.

## What to avoid

- Running one engine on a mixed collection and calling the vertical failures an accuracy problem.
- Relying on the default Tesseract page mode without a test on your own layouts.
- Treating high bag-of-characters recall as correct text; order is part of the answer.

## Check before committing

- A sample of real pages from the collection, horizontal and vertical, with hand-corrected text for at least 50.
- Whether pages mix scripts or contain rubi, marginalia or two-column layouts, none of which were rendered here.
- The font and print quality of the oldest material; the harness used modern Noto fonts.
- Not settled by this survey: photographed pages, handwriting, historical variant characters, and the hosted services.

# Use Case: An Intake Pipeline Where the Chinese Script Is Not Known

## Who needs this

A service that accepts uploaded documents or images in Chinese from many sources (mainland, Taiwan, Hong Kong, overseas
communities) and has to extract text without asking the sender which script it is.

## The pain

- Simplified and Traditional pages arrive mixed.
- Most engines make the script a language setting chosen by the caller.
- A wrong setting does not fail; it returns plausible text with many wrong characters.

## Requirements that follow

1. A model or a routing step that works for both scripts.
2. A way to notice when the setting was wrong.
3. Per-script quality that does not differ much, so one script does not become second-class.

## What the measurements say

| Option | Simplified | Traditional | With the wrong script's setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| PaddleOCR PP-OCRv6 | 0.2% CER | 0.3% | Not run: the library loads the same model pair for the Simplified, Traditional and Japanese settings (read from its code and seen in the run log) |
| Tesseract `best`, single-block | 2.0% | 2.2% | 31.9% on Simplified images and 31.8% on Traditional |
| EasyOCR | 4.0% | 9.6% | 34.7% and 36.6% |
| RapidOCR small | 0.8% | 0.9% | not tested |

EasyOCR reads Traditional noticeably worse than Simplified even when set correctly. The figures are on rendered pages in four
conditions with 25 sentences per script; they are not a statement about every Traditional-script typeface or regional
convention.

## The shape that works

Prefer a single model that serves both scripts (PaddleOCR in this sample). If the chosen engine has one model per script, run
a script check first: a short sample read with both settings and the better-scoring one kept, using the engine's own
confidence or a character-set test. That check was not built or measured here.

## What to avoid

- Choosing the setting from the sender's country or the file's language tag.
- Concluding from a clean output that the setting was right; the wrong-script output looks like Chinese.
- Comparing only aggregate error across scripts; per-script numbers hide the gap.

## Check before committing

- A sample of each script from your actual sources, scored separately.
- Whether the sample contains Japanese or Korean pages that will arrive in the Chinese queue; Paddle's shared model
  serves Japanese too, while Tesseract and EasyOCR need a separate setting for it.
- Not settled by this survey: a script-detection step, regional variant characters, and the hosted services (Google lists
  Simplified and Traditional under mapped languages that are "more prone to misidentification").

# Use Case: Photographs, Labels and Filled-In Forms

## Who needs this

A team reading text from camera images (product labels, signs, menus, receipts) or from forms that mix printed fields with
handwritten entries, in Chinese, Japanese or Korean.

## The pain

- Photographs have perspective, glare, curved surfaces and busy backgrounds.
- Forms mix machine print with handwriting; the handwritten entries carry the values that matter.
- A wrong digit or character in a field is a wrong record, not a missed search hit.

## Requirements that follow

1. Robust detection on photographs, not only on straight lines of black on white.
2. Handwriting support, or a way to route handwritten fields to a person.
3. Confidence signals that separate reliable fields from doubtful ones.
4. A review step before values enter a record.

## What the measurements say

Very little. The harness measured rendered printed text only, so none of this persona's conditions were tested:

- The degraded condition (blur, noise, low contrast, 2 degree skew, JPEG compression) is the nearest proxy. PaddleOCR PP-OCRv6
  was at 0.5% CER, Tesseract in single-block mode at 2.1%, EasyOCR at 6.5%, RapidOCR small at 12.0%. A rendered page that is
  blurred is not a photograph of a curved label.
- Among the open engines run here, none documents handwriting support in the README pages read on 2026-11-05; EasyOCR lists it as
  upcoming. Amazon's
  Textract documentation says handwriting is supported in English only. The handwriting survey in this series (1.162) covers
  that problem.
- Hosted services and vision-language models, the plausible candidates for photographed material, were not run.

## The shape that works

Treat the OCR output as a draft for review. Route printed fields to an engine and handwritten fields to review or to a
handwriting-specific tool. Keep the image with every extracted value so a reviewer can check one against the other, and record
the engine and model version.

## What to avoid

- Reading the S2 rendered-text figures as figures for photographs.
- Letting a language model reading a photograph supply text it could not see; check generative readers against a held-out set.
- Auto-accepting values below a confidence threshold that was never calibrated on your own images.

## Check before committing

- 100 real photographs or form scans from the actual source, with hand-corrected text, scored the same way for every
  candidate including any hosted service.
- Whether the images may leave your control; this decides whether hosted services are an option at all.
- The cost of one wrong field against the cost of a human reviewing every field.
- Not settled by this survey: everything photographic, handwriting, and generative readers.

# Use Case: Indexing Screenshots and Captures

## Who needs this

A developer or analyst making screenshots, slides, PDF renders or app captures searchable: clean, horizontal, often small
text, in Chinese, Japanese or Korean mixed with Latin words and numbers.

## The pain

- The text exists on screen but not in the file, so search cannot find it.
- The text is small (UI type at 12 to 18 px) and sits next to icons and photos.
- The language is not always known when the file arrives.

## Requirements that follow

1. Low error on small, clean, horizontal text in all three languages.
2. Latin letters and digits inside CJK lines come out right.
3. Runs on an ordinary server or laptop, possibly without a GPU.
4. Resilient to a wrong language guess.

## What the measurements say

| Option | Small 18 px | Clean and degraded | Memory |
|---|---|---|---|
| PaddleOCR PP-OCRv6 | 0.3% CER | 0.3% and 0.5% | about 2.0 GB with four languages loaded |
| Tesseract, single-block page mode | 1.2% | 1.2% and 2.1% | 70 to 134 MB per process |
| EasyOCR | 7.1% | 6.7% and 6.5% | 1.6 GB |
| RapidOCR small | 8.7% | 9.7% and 12.0%, with 33% on Korean | 0.8 GB |

On rendered text the choice is between PaddleOCR's accuracy and Tesseract's footprint. Real screenshots have anti-aliased
text on colored backgrounds, icons next to words and dark mode, none of which was rendered here.

## The shape that works

Run the engine over each image at its native size, store the text with the image path, engine and model version, and keep the
raw output beside any cleaned text. If the language is unknown, PaddleOCR's one model covers Simplified Chinese, Traditional
Chinese and Japanese; Korean needs its own setting, so a language check comes first for Korean.

## What to avoid

- Resizing small text up or down without testing; the 18 px result was good for PaddleOCR and Tesseract, and the harness did
  not test other sizes.
- Choosing a language per image from the file name or locale without a check.
- Indexing the output without storing the engine version; the PaddleOCR default model changed inside the 3.x line.

## Check before committing

- 100 of your own screenshots with the text you care about, including dark mode and colored backgrounds.
- Whether your text is mostly short UI labels or paragraphs; the harness used full sentences of 18 to 55 characters, not isolated words.
- Not settled by this survey: text on photographs, text under 18 px, and how the vision-language models compare.

# S4 Approach: How Long an Option Stays Usable

S4 asks what happens to a choice over two to three years: who maintains the code, who maintains the models, what can change
without notice, and what it costs to leave. It uses the repository and registry records in
`S2-comprehensive/maintenance-and-licensing.md`, the install behavior in `S2-comprehensive/install-and-memory.md`, and the
S2 error rates. It does not forecast prices, and it does not rank vendors by size.

Four questions are asked of each option:

1. **Maintenance.** Are releases and merged changes still arriving?
2. **Model supply.** Do the models keep improving, and who controls them?
3. **Change risk.** What can alter the output without the adopter doing anything (a default model change, a new library
   version, a vendor update)?
4. **Exit cost.** What would moving to another engine require?

Limits: the evidence is repository and registry state on 2026-11-05 plus one 445-image rendered benchmark. No vendor
roadmap was read, and the hosted services were not called.


---

# S4 Recommendation: Staying Power and Exit Plans

| Option | Staying power | Main risk | Exit plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| PaddleOCR | Strong: active, vendor-backed, new model generations | An upgrade can change the default model; ARM and framework constraints | Pin the model generation; keep the ONNX route through RapidOCR in mind |
| Tesseract | Strong engine, frozen CJK data | The CJK models do not improve; accuracy depends on page mode and configuration | Keep output as plain text and position data; wrap the engine behind one function |
| RapidOCR | Good, tied to PaddleOCR's model releases | Per-language model availability; weak Korean in the sample | Same models as PaddleOCR |
| EasyOCR | Weak: stalled, no release since 2024 | No improvement, aging dependencies, no vertical text | Plan to replace; do not build features on its detection internals |
| MMOCR | Finished | Unmaintained | Do not adopt |
| Vision-language models and hosted services | Active, but unmeasured here | Hallucinated text, license limits (Surya), vendor changes, data leaving control | Convert vendor responses into your own record at the edge |

## Durable points

- **The engine is easy to swap; the sample is not.** A held-out set of your own pages with corrected text is the durable
  asset; every engine in this survey can be replaced behind a function that takes an image and returns text with positions.
- **Pin and record versions.** The PaddleOCR default model generation changed between 3.6.0 (PP-OCRv5) and 3.7.0 (PP-OCRv6), and Tesseract's output
  depends on its page mode and data-file set.
- **Watch the model supply as well as the code.** Tesseract's code is maintained while its CJK models are not; PaddleOCR is
  the reverse risk, with fast-moving models.
- **Revisit within a year.** The category moved from three open engines to those plus ONNX repackagings, document
  vision-language models and hosted services with CJK support in under a year. The harness did not measure the new branch.

## Not settled

Price comparisons (the pricing pages did not render when fetched on 2026-11-05), photographs, handwriting, speed, GPU behavior,
and x86-64 install behavior.

# Viability: Vision-Language Models and Hosted Services

None of these was run. Everything here is from documentation and repository records read on 2026-11-05.

## Vision-language document models

PaddleOCR-VL (1.6, 2026-05-28), olmOCR (0.4.27), DeepSeek-OCR, Surya and MinerU read whole pages and write text or Markdown.
They form a second branch of the category beside line recognizers: PaddleOCR-VL was released 2025-10-16 in the PaddleOCR
repository, beside its line recognizer.

- **Maintenance.** All are active; the dates are in `S1-rapid/other-engines.md`.
- **What they add.** Layout, tables, formulas and reading order, which a line recognizer does not give.
- **What they cost.** Larger weights and, for most, a GPU or a local inference server; Surya's README names vLLM on an NVIDIA
  GPU or llama.cpp and a peak resident memory of about 4.3 to 4.8 GB in its own Apple benchmark.
- **Failure mode.** A language model can write fluent text that is not on the page. A line recognizer fails with wrong
  characters or no output; a generative reader can fail with text that looks right. This survey did not test hallucination;
  a pipeline that moves to this branch needs its own check against a held-out sample.
- **License.** Surya's weights are free for research, personal use and startups under a funding or revenue threshold;
  MinerU's repository shows no recognized license; the others are Apache-2.0 or MIT at the repository level. Model-file
  licenses were not individually checked.

## Hosted services

Amazon Textract and Google Cloud Vision both document CJK support (S1). The trade is the usual one:

- **For:** no model to install, no memory to size, vendor-maintained models.
- **Against:** the image leaves the building; the price and quotas are the vendor's to change; the output can change when the
  vendor updates the model; handwriting in Textract is English-only by its own documentation, and neither service documents
  vertical text.
- **Exit cost.** Moderate. Output formats differ (word and line boxes, confidence fields), so a pipeline that keeps the
  vendor's response shape through the codebase is harder to leave than one that converts to its own record at the edge.

## What would change this picture

- A vision-language model that reads vertical Chinese and Japanese reliably on CPU at an acceptable size.
- A PaddlePaddle or ONNX path with first-class Linux ARM64 support.
- Retrained Tesseract CJK models, or a maintained fork that ships them.

# Viability: Open-Source Engines

## Tesseract

- **Maintenance.** Healthy: release 5.5.4 on 2026-10-28, 68 merged pull requests in 180 days.
- **Model supply.** The weak point. The CJK data files are static: the `fast` and `best` repositories were last pushed in 2024
  and have no release since 2021. Engine releases do not retrain them. Anyone who needs better CJK accuracy from Tesseract has
  to train their own models.
- **Change risk.** Low. The same data file gives the same output; the main variable is the engine version, and distribution
  packages lag (the machine here ran 5.3.4).
- **Exit cost.** Low to moderate. The interface is a command line and a plain-text or position output; the language-setting
  and page-mode choices carry over as concepts to other engines.
- **What the S2 results add.** With the single-block page mode Tesseract's horizontal error was 2.0 to 2.2% on Chinese, 0.6% on
  Japanese and 1.6% on Korean against PaddleOCR's 0.2 to 0.5%; with its vertical data and page mode it read vertical columns
  in order (0.5 to 7.6%), where PaddleOCR and RapidOCR emitted columns left to right; with the default page mode about one horizontal image in six
  failed (CER over 25%), mostly Korean. Its accuracy depends on configuration more than the others did.

## PaddleOCR (and the PP-OCR model family)

- **Maintenance.** Healthy: 3.7.0 on 2026-06-11, 50 merged pull requests in 180 days. A new model generation (PP-OCRv6)
  appeared in the 3.x line.
- **Model supply.** The strongest of the open engines: a vendor-backed team ships new generations, and the same models are
  repackaged by third parties (RapidOCR). Korean lags a generation behind.
- **Change risk.** The highest of the group. The library default moved to a new model generation inside a minor version, and
  Chinese, Traditional Chinese and Japanese now map to one shared model. An unpinned install can change output on upgrade.
  The PaddlePaddle framework also constrains platforms: its latest PyPI release has no Linux ARM64 wheel.
- **Exit cost.** Moderate: the same model files run under ONNX Runtime through RapidOCR, which is a built-in exit from the
  framework (not from the models).

## EasyOCR

- **Maintenance.** Stalled: last release 2024-09-24, no merged pull requests in 180 days.
- **Model supply.** Frozen. Its models do not change, which is stable and also means no improvement.
- **Change risk.** Low from the project; moderate from the dependencies, because PyTorch moves and an old pipeline can
  stop working with a new torch.
- **Exit cost.** Low to moderate. It needs a separate reader per script and has no vertical mode, so there is little
  EasyOCR-specific behavior to migrate away from.
- **What the S2 results add.** On the rendered sample EasyOCR's horizontal error was 4.0% (Simplified Chinese) to 9.6%
  (Traditional Chinese) and 5.5% on Japanese, and it produced almost nothing usable on vertical columns. The sample cannot say
  whether the stalled releases have anything to do with those figures.

## RapidOCR

- **Maintenance.** Healthy: 3.10.0 on 2026-10-08, last pushed 2026-11-05, 20 merged pull requests in 180 days.
- **Model supply.** Depends on PaddleOCR's models converted to ONNX; it follows the PP-OCR generations with a delay.
- **Change risk.** Moderate: defaults changed to PP-OCRv6 small, and a model size that is not available for a language raises
  an error.
- **Exit cost.** Low: the models are the same as PaddleOCR's.

## MMOCR and others

MMOCR has no release since 2023 and should not be chosen for a new project. CnOCR, docTR, manga-ocr and Surya were read, not
run; each is maintained, and each has an open question: CnOCR's scope is Chinese, docTR's README does not describe CJK
models, manga-ocr targets manga, and Surya's weights carry a revenue-linked license.
