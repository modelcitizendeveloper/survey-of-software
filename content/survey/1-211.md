**What the research found**

- **BGE-M3 is the only model at or tied with the top in Chinese, Japanese and Korean** — nDCG@10 0.827 zh (leader), 0.867 ja and 0.819 ko (tied with the leader on ja, e5-large, whose co-leader Ruri v3 was not tested against BGE-M3, and with Nemotron on ko); ties are tested against each leader only, and no other model that ran all three is tied with a leader in more than one (measured 2026-11-05; 150 MIRACL queries per language, about 2,000 passages; harness/1-211-cjk-embedding-models/results/scores.json).
- **Newer and larger is not better on this test** — Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B scored 0.797/0.783/0.743 against BGE-M3's 0.827/0.867/0.819 at 197 against 333 tokens/s and 3.75 against 2.92 GiB; Arctic L v2 and F2LLM-v2 0.6B landed mid-pack, tied with no leader; on zh, Harrier 0.6b, Qwen3 0.6B and F2LLM-v2 0.6B (0.800, 0.797, 0.794) are one group the test cannot order; Qwen3-Embedding-4B did not load in 7 GiB (measured 2026-11-05; same run).
- **Popularity and translation matching do not predict retrieval** — Paraphrase MiniLM, second in 30-day downloads, had point estimates below the BM25 bigram baseline on ja and ko (0.516, 0.477 against 0.635, 0.572; intervals apart on ja, overlapping on ko, so ko is unresolved); LaBSE's point estimates were below it on all three, with overlapping intervals, yet it scored 0.999 on its weakest Flores pair (measured 2026-11-05; same run).
- **Chinese-only models trail on Chinese and break outside it** — M3E 0.721, BGE large zh 0.705, BGE small zh 0.688 and text2vec 0.638 against BGE-M3's 0.827 on Chinese; M3E scored 0.044 on Korean and the specialists' translation matching with Korean stayed below 0.12 hit@1 as a pair mean (0.024 to 0.147 in single directions) (measured 2026-11-05; same run).
- **Japanese and Korean specialists tie the leaders rather than beat them** — Ruri v3 310m and e5-large are co-leaders at 0.879 on Japanese; KURE-v1 (0.816) and BGE-M3 (0.819) are tied with the Korean leader, Nemotron 3.5 1B (0.831) (measured 2026-11-05; same run).


**What the research recommends**

**Default** — BGE-M3, pinned to a commit, beside keyword search; test on your own text before building the index

# CJK Embedding Models: Domain Explainer

**Who this is for:** technical decision makers, product managers and architects who need to choose how to search Chinese,
Japanese or Korean text and have no background in language models.

---

## What This Solves

A shopper in China searches for 便宜的蓝牙耳机, "cheap Bluetooth headphones." A product page says 实惠的无线耳机, "affordable
wireless headphones." The two strings share only 的 and 耳机, and 便宜 and 实惠 have no character in common. A keyword index
ranks that page low or misses it. A person sees the same request.

An **embedding model** closes that gap. It turns each piece of text into a list of numbers, a vector, trained so that text with
similar meaning gets similar numbers. Search then becomes arithmetic: embed the query, find the stored vectors nearest to it, and
return their passages.

Chinese, Japanese and Korean (CJK) text makes this harder than English in three ways:

- **No spaces.** Chinese and Japanese do not mark word boundaries. A system has to work from characters or decide on its own
  where words end.
- **Mixed scripts and spelling systems.** Japanese mixes kanji, hiragana and katakana in one sentence. Chinese has Simplified
  and Traditional forms of the same text. Korean is written in Hangul, with sentence endings that change form a great deal.
- **Shared characters with different meanings.** The same Chinese character may appear in Japanese with a different sense. A
  model that treats them as one token can match the wrong thing.

## Who Runs Into This

- Sites searching their own product catalog, articles, or help pages in one of these languages.
- Companies with internal documents in several languages, where a question in one language should find an answer in another.
- Teams building a chatbot over their documents. The retrieval step in front of the language model is usually embedding search.
- Language-learning, dictionary and research applications that need to find similar sentences.

---

## Accessible Analogies

### Vectors are map coordinates

Think of a map where every sentence has a position, and sentences about the same thing sit close together. An embedding model
is the surveyor who assigns the positions. Two surveyors use incompatible maps. A position from one means nothing on the other.
That is why **changing models means re-embedding every document you have**, which is the main cost of a wrong choice.

### A language specialist versus a multilingual reference

A Chinese-only model is a local who knows one city well, and a multilingual model is a seasoned traveler. Sometimes the local
knows the back streets better, and sometimes the traveler has simply seen more. Which one wins is something you can measure on
your own text, and the answer has changed over time.

### Keyword search is a checker; an embedding is a reader

Keyword search checks whether the words in the query appear in the document. It is exact and easy to explain, and it never
confuses one product number with another. An embedding model reads for meaning and can miss exact strings. Many production
systems run both and merge the results.

---

## When You Need This

**You probably need it when** people describe things in their own words, your text has many ways to say the same thing, or
questions and answers may be in different languages.

**You may not need it when** users search for exact identifiers, names or codes, or your collection is small enough to browse. Try
keyword search first, and score it on your own queries beside any model you are considering.

---

## Trade-offs

### Multilingual or single-language

A single-language model promises better results in its language, while a multilingual model handles every language in one index
and can match across languages. Adding a language to a single-language system later means a second model and index, and
cross-language search does not work between them.

On a November 2026 test that tried 23 open models (22 produced results; one ran out of memory), that promise held only in part. The best Japanese and Korean specialists tied the best
multilingual model rather than beating it, and no Chinese-only model came near it on Chinese. A Chinese-only model given Korean
text did not raise an error; it returned results close to random (S2, `retrieval-quality.md`).

### Large or small

Larger models need more memory and take longer per passage. Inside one model family, the larger sizes retrieved better on the
2026 test; across families, newer and larger models did not beat a 2024 model, and several cost more to run. A small model gave
up a few points of quality for about eleven times the speed. The limit on an ordinary server is whether the model fits in memory
at all: one 4-billion-parameter model did not fit in 7 GiB (S2, `memory-and-throughput.md`). Model files are often stored in a
compact 16-bit form, but CPU inference usually loads them at 32 bits, so a 1.2 GB file becomes about 2.4 GB in memory.

### Run it yourself or pay per token

Hosted APIs need no machine, and their prices are low at small volume. Running a model yourself keeps text inside your network and
gives you a fixed version that will not change underneath you, but you take on the machine and the upkeep.

### License

Some popular models are licensed for research or non-commercial use only. The license tag on a model hub can be missing, so the
model's own page is what counts. Check this first, because it can remove a model from consideration regardless of its scores.

### Popular or proven

Download counts measure habit, not fit. One of the two most downloaded models in this field scored lower than plain keyword
search on Japanese and Korean, and a model that matches translations almost perfectly scored lower than keyword search on finding
answers in all three languages. Those comparisons are of average scores; on a test this size some of the gaps are within the
margin of error. Skill at one job does not carry over to the other (S2,
`retrieval-quality.md`, `cross-language.md`).

---

## What To Check Before Choosing

1. **Your own text.** Public benchmarks are mostly Wikipedia and web text. Build a test set of real queries from your domain, with
   the passages that should come back, and score candidates on it.
2. **The model's prompt.** Some models need specific text added to queries and documents, such as `query: `. Leaving it out
   does not cause an error. It lowers quality without any message.
3. **Truncation.** Models read a limited number of tokens. A passage past the limit is cut off silently, so chunk long documents.
4. **Your exact strings.** Test product codes, names and numbers. If they matter, run keyword search beside the embedding search.
5. **Memory on your actual machine,** with the batch size you will use.

---

## How This Fits With Other Pieces

An embedding model produces vectors. They need a place to live (see 1.203 Vector Databases), and the wider application around
them is usually a retrieval pipeline (1.204 Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) Pipelines, 1.206 Chunking Patterns). A reranker often follows the embedding step
and reorders its top results; rerankers are outside this survey.

# S1 Approach: The CJK Embedding Field, November 2026

An embedding model turns a passage into a vector so that passages about the same thing land near each other. This survey
covers models a developer would use to search Chinese, Japanese or Korean text, alone or across languages.

The field sorts by what the model was built to do, and that matters more than any brand:

1. **Multilingual retrieval models** trained on queries and passages across many languages. The 2023 to 2026 generations
   are in `multilingual-retrieval-models.md`.
2. **Single-language specialists** for Chinese, Japanese or Korean, in `language-specialists.md`.
3. **Paraphrase and translation-pair models** from before the retrieval-trained generations (LaBSE and the
   sentence-transformers paraphrase family), also in `multilingual-retrieval-models.md`.
4. **Hosted APIs**, in `hosted-apis.md`.
5. **Serving layers**, which are not models but decide what can be run, in `serving-layers.md`.

## Sample and boundaries

Inclusion took one of three routes: long use for CJK text (M3E, text2vec, LaBSE, multilingual-e5), a top rank claimed on a public benchmark, or heavy use
measured by 30-day Hub downloads. Every count, license tag, date and parameter figure in this pass comes from the Hugging Face
Hub API on 2026-11-03 (`harness/1-211-cjk-embedding-models/results/hub_facts.json`).

Out of scope: rerankers, sparse-only retrieval, image embeddings, and vector storage (see 1.203 Vector Databases). Keyword
search appears only as the baseline a model must beat.

## Three facts that reshape the field

M3E, long a common pick for Chinese, is non-commercial by its own card. Dedicated Japanese and Korean retrieval models exist
and are widely used. And a retrieval generation built on small language-model backbones arrived in 2025 and 2026, with sizes
from 80M to 27B parameters (S1 tables), so "base model or large model" no longer describes the size question.

## What moves the choice to hosted

- No machine with several gigabytes of free memory, or no one to run it.
- Low volume: at these prices a million passages of 150 tokens is 150 million tokens.
- Text may leave your network. If it may not, this group is out regardless of quality.

A switch in either direction means re-embedding the whole corpus, because vectors from two models cannot be compared.

## How the generations differ

- **XLM-R and BERT encoders (2020 to 2024): e5, BGE-M3, Arctic, Granite, LaBSE, MiniLM.** Mean or CLS pooling over an encoder, a
  shared multilingual vocabulary. Most store 32-bit weights, so disk size is close to memory size.
- **Small LLM backbones (2025 on): Qwen3, Harrier, F2LLM-v2, Nemotron.** A language model with last-token or mean pooling.
  Queries carry an instruction prompt, as "Instruct: ... Query: ...", and the query and document sides are treated
  differently. Weights ship in 16-bit formats, so a 1.2 GB file becomes about 2.4 GB of parameters when loaded as 32-bit
  floats, the usual form for CPU inference.
- **Paraphrase and translation-pair models.** LaBSE was trained on translation pairs and the MiniLM model on paraphrases;
  neither was trained on query-to-passage retrieval.

Each of these is a vendor's own statement. S2 tests the runnable subset on CJK text, and its results do not follow the
leaderboard order: on Chinese, Japanese and Korean retrieval the 2024 BGE-M3 is the only model at or tied with the top in all
three, while the newer models tie on at most one (Harrier 0.6b on Chinese, Nemotron 3.5 1B leading Korean)
(`../S2-comprehensive/retrieval-quality.md`).

- Harrier's card lists Multilingual MTEB v2 scores of 66.5 (270m), 69.0 (0.6b) and 74.3 (27b) (vendor: Microsoft model card, read 2026-11-03).
- Qwen3's card says the 8B model ranked first on the MTEB multilingual board as of June 5, 2025 (vendor: Qwen model card, read 2026-11-03).
- F2LLM-v2's card describes eight sizes from 80M to 14B, trained on public data that is also released (vendor: Codefuse model card, read 2026-11-03).
- Nemotron 3.5 Embed's card lists Chinese, Japanese and Korean among its languages and is released under OpenMDW-1.1 (vendor: NVIDIA model card, read 2026-11-03).

A license tag on the Hub is not always the whole answer:

- `jinaai/jina-embeddings-v3` and `v5-text-small` are tagged CC BY-NC. `jina-embeddings-v4`'s card states the Qwen Research

- `TencentBAC/Conan-embedding-v1` (Chinese) is tagged CC BY-NC.

# S1 Recommendation: Who Goes Into the Benchmark

The first pass settles the shape of the field and the shortlist. Which model retrieves best is an S2 question.

## What S1 settled

- **The license sorts before the score does.** M3E, Jina v3 and v5, Conan and Sarashina are non-commercial by their own
  terms, and only some of them show it in a Hub tag. A product team reading the Hub filter alone gets this wrong for M3E and
  Sarashina.
- **Japanese and Korean are not a gap.** Ruri v3, PLaMo, Sarashina, KURE, KoE5 and BGE-m3-ko all exist, most are Apache or MIT,
  and three (Ruri v3 with 867,661, PLaMo with 541,262 and KURE-v1 with 414,607) have more than 400,000 downloads in 30 days
  (source: Hub API, 2026-11-03).
- **"Base model" no longer describes the size question.** Sizes now run from 80M (F2LLM-v2) to 27B (Harrier) parameters, and the
  practical limit on a CPU box is whether the 32-bit parameters fit in memory.

Open weights, downloadable without an account or a terms gate, and runnable without executing code from a model repository:

## What to know

sentence-transformers reads a model's own `modules.json`, so pooling and normalization come from the model, and it reads
named prompts from `config_sentence_transformers.json` where a model ships them. That is the mechanism behind one real trap:
**a model that needs a prefix and does not ship one in its config gets no prefix unless the caller adds it.** e5 takes
`query: ` and `passage: ` by card only, and ruri v3 takes `検索クエリ: ` and `検索文書: ` by card only.
Dropping them silently changes the scores.

sentence-transformers is the common path, not the only one: several current models document plain Transformers use, and
server deployments reach for one of the two servers above.

# S2 Approach: What Was Run, What Was Read, What Was Not Done

S1 sorted the field and named a panel of 23 open models. This pass runs them on the same data under the same settings and
reports retrieval quality, cross-language matching, memory and encode throughput. Twenty-two finished; Qwen3-Embedding-4B ran
out of memory at the 7 GiB cap, which is a result in its own right (`memory-and-throughput.md`).

## The test

- **Retrieval.** 150 sampled dev queries per language from MIRACL (Chinese, Japanese, Korean), with every passage MIRACL's
  assessors judged for those queries, topped up with uniform random Wikipedia passages in the same language to 2,000 passages
  (2,334 for Korean, which has more judged passages). Scored by nDCG@10 with binary gain, Recall@10 and MRR@10 (measured
  2026-11-05; `harness/1-211-cjk-embedding-models/results/tasks.json`, `score.py`). The judged negatives come from MIRACL's
  own assessment pool, so no candidate model chose them.
- **Cross-language matching.** Flores-200 devtest: 1,012 aligned sentences in English, Simplified Chinese, Traditional
  Chinese, Japanese and Korean. A sentence retrieves its translation among all 1,012 candidates, in both directions. Scored by
  hit@1.
- **Keyword baseline.** BM25 (k1 0.9, b 0.4) over character bigrams, scored by the same function as the models.
- **One set of settings.** 256 tokens, batch 8, 32-bit floats, all four threads, CPU, unit vectors, title plus passage as the
  text. The only per-model inputs are the pinned commit and the prompts the model's own card or configuration prescribes
  (`tokenizer-behavior.md`).
- **Ties.** A model is reported as tied with the leader when the 95% bootstrap interval of its paired difference from the leader
  spans zero, on 2,000 query resamples shared by every model. Ties are tested against each task's leader only: two models
  that are not the leader are never compared with each other, and no model is tested against the BM25 baseline. Where the
  text compares those, it compares point estimates and says so.

Inputs are pinned by commit and SHA-256 and rebuilt on each run; the build stops if a task differs from the committed hash.

## The machine and the rules for memory and timing

Every model ran on a DigitalOcean c-4 droplet: 4 dedicated vCPUs at 2.70 GHz, 7.76 GiB of memory, no swap, one model at a
time, Python 3.11.18, PyTorch 2.15.0 CPU, Transformers 5.22.1, sentence-transformers 6.2.0 (measured 2026-11-05;
`results/runs/`, `results/launches/`). The panel took four launches. Three drew an Intel Xeon Platinum 8168; the fourth,
which ran Arctic L v2 and F2LLM-v2 0.6B, drew a Xeon Platinum 8280 (`tables.py machine`). Throughput for those two is on a
different CPU model of the same droplet type, and the throughput and score-against-cost tables carry a CPU column.

- **Memory.** Each model ran in its own 7 GiB cgroup with no swap, after its weights were downloaded outside the cap and the
  page cache was dropped. Peak resident memory and the cgroup's peak are both recorded. The cgroup peak also counts
  reclaimable page cache, so the resident peak is the closer guide to the memory a machine needs. A self-test at the start of
  each launch checks that the cap kills and that the kill is read as an out-of-memory at the cap; `results/cap_selftest.json`
  keeps only the most recent record, from launch 4.
- **Throughput.** The wall clock of the encode call over each MIRACL task's full passage set, with tokens counted after
  truncation. A model's single number is total tokens over total seconds across the sets it ran. Thread count, load average,
  CPU time and CPU steal are recorded beside it. Queries and Flores are not timed.

## Read, not run

Vendor benchmark claims (quoted with their source in S1), hosted APIs, BGE-M3's sparse and multi-vector modes, ONNX and
OpenVINO exports and their speed, vector truncation, and the models S1 left at read level: EmbeddingGemma, gte-multilingual,
nomic v2, Jina, PLaMo, Sarashina.

## Not done

No GPU. No phone or on-device run. No fine-tuning. No domain corpus (legal, medical, product). No mixed Chinese-English passages
as a separate test. No Traditional Chinese retrieval. No long documents, since passages are capped at 256 tokens. No query
latency at batch 1. No hosted API quality test.

## What the numbers can and cannot carry

A corpus of about 2,000 passages is far easier than full MIRACL, whose Chinese, Japanese and Korean corpora hold 4.9, 7.0 and
1.5 million passages (measured 2026-11-05; `results/tasks.json`). The scores rank models against each other here; they do not
compare with published MIRACL or MTEB numbers. Flores sentences are short and the candidate pool is small, so good models crowd
against the ceiling. Query samples of 150 leave 95% intervals of roughly 0.03 to 0.06 either side of each score (measured
2026-11-05; `results/scores.json`), which is why ties are computed rather than read off the table.

## What the table shows

- **The test nearly saturates for multilingual models.** Every multilingual model reaches at least 0.920 on its weakest pair, and thirteen of
  the fourteen clear 0.95 (measured 2026-11-05; `results/scores.json`). At that height the test separates only the weak; it does
  not rank the strong.
- **LaBSE and BGE-M3 sit at the ceiling** on every pair, with weakest pairs of 0.999 and 0.998 (same file). LaBSE was trained on
  translation pairs, and this is the job where it shines. It is the same model whose retrieval point estimates sit below the keyword
  baseline in all three languages, with overlapping intervals (`retrieval-quality.md`), so skill at translation matching does not predict skill at retrieval.
- **Pairs through Korean are the hard ones.** For most models the weakest pair is Chinese and Korean or Japanese and Korean
  (same file).
- **Chinese specialists fall away once the text is not Chinese.** `bge-large-zh` holds 0.955 between English and Simplified

  (`tables.py flores`). Simplified to Traditional stays at 0.987 or above for all of them, so a specialist can cover both Chinese
  scripts on sentence matching.

## What this test does not settle

Translation pairs are the easiest cross-language case. A sentence and its translation say exactly the same thing, in a pool of
about 1,000 candidates. A question in Korean whose answer sits in a Japanese document is a harder job, and it was not run. The
test also covers only English, Simplified and Traditional Chinese, Japanese and Korean. Treat a high score here as necessary and
not as sufficient, and test real cross-language queries before relying on them.

# S2 Recommendation: Findings and a Ranking by Job

All numbers below are nDCG@10 on the 150-query MIRACL samples, Flores hit@1, peak resident memory and tokens per second, measured
2026-11-05 on DigitalOcean c-4 droplets at 256 tokens, batch 8, 32-bit floats (`harness/1-211-cjk-embedding-models/results/`).

## Findings

1. **BGE-M3 is the one model at or tied with the top in Chinese, Japanese and Korean** (0.827, 0.867, 0.819): it leads
   Chinese and is tied with the leaders in the other two. Ties here are against each task's leader only. It sits at the ceiling on translation matching at 0.998 on its weakest pair, and it needed
   2.92 GiB resident and encoded 333 tokens per second on 4 vCPUs (measured 2026-11-05; `retrieval-quality.md`,
   `memory-and-throughput.md`).
2. **Newer and larger does not mean better at this job.** Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (2025) is below BGE-M3 on point estimate in
   all three languages while slower (197 tokens per second) and heavier (3.75 GiB). Arctic L v2 and F2LLM-v2 0.6B land
   mid-pack, tied with no leader. On Chinese, Harrier 0.6b, Qwen3 0.6B and F2LLM-v2 0.6B (0.800, 0.797, 0.794) form one group
   that this test cannot order; only Harrier's interval against the leader spans zero. Qwen3 4B did not load inside 7 GiB at
   32 bits (same files).
3. **Popular does not mean good at retrieval.** Paraphrase MiniLM, second only to BGE-M3 in 30-day downloads, has point
   estimates below the BM25 character-bigram baseline on Japanese (0.516 against 0.635, intervals apart) and Korean (0.477
   against 0.572, intervals overlapping, so unresolved). LaBSE's point estimates are below it in all three languages, with
   overlapping intervals, yet it sits at the ceiling on translation matching, so translation matching does not predict
   retrieval (same files; `retrieval-quality.md` "Against the keyword baseline", `cross-language.md`).
4. **Chinese-only models trail on Chinese and break outside it.** M3E (0.721), BGE large zh (0.705), BGE small zh (0.688) and
   text2vec (0.638) are all below BGE-M3's 0.827. Given Korean, M3E scores 0.044, and the Chinese specialists' translation
   matching with Korean is below 0.12 hit@1 as a pair mean (single directions 0.024 to 0.147) (same files).
5. **Specialists tie the multilingual leader rather than beat it.** Ruri v3 and e5-large are co-leaders on Japanese; on
   Korean, KURE-v1 and BGE-M3 are tied with the leader, Nemotron 3.5 1B (same files).
6. **Small models are a real trade, not a collapse.** `e5-small` gives up 0.050 to 0.082 to BGE-M3 across the three languages at
   about eleven times its token rate and under half its memory (same files).
7. **Translation matching nearly saturates.** Every multilingual model reaches 0.920 or more on its weakest Flores pair, so the
   test separates only the weak (same files).

## Ranking by job

Rank is an order for starting a project, set by the evidence above; check on your own text before committing.

### Multilingual retrieval on CPU, licensed for commercial use

1. BGE-M3 (MIT): at or tied with the top in all three languages (leads Chinese; tied with the Japanese and Korean leaders).
2. e5-large (MIT): Japanese co-leader with Ruri v3; 0.792 and 0.795 on Chinese and Korean, at about the same memory and speed as BGE-M3.
   Arctic L v2 (Apache-2.0) is in the same cost class and scores below e5-large on Japanese and level with it on Korean.
3. e5-base (MIT): 0.771, 0.853, 0.776 at 1.87 GiB and about three and a half times BGE-M3's token rate.
4. e5-small (MIT): 0.745, 0.817, 0.768 at 1.35 GiB and about eleven times the rate, when throughput or memory rules.

### Chinese only

1. BGE-M3. Behind it, Harrier 0.6b (MIT), Qwen3 0.6B and F2LLM-v2 0.6B are one group at 0.794 to 0.800 that this test cannot
   order, all at a lower token rate (194 to 198 against 333) and more memory.
2. e5-base or e5-small where memory or speed matters.
3. `bge-small-zh` (MIT) only when every megabyte counts: 0.68 GiB and 6,935 tokens per second, at 0.688 (0.648 to 0.725),
   above the keyword baseline's 0.562 (0.515 to 0.609) with no overlap.

M3E is out for commercial use by its own card, and below e5-small on point estimate.

### Japanese only

1. Ruri v3 310m (Apache-2.0) or e5-large, co-leaders at 0.879. Ruri uses less memory (1.95 against 2.86 GiB) and encodes a little
   faster (386 against 308 tokens per second).
2. BGE-M3 when other languages may follow.

### Korean only

1. BGE-M3 or KURE-v1, each tied with the leader at 0.819 and 0.816, with similar memory and token rate. BGE-M3 keeps the door open to other languages.
2. e5-large, Arctic L v2 or Harrier 0.6b (0.795, 0.795, 0.794), outside the tie with the leader. `e5-small` or `e5-small-ko` (0.768, 0.775) where
   speed rules.

Nemotron 3.5 1B leads on point estimate at 0.831, with BGE-M3 tied, and costs about three times the encode time per token and
more than twice the memory, at 6.96 GiB on a 7 GiB cap. Its rate was measured under reclaim pressure at that cap, so it may be
slower here than on a larger machine. Check its OpenMDW-1.1 terms first.

### Matching across languages

1. BGE-M3: the same model as the default, at the ceiling.
2. Harrier 0.6b, Arctic L v2, Harrier 270m, F2LLM-v2 0.6B, Qwen3 0.6B: 0.984 or above on the weakest pair.

LaBSE matches translations at the ceiling and should not also serve as the search model.

## What S2 did not settle

Hosted APIs (never run), query latency, ONNX and OpenVINO speedups, GPU behavior, fine-tuning gain, Traditional Chinese
retrieval, long documents, mixed-language passages and any domain corpus.

# S3 Approach: Five Situations and What Narrows the Choice in Each

S1 and S2 judged each model on its merits for any reader. S3 walks through five kinds of project that need CJK search and asks
which requirements remove which models. The situations are generic; each exists in many forms, and none of them shaped the
S1 or S2 verdicts.

| Situation | Core constraint | File |
|---|---|---|
| A catalog or article search in one language (Chinese, Japanese or Korean) | Best results in that one language; exact codes still matter | `use-case-single-language-catalog-search.md` |
| A help center answering questions in several CJK languages and English | One index for every language; commercial license | `use-case-multilingual-help-center.md` |
| A research or archive tool matching documents across languages | A query in one language finds documents in another | `use-case-cross-language-discovery.md` |
| A self-hosted internal knowledge base on an ordinary CPU server | Text stays inside; fixed memory; re-embedding is costly | `use-case-self-hosted-knowledge-base.md` |
| A feature on a small machine or a tight memory budget | The smallest footprint that still beats keyword search | `use-case-small-footprint.md` |

## How each file is built

- **Who needs this**: the situation in a paragraph.
- **Requirements that follow**: the constraints that remove options.
- **Walk-through**: the decision in order, with the S2 evidence for each step and its provenance.
- **What to avoid**: the models or habits that fail a requirement.
- **Check before committing**: what the survey's evidence does not cover for this situation, and what to test on your own text.

## Evidence base

All evidence comes from S1 and S2: a 150-query MIRACL sample per language over a pool of about 2,000 Wikipedia passages,
Flores-200 translation matching, and memory and throughput on a 4-vCPU DigitalOcean c-4 at 256 tokens, batch 8, 32-bit floats
(measured 2026-11-05; `harness/1-211-cjk-embedding-models/results/`). S3 adds no measurement. Wikipedia is not your catalog,
your tickets or your wiki, which is why every walk-through ends with a test on your own text.

# S3 Recommendation: Which Model for Which Situation

| Situation | Start with | Consider | Leave out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Search in one CJK language | BGE-M3, beside keyword search | Ruri v3 or e5-large for Japanese; KURE-v1 for Korean | M3E (non-commercial); a Chinese-only model for an edge the test did not show |
| Help center in several CJK languages and English | BGE-M3 | e5-base or e5-small for a large corpus | Chinese-only models; LaBSE and paraphrase MiniLM as the search model |
| Finding documents across languages | BGE-M3 | Harrier 0.6b, Arctic L v2 | LaBSE as the search model; Chinese-only models |
| Self-hosted knowledge base on a CPU server | BGE-M3 if memory and time allow | e5-base, then e5-small, as the step down | Qwen3 4B on a 7 GiB machine; hosted APIs if text may not leave |
| Small machine or tight memory budget | e5-small | BGE small zh for Chinese only | Paraphrase MiniLM; F2LLM-v2 80M |

Every entry rests on S2's measurements (measured 2026-11-05; `harness/1-211-cjk-embedding-models/results/`) and ends in the same
step: check before committing, on your own text.

## Patterns that repeat across situations

1. **One multilingual model kept coming out on top.** BGE-M3 is the start in four of five situations because it was at or
   tied with the leader in all three languages and at the ceiling on translation matching (`S2-comprehensive/retrieval-quality.md`,
   `cross-language.md`).
2. **The license check comes before the score.** M3E, Sarashina, Conan and Jina carry non-commercial terms, and not all of them
   show it in a Hub tag (S1).
3. **Keyword search stays in the design.** Every retrieval-trained model had a higher point estimate than the character-bigram
   BM25 baseline in its own languages, some with overlapping intervals and M3E on Korean the exception, but keyword search
   finds exact codes and names that an embedding can miss.
4. **Memory sets the ceiling on a CPU box.** The step down from BGE-M3 to e5-base and e5-small is a measured trade of score for
   speed and memory; the step up to Qwen3 4B did not load in 7 GiB (`memory-and-throughput.md`).
5. **A switch costs a full re-embed.** Vectors from two models are not comparable, so the first choice should be tested on the
   real text before the index is built.

## What S3 did not settle

Any domain corpus (catalogs, tickets, internal documents), mixed-language passages, long documents, Traditional Chinese
retrieval, on-device runtimes, query latency, and hosted API quality.

# Use Case: Finding Documents Across Languages

## Who needs this

A research database, patent search, news archive or library catalog holding documents in English, Chinese (both scripts),
Japanese and Korean. A reader searches in one language and wants the relevant documents whatever their language. Volume is
modest; quality matters more than speed.

## Requirements that follow

1. Vectors from different languages must land near each other when the meaning matches.
2. Same-language retrieval must still be good, since most matches are in the reader's own language.
3. Simplified and Traditional Chinese should meet.

## Walk-through

1. **Rule out the Chinese-only models.** Their translation matching with Korean fell below 0.12 hit@1 as a pair mean (0.024
   to 0.147 in single directions), and two of the four
   fell below 0.6 even between English and Simplified Chinese (measured 2026-11-05; `S2-comprehensive/cross-language.md`).
2. **Rule out by retrieval, not by translation matching.** LaBSE topped the translation test at 0.999 on its weakest pair, and
   its retrieval point estimates were below the keyword baseline in all three languages (0.556, 0.588, 0.541 nDCG@10, intervals
   overlapping; same date, `retrieval-quality.md`). Translation matching nearly saturates for multilingual models, so it cannot pick between them.
3. **Pick on same-language retrieval among the survivors.** BGE-M3 was at or tied with the leader in all three languages and at
   0.998 on its weakest translation pair (same files). Harrier 0.6b and Arctic L v2 came next on translation matching at 0.990,
   with lower retrieval scores.
4. **Script variants.** Simplified-to-Traditional sentence matching was at 0.987 or above for every model that ran it (same
   file). That is alignment of the same sentence, not search over a Traditional corpus.

## What to avoid

- Choosing LaBSE because it is "the cross-lingual model." It is the translation-matching model; retrieval is another job.
- Translating every document into one language before embedding when a multilingual model already places them together; check
  it first on your own pairs.

## Check before committing

Flores pairs say the same thing in a pool of 1,012 sentences, the easiest cross-language case. A short query in one language
against a long document in another was not run, and neither was Traditional Chinese retrieval. Build a set of queries whose
right answers are in another language and score candidates on it before relying on cross-language results.

# Use Case: A Help Center in Several CJK Languages and English

## Who needs this

A product sold in China, Japan and Korea, with help articles in each language and in English. Customers ask questions in their
own language, and the article that answers may exist only in English or in another CJK language. Answers feed a support
search box, and often a chatbot that retrieves passages before writing a reply.

## Requirements that follow

1. One model and one index across Chinese, Japanese, Korean and English, so a new language does not mean a new system.
2. Strong same-language retrieval in each of the three CJK languages.
3. Sound matching across languages for articles that exist in only one.
4. A license that allows commercial use.

## Walk-through

1. **Only multilingual models qualify.** A Chinese-only model given Korean text failed outright: M3E scored 0.044 nDCG@10 on
   Korean, and every Chinese specialist's translation matching with Korean fell below 0.12 hit@1 as a pair mean, 0.024 to 0.147 in single
   directions (measured 2026-11-05;
   `S2-comprehensive/retrieval-quality.md`, `cross-language.md`).
2. **Among multilingual models, one is at or tied with the top in all three languages.** BGE-M3 (MIT) scored 0.827, 0.867
   and 0.819, leading Chinese and tied with the leader on Japanese (e5-large) and on Korean (Nemotron 3.5 1B). e5-large co-led Japanese and fell to 0.792 and
   0.795 elsewhere; no other model that ran all three tied with a leader in more than one (same file).
3. **Cross-language matching does not separate the good candidates.** BGE-M3's weakest Flores pair was 0.998, and every
   multilingual model reached at least 0.920 (same date; `cross-language.md`). Use it to rule models out, not to rank them.
4. **Budget for the cost.** BGE-M3 needed 2.92 GiB resident and encoded 333 tokens per second on 4 vCPUs, about 114 hours per
   million passages at 256 tokens (measured 2026-11-05; `S2-comprehensive/memory-and-throughput.md`). Tens of thousands of
   passages are hours, not days. If the corpus is large, e5-base (0.771, 0.853, 0.776 at 1,151 tokens per second)
   is the next step down (same files).

## What to avoid

- A newer model picked on a leaderboard position: Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B was below BGE-M3 on point estimate in all three
  languages and slower
  (measured 2026-11-05; same files).
- LaBSE as the search model. It matched translations at the ceiling, and its retrieval point estimates were below the keyword
  baseline in all three languages, with overlapping intervals.
- Mixing vectors from two models in one index; they are not comparable.

## Check before committing

Help-center text mixes product names, English terms and CJK prose in one passage, and mixed-language passages were not run as
their own test. A question in Korean answered by a Japanese article is harder than translation matching, and it was not run
either. Test a set of real customer questions in each language, including some whose only answer is in another language.

# Use Case: A Self-Hosted Knowledge Base on an Ordinary CPU Server

## Who needs this

An organization whose internal documents (engineering notes, policies, meeting records) are in Chinese, Japanese or Korean mixed
with English, and which may not send them to a hosted API. The model runs on an existing CPU server with a few gigabytes free.
The corpus is hundreds of thousands of passages and grows daily.

## Requirements that follow

1. The model runs locally; text never leaves the network, which removes hosted APIs regardless of quality (S1,
   `hosted-apis.md`).
2. It fits the memory the server has, with room for the rest of the stack.
3. The first embedding of the whole corpus finishes in an acceptable time, and new documents keep up.
4. The model version stays fixed, since a change means re-embedding everything.

## Walk-through

1. **Check memory before score.** Peak resident memory at batch 8 ran from 0.68 GiB to 6.96 GiB across the panel; BGE-M3 needed
   2.92, e5-base 1.87 and e5-small 1.35. Qwen3-Embedding-4B did not load inside a 7 GiB cap at 32-bit floats, and Nemotron 3.5 1B
   finished at 6.96 GiB on that cap, under reclaim pressure (measured 2026-11-05; c-4 droplet; `S2-comprehensive/memory-and-throughput.md`).
2. **Price the first embedding.** On 4 vCPUs BGE-M3 ran at about 114 hours per million passages, e5-base about 33 and e5-small
   about 10 (same file). Five hundred thousand passages is roughly two and a half days of one such machine with BGE-M3, or
   under a day with e5-base.
3. **Choose the quality step that fits.** BGE-M3 is at or tied with the leader in all three languages (0.827, 0.867, 0.819 nDCG@10); e5-base
   gives up 0.014 to 0.056 for about three and a half times the token rate; e5-small gives up 0.050 to 0.082 for about eleven
   times (measured 2026-11-05; `retrieval-quality.md`, `memory-and-throughput.md`).
4. **Pin the commit.** Every panel model ran from a pinned Hub commit; the same practice protects an index from a silent model
   update (S2, `approach.md`).

## What to avoid

- The cgroup or container memory peak as a sizing number: it counts page cache from reading the weights. BGE-M3 showed 5.56 GiB
  there against 2.92 resident (same file).
- Models that need code run from the model repository, which the commit may not pin in full (S1, `recommendation.md`).
- A model whose license changes the terms for internal use: check Nemotron's OpenMDW-1.1 and any non-commercial tag first.

## Check before committing

Internal documents are long and mixed-language; this test capped passages at 256 tokens and did not run mixed Chinese-English
passages. ONNX or OpenVINO exports may change the speed picture and were not run. Time your own corpus on your own server at
the batch size you will use, and score a set of real internal questions.

# Use Case: Search in One Language, Chinese, Japanese or Korean

## Who needs this

A team whose catalog, articles or help pages are in one CJK language, and whose users describe what they want in their own
words: "cheap Bluetooth headphones" against a page that says "affordable wireless earbuds." Some queries are exact (a model
number, a brand), most are not. Nobody expects a second language soon, but nobody can promise it either.

## Requirements that follow

1. The best retrieval available in that one language.
2. A license that allows commercial use.
3. Exact strings (codes, part numbers, names) must still be found.
4. A switch of model later means re-embedding everything, so the first choice should hold.

## Walk-through

1. **Rule out by license first.** M3E's own card says the model is for research and non-commercial use, and the Hub shows no
   license tag that would catch it in a filter (S1, `language-specialists.md`). Sarashina and Conan carry non-commercial terms
   too (same file).
2. **Chinese: start from BGE-M3, not a Chinese-only model.** On the Chinese sample BGE-M3 led at 0.827 nDCG@10. Behind it,
   Harrier 0.6b, Qwen3 0.6B and F2LLM-v2 0.6B (0.800, 0.797, 0.794) form a group this test cannot order. The best Chinese-only model that ran, M3E, scored 0.721, and BGE large zh 0.705 (measured 2026-11-05;
   `S2-comprehensive/retrieval-quality.md`). The old trade, narrower model for a better Chinese score, did not appear.
3. **Japanese: a specialist is a fair choice.** Ruri v3 310m and e5-large are co-leaders at 0.879, with BGE-M3 tied with them
   at 0.867. Ruri used
   1.95 GiB resident against e5-large's 2.86 (measured 2026-11-05; `S2-comprehensive/memory-and-throughput.md`).
4. **Korean: the specialist ties, and so does the generalist.** KURE-v1 scored 0.816 and BGE-M3 0.819, both tied with Nemotron
   3.5 1B at 0.831; KURE and BGE-M3 cost about the same to run (same files). BGE-M3 keeps other languages open at no score cost
   on this test.
5. **Keep keyword search beside it.** BM25 over character bigrams scored 0.562, 0.635 and 0.572 on the three languages (same
   file). Every retrieval-trained model in its own languages had a higher point estimate, a few with overlapping intervals
   (`retrieval-quality.md`, "Against the keyword baseline"), and keyword search is what finds an exact part number. A
   hybrid of the two is the common design.
6. **Mind the prompt.** e5 needs `query: ` and `passage: `, Ruri its own Japanese prefixes, and the BGE v1.5 Chinese models an
   instruction on the query side. Leaving a prompt out raises no error (`S2-comprehensive/tokenizer-behavior.md`).

## What to avoid

- A Chinese-only model chosen for an expected Chinese edge that this test did not show.
- Paraphrase MiniLM as the search model: point estimates below the keyword baseline on Japanese and Korean (0.516, 0.477;
  the Korean intervals overlap) despite being the second most downloaded model in the field (measured 2026-11-05; `S2-comprehensive/retrieval-quality.md`).
- Dropping keyword search entirely when users type codes.

## Check before committing

The sample is Wikipedia with questions written by MIRACL's annotators. Product titles, short listings and colloquial queries are
a different shape of text, and no catalog corpus was run. Build a few hundred of your own queries with the passages that should
come back, score BGE-M3, the language's specialist and BM25 on them, and include your exact-string queries in the set.

# Use Case: A Small Machine or a Tight Memory Budget

## Who needs this

A feature that must run where memory is short: a small virtual machine, an edge box, a desktop app, or a service that shares
its host with much else. The text is CJK, the collection is modest, and the aim is "clearly better than keyword search" rather
than "the best there is."

## Requirements that follow

1. Small weights and low resident memory.
2. Fast enough encoding on a few CPU cores.
3. Retrieval that still clears the keyword baseline by a margin.

## Walk-through

1. **Know the floor you are trying to beat.** BM25 over character bigrams scored 0.562, 0.635 and 0.572 nDCG@10 on Chinese,
   Japanese and Korean (measured 2026-11-05; `S2-comprehensive/retrieval-quality.md`).
2. **Several languages: e5-small.** It scored 0.745, 0.817 and 0.768, each above the baseline with no interval overlap, at 1.35 GiB resident and 3,686 tokens per second, with 471
   MB of weights (measured 2026-11-05; c-4 droplet, batch 8; `memory-and-throughput.md`; S1 for size). For Korean alone,
   `e5-small-ko` (0.775) was close to it; the two were not tested against each other.
3. **Chinese only, smallest of all: BGE small zh.** 96 MB of weights, 0.68 GiB resident, 6,935 tokens per second, and 0.688 on
   Chinese, above the baseline with no interval overlap and below e5-small on point estimate (same files). It does not handle other languages: its translation matching
   with Korean was under 0.09 (same date; `cross-language.md`).
4. **Smaller than that loses ground.** F2LLM-v2 80M scored 0.703, 0.723 and 0.663 at 1.16 GiB, below e5-small on point estimate
   everywhere and slower (1,906 tokens per second); on Japanese and Korean its interval overlaps the keyword baseline's (same
   files).

## What to avoid

- Paraphrase MiniLM, the same footprint as e5-small (1.30 GiB, 3,754 tokens per second) and with point estimates below the
  keyword baseline on Japanese and Korean (same files).
- Assuming a small model is the same model at lower quality everywhere; check each language you need.

## Check before committing

No phone, browser or GPU run was made, and no quantized or ONNX export was measured; every number here is PyTorch on a 4-vCPU
server at 32-bit floats. Measure memory and speed on the target device in its own runtime, and score your own queries, before
committing to a small model.

# S4 Approach: What Lasts in an Embedding Choice

S4 asks which choices will still be sound in a few years and what it costs to move when one stops being sound. An embedding
model is unusual among software dependencies: a model that never changes is a feature, because every stored vector depends on
it, and the cost of leaving is a full re-embed of the corpus.

| Signal | Source | Where recorded |
|---|---|---|
| Repository created and last changed, 30-day downloads | Hugging Face Hub API, 2026-11-03 | `model-maintenance-and-licensing.md` |
| License tag, and the license the card states | Hub API and model cards, 2026-11-03 | S1, `model-maintenance-and-licensing.md` |
| Generation against measured result | S2 runs, 2026-11-05 | `S2-comprehensive/retrieval-quality.md` |
| Cost of a re-embed | S2 throughput, 2026-11-05 | `S2-comprehensive/memory-and-throughput.md` |
| Serving layer health | PyPI and GitHub, 2026-11-03 | S1, `serving-layers.md` |

## Three questions per choice

1. **What is the exit?** Leaving a model means re-embedding every passage with the new one. S2's hours per million passages
   price that move.
2. **Can the version be held still?** A pinned commit on the Hub keeps the same weights; a model that needs code from its
   repository may not be fully pinned by that commit (S1).
3. **Do the license terms stay usable?** A non-commercial clause, a custom license or a terms gate can rule a model out
   regardless of quality, and the Hub tag does not always show it.

## Method limits

Viability here is a reading of dated records, not a forecast. Hub dates are one observation on 2026-11-03; a repository
unchanged for two years can be finished rather than abandoned. No maintainer was contacted and no roadmap was read beyond the
model cards.

# S4 Recommendation: Choosing for the Long Run

## The durable choice

**BGE-M3 is the lowest-regret default for Chinese, Japanese and Korean retrieval on CPU today.** It was at or tied with the leader in all
three languages on S2's test, at the ceiling on translation matching, MIT-licensed, runnable with no repository code, and fits in
about 3 GiB resident (measured 2026-11-05; `S2-comprehensive/recommendation.md`). Its repository has not changed since
2024-07-03 (source: Hub API, 2026-11-03), which for an index built on it means stability, not neglect.

## What drives the long-run picture

1. **New generations arrive often and do not automatically win.** Between June 2025 and October 2026, Qwen3, Harrier, F2LLM-v2
   and Nemotron all shipped, Qwen3 and Harrier with vendor leaderboard claims (S1). On this CJK retrieval test none of them beat BGE-M3 outside
   a tie, and the ones near it cost more to run (measured 2026-11-05; `S2-comprehensive/memory-and-throughput.md`). Expect more
   releases; treat each one as a candidate to test on your own data.
2. **The exit cost is a re-embed, and it is measurable.** On 4 vCPUs BGE-M3 took about 114 hours per million passages and
   e5-small about 10 (same file). That number, not the model's age, is what a migration costs.
3. **Licenses move models in and out.** Several capable models are non-commercial by their own terms, and the Hub tag does not
   always show it (`model-maintenance-and-licensing.md`). Re-read the card at the commit you pin.
4. **Single-language specialists are a live but narrow bet.** Ruri v3 (Japanese) and KURE-v1 (Korean) tie the multilingual
   leaders; choosing one closes off other languages in the same index. On Chinese no specialist came close.
5. **The serving layer is stable.** sentence-transformers 6.2.0 ran all 22 measured panel models (Qwen3-Embedding-4B ran out of memory at the cap while loading) and reads each model's own pooling and
   prompts (S1, `serving-layers.md`); prompts that live only on a model card are the part to carry by hand.

## Paths

| Path | When | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Multilingual default: BGE-M3, pinned | Any mix of CJK and English, or uncertain future languages | Larger memory and slower encode than the small models |
| Measured step down: e5-base or e5-small | Large corpora, small machines, frequent re-embeds | A measured score gap of 0.014 to 0.082 nDCG@10 against BGE-M3 |
| Language specialist: Ruri v3 or KURE-v1 | One language for certain, and the specialist wins on your own test | A second model and index if a language is added |
| Hosted API | No machine to run a model, and text may leave the network | Not measured here for CJK quality; vendor terms and prices can change |

Score gaps from S2 (measured 2026-11-05; `S2-comprehensive/retrieval-quality.md`).

## When to revisit

Re-test when a new model generation claims gains on CJK retrieval, when a candidate publishes CPU-friendly exports that S2 did
not measure (ONNX, OpenVINO, int8), or when the corpus changes shape (long documents, mixed languages, a domain far from
Wikipedia). The models move monthly, so these verdicts carry a date for a reason.
