## At a glance

*Findings checked against this survey's current text on 2026-11-05.*


| Library | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Hugging Face Tokenizers** | Rust core reading tokenizer.json; BPE, Unigram, WordPiece and WordLevel | Counting and encoding for open-weight models that publish tokenizer.json |
| **SentencePiece** | C++ Unigram and BPE trainer and encoder with a Python binding | Training a vocabulary, and reading .model files |

| **transformers** | Python wrapper that selects a tokenizer backend | Python code already using AutoTokenizer |


**What the research found**

- **The vocabulary file sets the token cost, not the engine** — Chinese ran from 701 to 1,443 tokens per 1,000 codepoints across files exact on at least 99.99% of lines, and two encodings in tiktoken differ by 32% (measured 2026-11-04; 29.9M codepoints of zhwiki; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/summary.json).
- **No file leads across Chinese, Japanese and Korean** — Among files exact on at least 99.99% of lines, MiniMax M2.5 leads on Chinese at 701, LLM-jp-3 on Japanese at 556 and A.X 4.0 on Korean at 471. Stockmark-2 is lower on Japanese at 520 with NFKC rewriting 51.5% of lines, and costs 1,844 on Korean (measured 2026-11-04; same file).
- **Normalizers decide what comes back** — Published files with no normalizer returned all 1,964,779 lines exactly, while trained SentencePiece models with an identity normalizer and byte fallback lost whitespace on 1.3% of Chinese and 7.7% of English lines; NFKC and BERT-style files changed about 85% or more of Chinese lines (measured 2026-11-04; same file).
- **Rare characters survive byte fallback and are lost elsewhere** — 38 of 41 files emitted no unknown token on any CJK block tested; XLM-RoBERTa in both formats and BERT Chinese did, on nearly all of Extension B (measured 2026-11-04; 42,720 characters; results/probes/).
- **A token can split a character, and how often depends on the file** — cl100k_base has 53.8% partial tokens on Chinese, Qwen 3.8 has 1.9% and rises to 12.9% on Cantonese (measured 2026-11-04; results/summary.json). Streaming clients need byte buffering.

# What Are CJK Tokenizers?

A language model does not read characters. It reads a list of integers, and a **tokenizer** turns text into that list and back. For Chinese, Japanese and Korean (CJK) text, that step decides how many integers a sentence costs, whether the sentence comes back unchanged, and whether a rare character in somebody's name survives at all.

This matters to you in three ways:

- **Money.** Hosted models charge per token.
- **Room.** A context window is a limit in tokens, so a costly tokenizer fits less of your document.
- **Correctness.** Some tokenizers alter text, and some cut characters in half at token boundaries.

## An analogy: the phrasebook

Think of a tokenizer as a phrasebook of fixed size, say 200,000 entries, and a rule that you may only communicate by citing entry numbers.

A phrasebook written mostly for English has an entry for " international" but few for Chinese, so a Chinese sentence gets spelled out in small pieces, many of them less than a whole character. A phrasebook with lots of Chinese has entries for 你好 and 世界. Both can express anything, because each ends with an alphabet of raw bytes. They differ in how many citations a sentence takes.

The book is the **vocabulary file**. The person looking things up is the **engine**. A faster person does not change how many citations a sentence takes.

## Why CJK is a harder case

**No spaces.** English offers word boundaries for free, while a Chinese or Japanese sentence does not. A tokenizer that splits on spaces first sees a whole sentence as one word.

**A big inventory.** English needs a few dozen letters. Chinese needs thousands of characters for ordinary text and tens of thousands more for names and old sources. Unicode assigns over 90,000 of them. Korean is built from thousands of syllable blocks.

**Three or four bytes each.** In UTF-8, a typical CJK character is three bytes, and rarer ones four. A byte-based tokenizer that never learned a character pays one token per byte.

## The main kinds

**Byte-level BPE (byte pair encoding)** starts from bytes and learns merges for pairs that occur together, repeated many times over. Any text can be expressed, and nothing is ever "unknown." The price is that a token can stop in the middle of a character. Most recent models use it: OpenAI's, Qwen, DeepSeek, GLM (General Language Model), MiniMax, Llama.

**Unigram**, usually through the SentencePiece library, stores pieces with probabilities and finds the likeliest way to cut a sentence. It handles text with no spaces by design. Pieces are whole characters or strings. What happens to a character outside the vocabulary depends on how it was trained: it either becomes an unknown token and is lost, or falls back to bytes.

**Character BPE with byte fallback** merges characters and drops to bytes for anything unknown. Gemma and Mistral 7B work this way.

**WordPiece**, from BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers, a classifier model), gives each Han character its own token from a short list. It is simple and stays common for classification and embeddings, but it does not give text back unchanged.

**Hosted counters** have no file you can get. You ask the vendor's API how many tokens a request will cost.

## What measuring them shows

Results below come from 41 tokenizer files on Wikipedia text. Full tables are in `01-discovery/S2-comprehensive/`.

**The file decides the cost.** On Chinese, files ran from about 0.70 tokens per character to about 1.44, a factor of two on the same text. OpenAI's own older and newer encodings run 1.31 and 0.89, in the same engine (measured 2026-11-04; tiktoken 0.14.0, tokenizers 0.23.3; 29.9M codepoints of zhwiki; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/summary.json).

**No file wins every language.** Stockmark-2 has the lowest Japanese count, 520 per 1,000 codepoints, and its normalizer rewrites 51.5% of Japanese lines. LLM-jp-3 is lowest among files that keep the text (exact on at least 99.99% of lines), at 556. Stockmark-2 costs 1,844 on Korean, where A.X 4.0 costs 471 (same run).

**Rare characters are safe in most files and lost in a few.** Byte-based files kept every one of more than 42,000 characters in Unicode's Extension B block, at about four tokens each. XLM-RoBERTa and BERT Chinese replaced nearly all of them with an unknown token (measured 2026-11-04; each codepoint encoded alone; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/probes/).

**Some files rewrite ordinary text.** Files with an NFKC (Normalization Form Compatibility Composition) normalizer, a Unicode folding rule, turned a fullwidth Chinese comma into an ASCII one. About 85% of Chinese lines changed (measured 2026-11-04; same run, results/summary.json). Published files with no normalizer returned every line exactly. Trained models with no normalizer can still drop whitespace: the SentencePiece models with byte fallback here did so on 1.3% of Chinese lines and 7.7% of English lines (measured 2026-11-05; results/trained_tok/).

**Half a character is common in some files.** With OpenAI's older encoding, more than half of the tokens on Chinese text end inside a character. Print them one at a time and the user sees replacement marks. In newer files with better CJK coverage it falls to about 2%, and rises again on Cantonese (same file).

## Things that look like choices and are not

**A trained model's tokenizer is fixed.** The weights learned those integers. You cannot swap in a cheaper file. The decision is which model, with token cost as one input.

**"CJK-optimized" describes a file.** The Hugging Face engine reads Qwen's file and Llama's file equally well.

**Word segmenters are a separate category.** Jieba and MeCab find words for search and analysis. A language model's tokenizer has no duty to respect word boundaries, and its tokens often do not. See 1.033.2 and 1.148.

## Where the choices do live

- **Which model**, with token cost as one input.
- **Training your own vocabulary**, where trainer, size and normalization are all yours.
- **Which encoder runs a given file**, when more than one can.
- **What you do around the tokenizer:** normalize input, buffer bytes when streaming, keep the original string, and count with the right file.

## Words you will meet

- **Token:** one entry in the vocabulary, one integer.
- **Vocabulary:** the file of entries, 21,128 to 262,144 in this sample.
- **Round trip:** encode then decode; is the text identical?
- **Normalizer:** a step that rewrites text before encoding. NFC (Normalization Form Composition) composes characters, while NFKC also folds compatibility forms.
- **Byte fallback:** expressing an unknown character as its bytes instead of an unknown token.
- **Partial token:** a token that stops inside a character.
- **Special token:** a control entry such as end-of-text. Some engines turn a matching string in user text into one, which matters for untrusted input.

# S1 Approach: The Field in Three Layers

A CJK tokenizer choice is three separate things that the phrase "which tokenizer" runs together:

1. **Kind**: byte-level BPE, Unigram, character BPE with byte fallback, WordPiece, or a hosted counter (`kinds-of-tokenizer.md`).
2. **Engine**: the code that reads a file: Hugging Face Tokenizers, tiktoken, SentencePiece and the wrappers around them (`engines.md`).
3. **Vocabulary**: the file a model was trained with (`vocabularies.md`). This layer sets the token count.

## The sample

Forty-one tokenizer files, chosen to cover model origin and format rather than to rank models: global open-weight and OpenAI files, multilingual files, and files from Chinese-, Japanese- and Korean-origin models. The Hugging Face repos were pinned to a commit SHA on 2026-11-03 and listed in `harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/pins.json`. Hugging Face's 30 most-downloaded text-generation repos, read 2026-11-05 UTC, are mostly Qwen, DeepSeek and OpenAI repos and derivatives of them, with older repos such as GPT-2 and OPT among them (https://huggingface.co/api/models?sort=downloads&limit=30&pipeline_tag=text-generation; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/field_check.json). One family outside the sample appears there: Ornith 1.5. Its 9B repo's model, normalizer, pre-tokenizer and decoder sections are identical to Qwen 3.8's (same file), so its regular vocabulary is not new. Its added tokens and post-processor were not compared.

## Boundaries

Word segmentation for search and analysis is 1.033.2 and 1.148. Named-entity recognition is 1.033.4. Choosing a model on output quality is outside this survey: only tokenizer files were read, and no model was loaded.

# Tokenizer Engines: What Runs a Vocabulary File

An engine reads a vocabulary file and turns text into ids. Picking an engine rarely decides how much Chinese costs, because
the file does that. The engine decides the file formats you can load, whether you can train, how special strings are handled,
what happens with a broken string, and what runs on your platform.

Registry and repository facts below were read on 2026-11-04 (harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/library_facts.json; that file's `read_at` is in UTC, where the date had already turned).
Stars are a popularity reading only.

## The three main engines

**Hugging Face Tokenizers** (`tokenizers` on PyPI, huggingface/tokenizers, Apache-2.0, 0.23.3 released 2026-10-09). A Rust core
with Python and Node bindings that reads `tokenizer.json`: a declarative pipeline of normalizer, pre-tokenizer, model (BPE, Unigram,
WordPiece, WordLevel), post-processor and decoder. It trains as well as encodes. This format is where most open-weight models now
publish their vocabulary, and `transformers` version 5 makes it the default backend, with SentencePiece and mistral-common as
non-default alternatives (source: https://huggingface.co/blog/transformers-v5, accessed 2026-11-04). A rewrite is in release
candidates: 1.0.0-rc.3 shipped 2026-10-19, with a stated goal of "same API, same token IDs" and a statement that feature parity with
0.23 is the milestone left before a stable 1.0 (source: https://github.com/huggingface/tokenizers/releases, accessed 2026-11-04).

**tiktoken** (`tiktoken` on PyPI, openai/tiktoken, MIT, 0.14.0 released 2026-08-17). A Rust BPE encoder with a Python wrapper. It does not
train. OpenAI publishes `cl100k_base` and `o200k_base` for it, and nothing in the changelog from 0.9.0 to 0.14.0 adds an encoding; the entries
add model names, wheels and performance work (source: https://github.com/openai/tiktoken/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md, accessed 2026-11-04). OpenAI is not its only
user: Kimi K2 ships a `tiktoken.model` file, and mistral-common builds its Tekken tokenizer on the same encoder. There is no
normalizer stage, so what goes in is what comes back.

**SentencePiece** (`sentencepiece` on PyPI, google/sentencepiece, Apache-2.0). A C++ trainer and encoder for
Unigram and BPE models with a Python binding, built to segment text with no spaces. Its defaults are `character_coverage` 0.9995,
`byte_fallback` false, `normalization_rule_name` nmt_nfkc and `model_type` unigram; the documentation advises 0.9995 for Chinese, Japanese and
Korean and calls byte fallback highly recommended for modern LLMs (source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/sentencepiece/master/doc/options.md,
accessed 2026-11-04). Development picked up: 0.2.2 moved the binding from SWIG to pybind11, and 0.2.3, released on PyPI 2026-11-05 UTC, adds automatic
character coverage, Sparse L1 Unigram pruning, a zero-dependency C++20 runtime that reads a new FlatBuffers model format, and removals of deprecated API
(source: https://github.com/google/sentencepiece/releases, accessed 2026-11-04). The token counts here ran on 0.2.2; `S2-comprehensive/engine-behavior.md` compares the two releases. Several Chinese and Japanese models still publish its `.model` format: Yi, InternLM 3, Baichuan 2 and Sarashina among the
repos checked on 2026-11-04.

## Wrappers and vendor layers

**transformers** (`transformers` on PyPI, 5.22.1) is what most Python code calls. `AutoTokenizer` chooses a backend and adds its own behavior
on top, including chat templates and space cleanup on decode. Some repos ship a Python tokenizer file, as Kimi, InternLM and Baichuan do, and loading those through `AutoTokenizer` means `trust_remote_code`: running the repo's code to get a token count. The harness read the vocabulary files directly and ran none of that code.

**mistral-common** (`mistral-common` on PyPI, mistralai/mistral-common, Apache-2.0, 1.13.1 released 2026-11-02) is Mistral's request and tokenizer
layer. Its Tekken files are a byte-level vocabulary in JSON. It manages special tokens by hand, separate from text.

**google-genai** (`google-genai` on PyPI, 2.34.0) carries an experimental `LocalTokenizer`, text only, that maps Gemini model names to open tokenizer files:
a pinned Gemma 3 SentencePiece file for the 2.x and 3.0 preview names and `google/gemma-4-E4B-it` for the 3.1 and 3.5 names. Its docstring says forward compatibility
"depends on the open-source tokenizer models for future Gemini versions" (source: https://github.com/googleapis/python-genai/blob/main/google/genai/_local_tokenizer_loader.py,
accessed 2026-11-04).

# Kinds of Tokenizer for CJK Text

A tokenizer has two parts that are easy to run together: an **engine** (the code) and a **vocabulary** (the file). The same engine with
two files can spend very different token counts on Chinese. Most decisions in this
category are decisions about a file, and for most readers the file comes with the model.

Three things make CJK a harder case than English:

- **No spaces to lean on.** A pre-tokenizer built around whitespace sees a whole Chinese sentence as one word. Every kind below needs its own answer.
- **A large inventory.** Chinese needs thousands of characters to reach ordinary text and tens of thousands more for names and historical sources.
  A vocabulary has to spend entries on them, or fall back to something smaller.
- **Three bytes per character in UTF-8, four above the Basic Multilingual Plane.** A byte-level tokenizer that has not learned a character spends up to that many
  tokens on it, and the pieces mean nothing on their own.

## Byte-level BPE

Text is cut by a regular expression, each piece becomes UTF-8 bytes, and learned merges join the bytes back into larger tokens. Any string can be encoded, with no unknown token, because
all 256 bytes are in the vocabulary. GPT-2 introduced the pattern; `cl100k_base`, `o200k_base`, Qwen, DeepSeek, GLM, MiniMax, Llama 3, EXAONE and most recent open-weight models follow it.
How well it serves Chinese depends on how much CJK text was in the training mix and how many entries the file has. It was not designed around any one language.

A token can end partway through a character. Qwen's own tokenization note says so: uncommon text may fall back to single bytes, and decoding those alone prints the replacement character
(source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/QwenLM/Qwen/main/tokenization_note.md, accessed 2026-11-04). A streaming client that prints token by token has to hold bytes back. `S2-comprehensive/vocabulary-composition.md` measures how often it happens per file.

## Script-aware splitting ahead of byte-level BPE

A variation on the same family: the regular expression treats Han as its own class, so a run of Han reaches the merges whole and never joins Latin. Kimi K2's pattern opens with a
`[\p{Han}]+` alternative (read in its `tokenization_kimi.py`). It is still byte-level underneath.

## Unigram with character pieces (SentencePiece)

Instead of merging upward, Unigram scores a vocabulary of pieces and picks the most probable segmentation of a sentence, with no need for spaces. Entries are characters and strings, so every token prints alone.
Characters outside the vocabulary become `<unk>`, or UTF-8 byte tokens if the file was trained with `byte_fallback`. That choice decides whether the text survives a round trip. SentencePiece also carries a normalizer
inside its model file, and the default rewrites text. LLM-jp's tokenizers use this form, extended from Mistral's vocabulary for Japanese, Chinese and Korean, with 99,574 entries in the 3.0 release
(source: https://github.com/llm-jp/llm-jp-tokenizer, accessed 2026-11-04).

## Character-level BPE with byte fallback

SentencePiece BPE and files converted from it (Mistral 7B, Gemma) start from characters rather than bytes. Merges work on characters, and unknown ones drop to byte tokens. A small file of this kind has few CJK entries, so much CJK text falls to bytes: Mistral 7B's 32,000 entries spend 1,459 on tokens containing Han. A large one (Gemma has 262,144 entries) holds 20,610 (measured 2026-11-04; tokenizer files at pinned revisions; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/probes/).

## WordPiece

BERT's scheme. `bert-base-chinese` puts a space around every Han character and looks each one up in a list of 21,128 entries (measured 2026-11-04; same results directory). Each character is one token, unknown ones become `[UNK]`, and text is normalized and lowercased
along the way. It was built for classifiers rather than for generating text, and it does not give the original string back. It is here because it remains a common reach for Chinese embedding and classification work.

## Morphological pre-segmentation

Some Japanese and Korean tokenizers split text into words with a dictionary-based analyzer before anything else, as `bert-base-japanese` does with MeCab. Word segmentation itself is covered in 1.033.2 and 1.148. For LLM vocabularies it has largely given way to the kinds above.

## Hosted counters

Some providers publish no file. Claude's count comes from an API endpoint, and the provider's own documentation says the count moved by roughly 30 percent between model generations (vendor: https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/token-counting, accessed 2026-11-04). See `engines.md`.

## Things that are easy to mistake for a tokenizer choice

- **A model's tokenizer is not optional.** With a trained model, you use its file. The ids are what the weights were trained on. The open question is which model, and token cost is one input.
- **"CJK-optimized" is a property of a file, not of an engine.** Nothing about Hugging Face's engine makes text cheaper. It loads Qwen's file and Llama's file equally.
- **Word segmenters are a different category.** Jieba, MeCab and their kin find words for search and analysis. A language model's tokenizer has no obligation to respect word boundaries.

# S1 Recommendation: The Field and Starting Points

## The finding that reorganizes the field

The vocabulary sets the token count, and the engine does not. The same tiktoken engine spends 1,314 tokens per 1,000 codepoints of Chinese Wikipedia text with `cl100k_base`, 890 with `o200k_base` and 780 with Kimi K2.6's file. The two formats of `o200k` (tiktoken and `tokenizer.json`) produced identical ids on every line compared (measured 2026-11-04; tiktoken 0.14.0, tokenizers 0.23.3; zhwiki dump 20261001; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/summary.json and results/interop.json).

So "SentencePiece or tiktoken or Hugging Face" is the wrong first question. For most readers the model is chosen first and its file comes along.

## Starting points by job

| Job | Start with | Why | Avoid for this job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Counting tokens for an OpenAI model | tiktoken with the encoding the model maps to | It is the vendor's encoder | Estimating from characters |
| Counting tokens for an open-weight model | Hugging Face Tokenizers reading that model's `tokenizer.json` | Most files in the sample ship in this format | Any other model's file as a stand-in |
| Counting tokens for a hosted model with no file | The vendor's counting endpoint | No file is published to count with | A ratio from an old model |
| Training a new vocabulary with CJK text | SentencePiece or Hugging Face BPE; compare both on your text | `S2-comprehensive/training.md` | `nmt_nfkc` default normalization if text must come back intact |
| Text that must decode back exactly | A published byte-level file with no normalizer | Exact on every line tested; a model you train needs its whitespace option checked | NFKC-normalizing files (XLM-RoBERTa, mT5, Stockmark-2); BERT Chinese |
| Rare, historical or name characters | A file with byte fallback and no NFC or NFKC normalizer | No unknown tokens across every assigned CJK block, and compatibility forms kept | XLM-RoBERTa, BERT Chinese; NFC files such as Qwen 3.8, MiniMax and K-EXAONE for compatibility ideographs (12 of 472 kept) |
| Classifiers and embeddings already built on BERT Chinese | Keep it, knowing what it drops | One token per Han character | Using its counts as an LLM cost estimate |

Sources for the table's measured claims: `S2-comprehensive/round-trip-fidelity.md` and `S2-comprehensive/rare-and-mixed.md`.

## Rules that held in the measurements

- No file is cheapest across languages. Among files exact on at least 99.99% of a language's lines, MiniMax M2.5 was lowest on Chinese (701), LLM-jp-3 on Japanese (556) and A.X 4.0 on Korean (471). Stockmark-2 reaches 520 on Japanese with an NFKC normalizer that rewrites 51.5% of lines, and costs 1,844 on Korean (measured 2026-11-04; same run, tokens per 1,000 codepoints).
- The origin of a model predicts its cost on its own language and says little about its neighbors. The range on Korean runs from 471 to 1,873, and the top of that range is Yi 1.5, a Chinese-origin model (same run).
- A bigger file is not automatically cheaper: Kimi K2.6 holds 69,451 tokens containing Han and costs 780 on Chinese, while Qwen 3.8 holds 55,335 and costs 728 (same run).

Three vocabularies ship in two formats. Each line was encoded by both sides, across 5M-codepoint Chinese and Korean samples, 5M codepoints of Japanese and 2M each of mixed text.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| o200k: tiktoken and `tokenizer.json` (gpt-oss) | 278,582 | 278,582 | 278,582 | 278,582 |
| Mistral 7B: SentencePiece and Tokenizers | 278,582 | 278,581 | 278,581 | 278,581 |
| XLM-RoBERTa: SentencePiece and Tokenizers | 278,582 | 278,575 | 0 | 274,068 |

Measured 2026-11-04; tiktoken 0.14.0, tokenizers 0.23.3, sentencepiece 0.2.2; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/interop.json. XLM-RoBERTa's `tokenizer.json` offsets every id relative to the SentencePiece file, so its ids never match even when the tokens do. Its token-text column understates agreement: the mismatches include the same unknown token rendered ` ⁇ ` by one engine and `<unk>` by the other. The result keeps only sample mismatches, so the share caused by this is not computed.

A converted file can match, and matching is not guaranteed by the format. On decomposed Korean, XLM-RoBERTa's SentencePiece file normalizes to the composed form and its `tokenizer.json` does not: the same text cost 1.0 and 3.23 times its composed count (`token-cost.md`).

## Text that looks like a special token

Each file's own special strings were placed inside Chinese text and passed to the engine's default encode call.

| Engine | Files | Default behavior |
|---|---:|---|
| Tokenizers | 29 | Every tested string became its control id |
| tiktoken, `encode` | 2 | Raises an error; `encode_ordinary` treats it as text |
| SentencePiece | 8 | Treated as text |
| mistral-common Tekken | 1 | Treated as text |

Measured 2026-11-04; up to 12 special strings per file; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/probes/. Kimi's file was not tested here, since the repo's wrapper holds its special table and repo code was not run.

For a service tokenizing untrusted text, this is the one engine difference with security consequences. With Tokenizers' default call, a user who types a chat-template control string produces the control token, unless the application handles that string first. The corpus contains examples: Wikipedia lines holding a literal `</s>` came back altered from LLM-jp-3.

# S2 Recommendation: What the Measurements Settle

## Findings

1. **The file sets the cost, and the spread is wide.** Among files exact on at least 99.99% of a language's lines, Chinese ran from 701 to 1,443 tokens per 1,000 codepoints, Japanese from 556 to 1,464, and Korean from 471 to 1,873. The lowest cost overall differs on two of those: Japanese 520 (Stockmark-2, whose NFKC normalizer leaves 48.5% of lines exact) and Korean 298 (BERT Chinese, through unknown tokens) (measured 2026-11-04; tiktoken 0.14.0, tokenizers 0.23.3, sentencepiece 0.2.2; Wikipedia dumps 20261001; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/summary.json).
2. **No file leads in all three languages.** Among files that keep the text, MiniMax M2.5 leads on Chinese (701), LLM-jp-3 on Japanese (556) and A.X 4.0 on Korean (471). Stockmark-2 is lower on Japanese (520) and rewrites the text. LLM-jp-4, Qwen 3.8 and MiniMax are the most even across the three (same file; `token-cost.md`).
3. **Fidelity divides on the normalizer.** Published files with none returned all 1,964,779 lines exactly; the trained SentencePiece models with an identity normalizer and byte fallback did not, losing whitespace on 1.3% of Chinese and 7.7% of English lines (`training.md`). NFC files missed five. NFKC and BERT-style files changed most Chinese lines (same file; `round-trip-fidelity.md`).
4. **Unknown tokens are rare in LLM files.** Of the 41 files, only XLM-RoBERTa in both formats and BERT Chinese emitted unknown tokens on the CJK blocks (measured 2026-11-04; results/probes/; `rare-and-mixed.md`).
5. **Partial tokens range from none to most.** `cl100k_base` is at 53.8% on Chinese, Qwen 3.8 at 1.9% and Gemma at 0.3% (results/summary.json; `vocabulary-composition.md`).
6. **Engines differ on hostile input and memory.** Tokenizers turns special strings into control ids by default, while the others do not, and the same vocabulary used 2.6 to 5.3 times the memory as `tokenizer.json` (results/probes/; `engine-behavior.md`).
7. **A small trained file competes on CJK text close to its training text.** 32,000 entries trained on 30M codepoints reached 724 on Chinese, in the range of files holding 150,000 to 250,000 entries, with a large English cost; no English was in the training text. Hugging Face byte-level BPE ended at a 3G memory cap on that text (measured 2026-11-05; sentencepiece 0.2.2, tokenizers 0.23.3; results/train/ and results/trained_tok/; `training.md`).

## Ranking by job

| Job | First choice | Second | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Count tokens for a given open-weight model | Tokenizers with that model's file | The vendor's own layer, where one exists | Only the model's file is right |
| Count tokens for OpenAI models | tiktoken | `tokenizer.json` conversion | Same ids on all 278,582 lines compared |
| Encode untrusted text | tiktoken `encode_ordinary`, SentencePiece, or Tekken | Tokenizers with special strings handled first | Default behavior differs |
| Train a vocabulary for CJK text | SentencePiece BPE or Unigram with byte fallback, whitespace option checked | Hugging Face byte-level BPE where memory allows and text must come back exact | `training.md` |
| Keep memory low | `.model` or `.tiktoken` format where one exists | `tokenizer.json` | `engine-behavior.md` |

Sources: the files named in the Why column and the findings above. Encode speed is left out of this ranking because it is unmeasured.

## What these numbers do not cover

Output quality per tokenizer, encode speed, phone or browser runtimes, a hosted model's live counting, and text registers other than Wikipedia. `approach.md` lists the conditions.

# Round-Trip Fidelity: Does the Text Come Back?

Each line was encoded, decoded and compared with the original byte for byte, across 1,964,779 lines of the main corpora. A mismatch takes the first class that makes both sides equal: unknown token present, NFKC, whitespace, NFKC plus whitespace, case, else other.

## The result falls into three groups

DeepSeek V4.1, MiMo and Kanana were not run on the corpus. `dedupe.py` found their model, normalizer, pre-tokenizer and decoder sections identical to DeepSeek V3.2, Qwen 3 and Llama 3.2, so their counts here are inferred from the matching file. The fingerprint leaves out added tokens, which differ (DeepSeek 818 and 1,283, Llama and Kanana 256 and 259), and the post-processor, which matched in all three pairs (results/dedupe.json). Each pair's id lists also hashed the same over a 3.5M-codepoint sample (results/tokenizers_versions.json).

**Exact on every line:** every published byte-level or byte-fallback file with no rewriting normalizer. That covers OpenAI's files, Llama 3.2, Gemma, Mistral 7B, Tekken, Nemotron 3, DeepSeek, GLM, Hy3, Kimi, Yi, InternLM 3, Baichuan 2, LLM-jp-4, Sarashina, EXAONE 4.0, A.X 4.0 and Solar Open.

**Exact except rare characters:** files with an NFC normalizer: Qwen 2.5, 3 and 3.8, MiMo, MiniMax, CALM3, K-EXAONE, A.X K1 and HyperCLOVA X SEED. Five Japanese lines failed in each, on compatibility characters that NFC folds to a standard form: U+FA19, a variant of 神, and U+2329, an angle bracket. LLM-jp-3 failed on one Korean line where a literal `</s>` in the article text parsed as a control token and came back with a space after it; two Cantonese lines outside the main count did the same.

Measured 2026-11-04; line counts over the nine main corpora; same file. NFKC-class failures change forms and keep meaning in most text: fullwidth punctuation, halfwidth katakana, compatibility characters. Unknown-token failures lose text. ## Normalizers and NFD

An NFC normalizer rewrites NFD input. The files in the second group failed 93,843 of 93,884 lines of decomposed Korean, and decoded each to the composed form. A pipeline that hashes or diffs its text needs to know which form a tokenizer gives back.

## Decoding is not the same as streaming

Exact whole-line decoding hides a separate problem: a token can end inside a character. `vocabulary-composition.md` measures how often. A client that decodes each token as it arrives prints U+FFFD at those points, even with a file that is exact end to end.

## What to take from this

Any file a model already uses is fixed, so fidelity is a property to know about instead of a choice. It matters for chunking, offsets, redaction and any flow that reconstructs the user's text from token ids. Keep the original string and treat decoded text as display, for the files in the third group above all.

## What the two tables show

- **A small file can match a large one on text like its training text.** 32,000 entries reached 724 on Chinese. MiniMax's 200,054 entries cost 701 and Qwen 3.8's 248,077 cost 728, on the same test lines (results/summary.json). - **A low count with unknown tokens is not a saving.** On Chinese, character BPE at 6M codepoints has the lowest count and the most lost text: it emitted 58,828 unknown tokens and returned 92.2% of Chinese lines exact. Each unknown token stands in for text it did not encode.
- **Byte fallback costs little.** SentencePiece Unigram went from 727 to 731 on Chinese with byte fallback on, and exact Chinese lines went from 92.3% to 98.7%.
- **The text a model never saw costs most.** Cantonese is the widest case: 864 for SentencePiece Unigram with byte fallback at 6M codepoints, against 747 on modern Chinese.
- **More text helps by a small margin.** SentencePiece Unigram with byte fallback went from 747 to 731 on Chinese when training text grew from 6M to 30M codepoints. A larger vocabulary was not tested.

## Whitespace

Every SentencePiece model lost whitespace on some lines: 7.7% of English lines and 1.3% of Chinese lines with byte fallback on, and these lines matched once whitespace was ignored. The Hugging Face models had no whitespace failures. SentencePiece's `remove_extra_whitespaces` option defaults to on (source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/sentencepiece/master/doc/options.md, accessed 2026-11-05), and these runs left it there, which is the likely cause; turning it off was not tested. Published SentencePiece files in the sample, such as Yi and Sarashina, returned every line exactly.

## Limits of this comparison

SentencePiece logged and skipped lines over its 65,536-byte limit, and release 0.2.3 lifts a related limit for BPE (read, not run). The pre-tokenizers differ between engines by design. `auto_character_coverage`, new in SentencePiece 0.2.3, was not run.

What this means in practice:

- **Streaming.** With `cl100k_base`, more than half of Chinese tokens cannot print alone. - **Weakness shows up in the out-of-language column.** Yi is at 71.8% on Korean and Stockmark-2 at 89.2%: almost everything falls to bytes.

# S3 Recommendation: Which Option for Which Situation

| Situation | What decides it | Start with | Check before committing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paying per token on Chinese text | Count times price, on your script | The count from your model's file or the vendor's endpoint | A month of real traffic against the bill |
| Chunking Japanese or Korean | Chunk text length; offsets back to source | The reading model's file, with splits on character boundaries | Citations landing on the right span |
| Streaming CJK output | How often tokens split characters | A byte-buffering client, whatever the file | A test string with Extension B and emoji |
| Rare characters and names | No unknown tokens, no folding | Files with byte fallback and no NFC or NFKC normalizer | The codepoints your collection holds |
| Training a vocabulary | Trainer settings and memory | SentencePiece with byte fallback, or Hugging Face byte-level BPE if memory allows | Your own text, including the languages left out of training |
| Offline or on-device | Format and memory | The tokenizer embedded in your runtime, or a `.model` file | The lowest device you support |

Details and evidence are in each `use-case-*.md` file.

## Three cross-cutting points

- **The model usually comes first.** Five of the six situations take the file as given and decide what to do around it. Training is the exception.
- **Normalize at your boundary.** NFC ahead of the tokenizer removes the decomposed-Korean penalty of up to ten times in any file (measured 2026-11-04; kowiki sample; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/summary.json). Skip this for archives where the original form is the record.
- **Keep the original string.** Several situations break when decoded text stands in for the source.

# Use Case: A Service Paying per Token on Chinese Text

## Who needs this

A team running a customer-support assistant, search summarizer or document tool whose users write mostly Chinese, billed per token by a model vendor or limited by a context window on its own hardware.

## The pain

- A budget built on an English characters-per-token ratio is wrong by a large margin. English Wikipedia ran 210 to 267 tokens per 1,000 codepoints across files exact on at least 99.99% of lines; Chinese ran 701 to 1,443 (measured 2026-11-04; Wikipedia dumps 20261001; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/summary.json).
- A model upgrade can move the bill with no code change. `cl100k_base` to `o200k_base` took off 32% on Chinese (same file).
- A price per token and a tokens-per-character figure only mean something together. The cheapest count may sit on the most expensive token.

## Requirements that follow

1. A count from the file the model uses, or from the vendor's endpoint if none is published.
2. A measurement on your own text, in your script: simplified, traditional or both.
3. A count of the full request, with system prompt and tool definitions.

## Options that survive

| Option | Chinese corpus / all-traditional sample, tokens per 1,000 codepoints | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| MiniMax M2.5 file | 701 / 753 | Lowest on Chinese in this sample |
| DeepSeek V3.2 file (V4.1 inferred from matching model parts) | 707 / 740 | Partial tokens about 1.8% on Chinese |
| Qwen 3.8 file | 728 / 757 | Partial tokens about 1.9% |
| Gemma 3 and Gemma 4 file | 804 / 811 | Little script penalty; `LocalTokenizer` names this file for some Gemini models |
| OpenAI `o200k_base` | 890 / 945 | Current OpenAI models map here |
| OpenAI `cl100k_base` | 1,314 / 1,401 | gpt-4 and gpt-3.5 |

Measured 2026-11-04; first figure from the 29.9M-codepoint zhwiki corpus, second from a 5M-codepoint sample converted to traditional by OpenCC 1.4.2; same file.

The table compares counts and not prices. Multiply by the vendor's price per token, for input and output separately, before drawing a conclusion.

## What to avoid

- Reading a low BERT count as cheap. It is lossy, and no current LLM in this sample uses it.
- Using another model's file as a proxy for a hosted model. Google's `LocalTokenizer` names Gemma files for Gemini models but is marked experimental, and Anthropic publishes no file (`S1-rapid/engines.md`).
- Chinese Wikipedia as your only test text. It mixes scripts and leans encyclopedic.

## Check before committing

- Count a month of real traffic with the file, or a sample through the vendor's endpoint, and compare with the bill.
- Check the model name-to-encoding mapping at each upgrade, and re-count when it changes.
- Confirm that the current price makes the lower count a lower bill.

# Use Case: A Team Training Its Own Vocabulary

## Who needs this

A team training a language model from scratch, or a small domain model, on text with a large CJK share, deciding which trainer to use and how to set it.

## The pain

- Training defaults can lose or alter text. SentencePiece defaults to `byte_fallback` false and `nmt_nfkc` normalization (source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/sentencepiece/master/doc/options.md, accessed 2026-11-04).
- A trainer can run out of memory on a modest corpus. Hugging Face byte-level BPE ended at a 3G cap on 30M codepoints of Chinese, Japanese and Korean, and finished at 929 MB on 6M (measured 2026-11-05; tokenizers 0.23.3; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/train/).
- A vocabulary is permanent once a model trains on it. A mistake means retraining.

## Requirements that follow

1. Every string encodable, by byte fallback or an accepted unknown token.
2. A normalization decision made on purpose.
3. Training memory that fits your machine.
4. Coverage of the languages in the model's future use, including English and code if it will meet them.

## Options that survive

See `S2-comprehensive/training.md` for the table.

| Option | Fits when | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| SentencePiece BPE with byte fallback | Lowest Korean count among the trained models with no unknown tokens, at 98.3% of Korean lines exact | Nothing unknown; streaming needs buffering at a low rate |
| SentencePiece Unigram with byte fallback | The same, with Unigram's segmentation | Close to BPE in count |
| Hugging Face BPE on characters | An unknown token is acceptable and pieces must print alone | No byte fallback, so rare text is lost; highest training memory of those that finished on 30M codepoints |
| Hugging Face byte-level BPE | Exact text matters and memory is available | The only trained kind exact on every line; ended at the 3G cap on the larger set, and at 6M codepoints cost 737 on Chinese |
| SentencePiece with no byte fallback | Rarely | Unknown tokens on 31.9% of Cantonese lines |

All at 32,000 entries, as tested. A larger vocabulary was not trained here.

## What to avoid

- Leaving `nmt_nfkc` in place if text must come back exact. All models here set normalization to identity.
- Training on CJK alone for a model that will meet English: English cost 413 to 446 tokens per 1,000 codepoints on these models, against 210 to 267 for the published files (measured 2026-11-05; results/summary.json).
- Treating a test on your training corpus's own source as proof. Test lines were unseen here but came from the same Wikipedia dumps, which flatters every model.

## Check before committing

- Train on your own corpus at your target size, and test on a different register.
- Confirm your serving stack can read the format.
- SentencePiece 0.2.3 adds `auto_character_coverage` and sparse pruning for vocabulary selection (read, not run), so compare them against the settings above before fixing a choice.
- Check whitespace handling: SentencePiece models here failed on 0.4% to 7.7% of lines on whitespace alone, while the Hugging Face model did not.

# Use Case: An Offline or On-Device Application

## Who needs this

A developer shipping a local model, or local token counting, inside a phone or desktop application that must run without a network, in a Japanese or Chinese product.

## The pain

The tokenizer file and its in-memory structures are small next to weights, and easy to overlook in a tight budget. The same vocabulary can differ widely by format:

| Vocabulary | Format | File bytes | Added process memory on load (kB) |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| o200k | `.tiktoken` through tiktoken | 3,613,922 | 78,944 |
| o200k | `tokenizer.json` through Tokenizers | 27,868,174 | 244,668 |
| Gemma | SentencePiece `.model` | 4,689,074 | 54,636 |
| Gemma 4 | `tokenizer.json` through Tokenizers | 32,169,626 | 289,176 |
| Mistral 7B | SentencePiece `.model` | 493,443 | 11,872 |
| Mistral 7B | `tokenizer.json` through Tokenizers | 1,795,188 | 30,572 |
| XLM-RoBERTa | SentencePiece `.model` | 5,069,051 | 64,704 |
| XLM-RoBERTa | `tokenizer.json` through Tokenizers | 9,096,718 | 343,948 |

Measured 2026-11-04; resident memory before and after loading one file in a fresh process on Python 3.13, aarch64 Linux, including the engine's first import; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/probes/. Desktop Python numbers, with the size relationship as the finding. A phone runtime will not match them.

## Requirements that follow

1. The format your runtime reads. A local runtime usually embeds its tokenizer with the weights, in GGUF for llama.cpp for example (read, not run).
2. Memory and package size within the budget.
3. Local counting that matches the model.

## Options that survive

| Option | Notes |
|---|---|
| The tokenizer embedded in the runtime's model file | The choice is the model's; check the runtime's encoder against the original on your text |
| SentencePiece runtime with a `.model` file | Smallest load in the table; SentencePiece 0.2.3 adds a zero-dependency runtime that reads a FlatBuffers format (vendor: https://github.com/google/sentencepiece/releases, accessed 2026-11-04), not tested here |
| `tokenizer.json` through `@huggingface/tokenizers` or the `tokenizers-wasm` candidate | JavaScript and WebAssembly readers exist (read, not run) |

## What to avoid

- Assuming the two formats give the same result without checking. Mistral's pair and OpenAI's pair matched almost everywhere, while XLM-RoBERTa's two formats disagreed on decomposed Korean (`S2-comprehensive/engine-behavior.md`).
- Quoting load or encode timings from old sources. Speed on a phone is unmeasured in this survey.

## Check before committing

- Measure load time, memory and encode speed on the lowest device you support.
- Compare ids from the embedded tokenizer with the reference file over text in your language.
- Check the app-store size impact of the file itself.

# Use Case: A Catalog, Archive or Registry With Rare Characters

## Who needs this

A library, genealogy service, government-records project or digital-humanities team whose text holds personal-name characters, historical forms, Classical Chinese or variant ideographs, where losing or folding a character changes a fact.

## The pain

- Some files replace rare characters with an unknown token. XLM-RoBERTa does this to 42,719 of Extension B's 42,720 characters, and BERT Chinese to all of them (measured 2026-11-04; each assigned codepoint encoded alone; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/probes/).
- Normalizers fold forms that may matter. NFC files such as Qwen and MiniMax kept 12 of 472 compatibility ideographs and turned the rest into their standard forms (same directory). NFKC goes further: Stockmark-2 and mT5 kept 0 of 214 Kangxi radicals.
- Rare characters cost more. A character above the Basic Multilingual Plane is about four tokens in most files, five with a leading space piece, and Classical Chinese ran above modern Chinese in nearly every file.

## Requirements that follow

1. No unknown tokens across the blocks your text uses.
2. No folding of the forms you must keep.
3. Round trip tested at the character, not the line.

## Options that survive

| File | Unknown tokens on every CJK block tested | Keeps compatibility ideographs | Classical Chinese cost per 1,000 codepoints |
|---|---|---|---:|
| OpenAI o200k, gpt-oss | None | Yes | 1,082 |
| DeepSeek V3.2 (V4.1 inferred from matching model parts) | None | Yes | 920 |
| Gemma 3, Gemma 4 | None | Yes | 942 |
| Baichuan 2 | None | Yes | 921 |
| LLM-jp-4 | None | Yes | 909 |
| Qwen 3.8, MiniMax | None | No (NFC) | 919, 892 |

Measured 2026-11-04; same directory and results/summary.json; zh_classicalwiki 20261001, 6.7M codepoints.

Tokens per character is the lesser concern here. Baichuan 2 holds a whole token for all 3,008 GB2312 level-2 characters and 7,320 of Big5's 7,652 less-frequent ones, and this reduces byte fallback on older text; it is also an older model.

## What to avoid

- Assuming a token count reveals a loss. BERT's count looks low because it has dropped the character.
- Running NFC or NFKC over a source archive before tokenizing, and then writing the result back.

## Check before committing

- Test the codepoints your collection holds, including ideographic variation sequences, which came back exact from every file but XLM-RoBERTa and BERT in the one string tested.
- Check whether the model's output preserves them. A tokenizer can round trip a character a model never produces; no model was run here.
- Keep source text as the record and treat tokenized text as derived.

# Use Case: A Retrieval Pipeline Chunking Japanese or Korean Documents

## Who needs this

A team splitting manuals, contracts or wiki pages into chunks for embedding and retrieval, with chunk sizes set in tokens and citations that point back to the source text.

## The pain

- A chunk size set in tokens holds a different amount of text per file. Among files exact on at least 99.99% of lines, costs run from 556 to 1,464 tokens per 1,000 codepoints on Japanese and from 471 to 1,873 on Korean (measured 2026-11-04; jawiki and kowiki dumps 20261001; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/summary.json).
- Cutting at a token boundary can cut a character: `cl100k_base` has 36.7% partial tokens on Japanese and 50.7% on Korean (same file).
- A tokenizer with a normalizer can break the path back to the source. Stockmark-2's NFKC changed 51.5% of Japanese lines, and mapping decoded text back to source offsets fails unless offsets come from the original string.
- Decomposed Korean, common in text from macOS filesystems, costs up to ten times as much in files that do not normalize (`S2-comprehensive/token-cost.md`).

## Requirements that follow

1. Chunk sizes set against the file of the model that will read the chunks.
2. Splits on decoded character or sentence boundaries, with a token count as a limit and not as a cut point.
3. Source offsets from the original string.
4. NFC normalization ahead of the tokenizer, for ingest that takes files from many systems.

## Options that survive

| Language | Lower cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Japanese | LLM-jp-3 (556), MiniMax (579), Sarashina (589), LLM-jp-4 (589), CALM3 (593) | Stockmark-2 is 520 and rewrites text with NFKC |
| Korean | A.X 4.0 (471), A.X K1 (490), Solar Open (502), LLM-jp-3 (503), K-EXAONE (505) | Japanese-origin byte-level files without Hangul run above 1,500 |
| Both in one corpus | LLM-jp-3 (556, 503), LLM-jp-4 (589, 578), XLM-RoBERTa (667, 593) | XLM-RoBERTa is not an LLM file and rewrites text |

Measured 2026-11-04; tokens per 1,000 codepoints; same file. An embedding model has its own tokenizer, and 1.211 covers CJK embedding models.

## What to avoid

- One global chunk size carried across a model change.
- A chunk boundary that comes from slicing token ids.
- Trusting decoded text as the citation source.

## Check before committing

- Measure chunk text length at your size on your own documents, in each language you hold.
- Verify that citations land on the right source span after normalization.
- Test with decomposed input if files come from macOS or older pipelines.

# Use Case: A Chat Interface Streaming CJK Output

## Who needs this

A developer building a chat or completion interface that prints model output as it arrives, against a local or self-hosted model where the client handles token ids directly.

## The pain

A token can end inside a character. Printed alone, it shows U+FFFD. How often depends on the file and the language, and a test in one language can miss it entirely:

| File | Chinese | Cantonese | Japanese | Korean |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| OpenAI cl100k | 53.8 | 66.0 | 36.7 | 50.7 |
| OpenAI o200k, gpt-oss | 12.4 | 25.4 | 6.3 | 5.2 |
| Kimi K2.6 | 17.7 | 31.0 | 9.8 | 28.0 |
| Qwen 3.8 | 1.9 | 12.9 | 0.5 | 2.4 |
| DeepSeek V3.2 | 1.8 | 8.7 | 3.0 | 10.9 |
| Gemma 3, Gemma 4 | 0.3 | 0.9 | 0.2 | 0.1 |
| LLM-jp-4 | 0.1 | 0.5 | 0.0 | 0.0 |

Measured 2026-11-04; percent of tokens whose lone decode contains U+FFFD, on Wikipedia text and not on model output; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/summary.json. A model's generated text may differ from this mix.

Qwen 3.8 on modern Chinese looks close to safe, and rises to 12.9% on Cantonese. A client with no buffer passes a Mandarin demo and fails later.

## Requirements that follow

1. A client that holds incomplete UTF-8 bytes until a character completes, for any byte-level or byte-fallback file.
2. Tests in each script the product serves, including rare characters: an Extension B character costs about four tokens in most files, all of them partial.

## Options that survive

All files can stream correctly with a buffer. Tokenizers ships a `DecodeStream` decoder and tiktoken returns per-token bytes (read in each package's source, not run). The harness tested whole-line decode and lone-token decode, and no incremental decoder.

## What to avoid

- `decode([id])` per token, joined and printed. It is correct on English, so the bug stays hidden until CJK text arrives.
- Counting on a low-partial file for safety: LLM-jp's Unigram files and Gemma's character BPE are low on common text, and rare characters still reach their byte fallback.

## Check before committing

- Stream a test string with Extension B, emoji joined by ZWJ and halfwidth katakana, and compare the concatenated output with the whole decode.
- Confirm your serving layer's behavior: hosted APIs usually send text deltas and handle this themselves. Check per vendor.

# S4 Approach: What Can Change Under You

A tokenizer's long-term risk has three separate sources, and they call for different responses.

1. **The engine** can change behavior or stop being maintained. The signals are release record, who commits, what a new major version promises, and whether ids hold across releases.
2. **The vocabulary** moves with a model family. A new model name can bring a new file, and the cost can change at that boundary.
3. **Hosted counts** can change with no file to diff.

Forecast percentages are left out: a five-year viability score carries no information a reader can check. The signals below are dated, and the release comparison in `S2-comprehensive/engine-behavior.md` is measured. Repository facts come from `harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/library_facts.json`, read 2026-11-04.

| Engine | License | Latest stable | Last pushed | Last 100 commits reach back to | Distinct authors in those | Top author's share |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| huggingface/tokenizers | Apache-2.0 | 0.23.3, 2026-10-09 | 2026-11-04 | 2026-08-25 | 12 | 57% |
| google/sentencepiece | Apache-2.0 | 0.2.3, 2026-11-05 | 2026-11-05 | 2026-09-08 | 10 | 77% |

| mistralai/mistral-common | Apache-2.0 | 1.13.1, 2026-11-02 | 2026-11-04 | 2026-04-28 | 21 | 75% |
| huggingface/transformers | Apache-2.0 | 5.22.1, 2026-10-30 | 2026-11-05 | 2026-10-30 | 47 | 11% |

## Hugging Face Tokenizers

The format is the asset. Twenty-nine of the 41 files in this sample are `tokenizer.json`, and `transformers` version 5 made this engine its default backend. Commercial backing and that dependency make abandonment an unlikely worry.

The live question is the 1.0 rewrite. The release notes promise the same token ids and say parity with 0.23 is still the milestone left, and rc.3 fixed load failures on regex normalizers used by NLLB and SentencePiece-converted models (source: https://github.com/huggingface/tokenizers/releases, accessed 2026-11-04). A team pinning the 0.23 line has time. `S2-comprehensive/engine-behavior.md` reports the comparison on this sample's files; repeat it on your own before moving.

## SentencePiece

The 2026 record changes the picture of a finished library in slow maintenance. It shipped 0.2.2 in July with a binding rewrite and hardening, and 0.2.3 on 2026-11-05 removes long-deprecated API, raises the baseline to C++20 and Python 3.10, and adds a zero-dependency runtime that reads a new FlatBuffers format (source: https://github.com/google/sentencepiece/releases, accessed 2026-11-04). One maintainer does most of the work: 77 of the last 100 commits come from a single author. Google's ownership and the installed base of `.model` files both argue for continued loading support. The risk to plan for is breakage from that cleanup, and the fact that vocabularies that began as SentencePiece also ship as `tokenizer.json`: Gemma 4 and LLM-jp-3 do. Yi, InternLM 3, Baichuan 2 and Sarashina were read here as `.model` files.

For training, it remains the tool with Unigram, character coverage and byte fallback as first-class options.

## tiktoken

Small, stable and narrow. It reads a simple vocabulary format, and that format has spread beyond OpenAI (Kimi K2, and Tekken through mistral-common). Changes between 0.9.0 and 0.14.0 are model names, wheels and performance, with no new encoding, and the public history is 79 commits since 2022. It is single-vendor in governance, though MIT licensing and the simplicity of the format make it easy to replace: `gpt-tokenizer`, `js-tiktoken` and the `bpe` crate read these vocabularies (read, not run), and `tokenizers` reads the `o200k` vocabulary converted into `tokenizer.json`, with identical ids on every line compared (measured 2026-11-04; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/interop.json).

A reader's lock-in risk here belongs to the vocabulary rather than the code. `cl100k_base` and `o200k_base` are tied to OpenAI's models; the vocabulary cannot be swapped under a trained model at all.

## mistral-common and vendor layers

These exist because a vendor needs control over how special tokens and requests are assembled. They move with the vendor, on the vendor's schedule. Using one is reasonable when you run that vendor's models, and unreasonable as a general tokenizer.

## Smaller engines

YouTokenToMe is archived. BlingFire has had no release since 2021. TokenDagger and rs-bpe each show a burst of work in 2025 and nothing since. The `bpe` crate in github/rust-gems is active, but the Python binding on PyPI comes from a third party with 2 stars. Treat any of them as something to vendor and test, not something to depend on unattended.

## Hosted counters

There is nothing to maintain and nothing to pin. The provider can change the count, and Anthropic's did, by about 30 percent across a model boundary (vendor: https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/token-counting, accessed 2026-11-04). Budget code should treat a count as a property of a model name rather than as a constant, and re-measure when the name changes.

# S4 Recommendation: Plan for the Vocabulary to Change

## The strategic finding

Engine risk is low across the three main engines and vocabulary risk is high. Over the versions sampled here, a model family's move changed the Chinese cost more than any engine decision could:

| Family move | Chinese before | Chinese after | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| OpenAI, cl100k to o200k | 1,314 | 890 | -32% |
| Qwen 3 to Qwen 3.8 | 799 | 728 | -9% |
| LLM-jp-3 to LLM-jp-4 | 872 | 753 | -14% |
| EXAONE 4.0 to K-EXAONE | 1,443 | 900 | -38% |
| A.X 4.0 to A.X K1 | 1,181 | 898 | -24% |
| DeepSeek V3.2 to V4.1 | 707 | Not run; inferred | 0% inferred |
| Qwen 2.5 to Qwen 3 | 799 | 799 | 0% |
| Gemma 3 to Gemma 4 | 804 | 804 | 0% |

Measured 2026-11-04; tokens per 1,000 codepoints on 29.9M codepoints of zhwiki; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/summary.json. DeepSeek V4.1 was not run on the corpus: its model parts match V3.2's and its ids hashed the same over a 3.5M-codepoint sample. Family moves do not always change the file, so check each one.

Anthropic documents a change of roughly 30 percent more tokens at the Claude 4.7 boundary, in the other direction (vendor: https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/token-counting, accessed 2026-11-04).

## What this asks for

- **Treat a token count as a property of a model name.** Store the model name next to any budget, chunk size or quota derived from a count.
- **Keep a small corpus of your own text and re-count at every model change.** It takes minutes and catches both directions.
- **Pin the file by commit, not by repo name.** Gemma 4 E4B's repo was last modified on 2026-11-03, the day before this measurement. A moving branch means a moving count.
- **Do not depend on one vendor's character ratio.** Gemini's rule of thumb of about 4 characters per token is not qualified by language (vendor: https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/tokens, accessed 2026-11-04), while Gemma's own file gave about 1.5 codepoints per token on Japanese Wikipedia text (measured 2026-11-04; tokenizers 0.23.3; 29.5M codepoints; harness/1-033-3-cjk-tokenizers/results/summary.json).

## Engine by engine

| Engine | Position on 2026-11-04 | Watch |
|---|---|---|
| Hugging Face Tokenizers | Default backend of `transformers` 5; the format most open-weight files ship in | The 1.0 rewrite, in release candidates; run the comparison on your files |

## If you are training a vocabulary

A trained file outlives the engine that built it only if another engine can read it. `tokenizer.json` and the `.model` format are both read by more than one engine (`S2-comprehensive/engine-behavior.md`), and Tekken and `.tiktoken` are simple enough to convert. Training itself is compared in `S2-comprehensive/training.md`.

## What is not settled

How much token cost should weigh against model quality is outside this survey. Output quality per tokenizer, and whether a vendor's next encoding will spend more of its budget on CJK, are both unknown. OpenAI's move from `cl100k_base` to `o200k_base` did; no one has announced the next one.
