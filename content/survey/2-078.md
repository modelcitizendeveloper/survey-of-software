# 2.078 Client-Side AI Handoff (BYOAI Patterns)

Survey of the mechanisms by which a hosted web service hands work to an AI the END USER already pays for, rather than calling a model on the service's own meter. Covers the outbound leg (vendor prefill URLs, navigator.share / OS share sheets, registerProtocolHandler, browser AI extensions) and the inbound leg (clipboard paste-back with a versioned envelope, remote MCP with OAuth, A2A agent cards). Central question: is there a standard here, or only conventions — and if the transport already exists, what is actually missing?

> [!NOTE]
> **Evidence: [desk research](/survey/method/#the-evidence-ladder).** Drawn from registries, documentation and published benchmarks, read and dated. Nothing here was measured with a published method.

# Domain Explainer: Client-Side AI Handoff

**Is this domain relevant to me?**

Read this if you build or publish anything on the web and have wondered whether your users'
own AI could do work your product cannot afford to do. This explains why that is harder than
it sounds, and what shape the difficulty has.

---

## The Hardware Store Analogy

You run a hardware store. A customer comes in with a broken cabinet hinge and asks whether you
can fix it.

**You could do the repair yourself.** Hire a carpenter, keep a workshop out back, charge for
the service. This is what most AI products do: they call a model on their own account and bill
the customer enough to cover it. It works, and it means every customer costs you money before
they pay you anything.

**Or you could notice that the customer already owns a very good workshop.** Most people
handing you a broken hinge have a garage with tools in it — often better tools than you keep
out back. They came to you for the *right part and the right instructions*, not for labor.

So you hand them the correct hinge and a card that says exactly what to do: which screws, which
order, what to watch out for. They walk home, do the job in their own workshop with their own
tools, and it costs you nothing but the card.

**That card is a client-side AI handoff.** Your users are already paying for a workshop — a
ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini subscription sitting idle while they read your page. The handoff is
how you give them the part and the instructions instead of doing the work on your meter.

Now the complication, which is the entire subject of this domain:

**The customer often needs to bring something back.** Sometimes the repair produces a
measurement, or reveals the hinge was the wrong size. If your store has no way to receive that
— no counter, no form, no way to match their answer to their original question — then you gave
them a card and lost the thread. They fixed the cabinet, and you learned nothing and can do
nothing further.

Handing something out is easy. **Getting something back is the hard part**, and most of this
domain is about that asymmetry.

---

## The Problem

Three facts, and their collision.

**Fact one: AI capability now sits with the user, not the service.** A large share of the
people reading any technical page are paying monthly for a frontier model — frequently a
better one than a small operator could afford to call on their behalf. That capacity is bought
and idle.

**Fact two: the default architecture ignores this.** The standard way to build an AI feature is
to call a model API server-side and bill for it. This makes every user a metered cost, sets a
price floor, creates a vendor dependency, and turns a traffic spike into a bill. For a small
team or a free resource, it can make the feature impossible rather than merely expensive.

**Fact three: there is no agreed way to hand work from a page to a person's own AI and get an
answer back.**

That third fact is surprising. The web has a standard for almost everything — sharing, payment,
notifications, credentials, geolocation. But the specific motion of *"take this task to
whichever assistant you use, and bring the answer back here"* has no standard covering it end to
end.

Not for lack of parts. There are more transports available than you would expect. What is
missing is agreement on **what to put in them.**

---

## Why This Is Genuinely Hard

Four difficulties, none of which is about technology.

### 1. The page does not know which AI the user has

There is no way to ask the browser "which assistant does this person use?" There is no registry,
no capability check, no default. Every mechanism either guesses a vendor, shows the user a row
of buttons, or delegates the choice to the operating system's share sheet.

This is a **coordination problem**. The web has long had a way for an application to claim a
category of link — the mechanism that lets a webmail site claim `mailto:`. It works. No AI
vendor has claimed a shared category for AI tasks, because each would rather you send users to
*their* product specifically. A decade of that mechanism's availability has produced an empty
namespace.

### 2. Sending is easy; receiving is not

A page controls exactly what it hands out. It controls nothing about what comes back.

The answer arrives having passed through a model the page did not configure, in a client the
page cannot see, possibly edited by a human along the way. Models wrap output in prose, add
explanations, apologise, and reformat. A page expecting an exact format will be disappointed
constantly.

So the receiving side cannot be built like a normal parser. It has to **recognize and extract**
rather than validate and reject — closer to finding a phone number in a paragraph than to
reading a data file.

### 3. Convenience and safety pull against each other

If a page can compose text that lands in your AI, a *malicious* page can too. The mechanisms
that make handoff smooth are the same ones that let an untrusted page put words in your
assistant's mouth.

The industry's answer so far is to **keep a human in the loop**: staging the text so you read
it before it runs, warning you when it came from an external link, escalating the warning when
the text is too long to review at a glance. Those steps look like friction. They are also the
safety mechanism — and every attempt to automate them has had to invent a replacement.

### 4. Anonymous users cannot be authenticated, by definition

The robust way to connect a service to an AI is a proper authorized connection — the same
model as "sign in with Google." It gives structured results, no size limits, and revocable
access.

It also requires the user to *make a decision* before anything happens. That is fine for
someone who uses your product daily. It is impossible for someone who arrived from a search
result and will leave in ninety seconds — and asking is worse than not offering, because you
have demanded a security decision before showing any value.

**These are different customers needing different mechanisms**, and that is the domain's
central structural fact.

---

## The Broad Approaches

Four families, in rough order of how much they ask of the user.

**Carry it by hand.** The page prepares the task, the user copies it, pastes it into whatever
AI they use, and pastes the result back. Universally available, works with any AI including
local ones, no size limit, no setup. Manual, and every implementation invents its own format.

**Send it by link.** A link that opens the user's AI with the task already staged. One click,
but the page must guess the vendor, the amount of text a link can carry is limited, many
platforms silently strip these links, and there is no return path.

**Let the operating system route it.** The share sheet — the menu that appears when you share a
photo. It solves "which AI does this person use" by asking them. Standardized and well
integrated on phones, weak on desktop, and strictly one-way with no result.

**Connect the service to the AI properly.** An authorized connection the user sets up once,
after which their AI can work with the service directly. Structured, unlimited, revocable — and
requires that setup step, so it cannot serve someone arriving for the first time.

A fifth is emerging: **the AI comes to the page.** Browser-attached assistants read what you
are looking at, so no handoff is needed at all. This changes the picture where it applies —
but only for users who have already opted in, and the page has no way to detect or invoke it.

---

## The Trade-Off That Organizes Everything

Every mechanism trades **setup cost** against **structure**.

At one end: no setup, no structure. Anyone can use it immediately; the page gets back
free-form text it must interpret, if anything comes back at all.

At the other: real setup, real structure. The AI works with the service directly and returns
data the service can act on; the user had to authorize it first.

**Nothing occupies the middle**, and not for lack of trying. The setup cost is not
inefficiency — it is a person deciding to grant access, which is what makes the connection
trustworthy. Removing it would remove the property that made it worth having.

The practical consequence is that **mechanisms are stages, not alternatives.** A stranger gets
the manual path. A returning user gets a link. A committed user connects properly. A product
that offers only the last one is asking strangers for permissions they will refuse; one that
offers only the first never gives loyal users anything better.

---

## When This Domain Is Relevant

**Strongly relevant if:**

- Your product would benefit from AI work you cannot afford to pay for per user.
- Your users are technical or professional — populations with high rates of paid AI
  subscriptions.
- You publish material readers need to apply to their own situation.
- Your users' data cannot go to a third party, but *they* can take it to an assistant they
  control.
- You are a small operator who needs marginal cost per user to stay at zero.

**Not relevant if:**

- Your AI feature is core to your product and your pricing supports paying for it. Then run it
  yourself — you get control, consistency, and quality guarantees no handoff can offer.
- Your users are non-technical consumers unlikely to have their own AI. The handoff routes to
  something that is not there.
- The work needs to happen automatically, in the background, without a person present. Most of
  this domain requires a human in the loop, by design and sometimes by requirement.
- The task is small enough for a model built into the browser. That is a different domain and
  a simpler one.

---

## What to Take Away

**The transports are largely solved. The agreement about what to put in them is not.**

The web already has: a standard for routing to a user's chosen destination, a standard for
claiming a category of link, a draft for pages to describe their capabilities to an agent, and
a mature protocol for authorized connections between services and AI clients. Several of these
are real specifications from real standards bodies.

What none of them says is **what a task and its answer should look like as text a person can
carry from a page to an assistant and back.** That is a small, specific gap in an otherwise
well-equipped area — and understanding that the gap is *there*, rather than in the plumbing, is
the main thing to take from this domain.

If you build here, build the layer you control first, expect to keep it, and treat everything
that depends on a vendor's future decision as a bonus.

# S1 Approach — Client-Side AI Handoff (BYOAI Patterns)

**Survey**: 2.078
**Pass**: S1 Rapid Discovery
**Date**: 2026-08-04
**All claims verified**: 2026-08-04 unless otherwise noted

---

## The Category

This survey covers the mechanisms by which **a web page hands a unit of work to an AI
that the end user already has** — their own subscription, their own browser agent, their
own local model — instead of the page's operator calling a model on their own meter.

Two legs define the category:

- **Outbound** — getting a prepared task *out of the page* and *into* the user's AI.
- **Inbound** — getting the AI's answer *back into the page* in a form the page can act on.

A third concern cuts across both:

- **Discovery** — how a page advertises that it supports this at all, and how an AI
  client learns what the page can accept.

A mechanism that solves only one leg is not a complete handoff. Much of the confusion in
this space comes from comparing an outbound-only mechanism (a prefill link) against a
bidirectional one (a remote MCP server) as though they were substitutes.

---

## The Central Question

The `metadata.yaml` for this slot records `qualifies_as_2xxx: undetermined`, on purpose.
Every other survey in the 2.07x neighborhood (gRPC, GraphQL, WebSocket, MCP, A2A) documents
a governed standard with a spec, a version, and an owner. This slot may not have one.

**S1 must answer: is there a governed standard for page-to-user's-own-AI handoff, or only
vendor conventions?**

"There is no standard here, only conventions" is a legitimate and publishable finding. The
failure mode to avoid is promoting a convention into a standard because the slot number
implies one should exist. Where this survey finds a convention, it says convention. Where it
finds a draft, it says draft — and names the body, the status, and the implementation reality.

---

## Method

Mechanisms were selected by walking both legs of the handoff and asking, for each, *what
could carry this today*. That produced eight candidates, each profiled as a peer in its own
file regardless of how mature or popular it is:

| File | Mechanism | Leg |
|------|-----------|-----|
| `vendor-prefill-urls.md` | Prefill URLs and custom URL schemes | Outbound |
| `web-share-target.md` | Web Share API / Web Share Target | Outbound |
| `register-protocol-handler.md` | `registerProtocolHandler()` and `web+` schemes | Outbound |
| `browser-ai-agents.md` | Browser extensions and agentic browsers | Both (ambient) |
| `webmcp.md` | WebMCP / `document.modelContext` | Inbound-agent |
| `remote-mcp-oauth.md` | Remote MCP servers with OAuth 2.1 | Both (structured) |
| `clipboard-envelope.md` | Clipboard copy-out / paste-back envelopes | Both (manual) |
| `discovery-conventions.md` | `llms.txt`, `.well-known`, schema.org, agent cards | Discovery |

Each mechanism is judged on **governance** (who owns the spec, what status), **direction**
(which leg it actually serves), **implementation reality** (what ships, in which browsers or
clients), and **friction cost to the user** (what the user must install, connect, or approve).

Popularity is reported, not rewarded. A mechanism with a real spec and no consuming clients
is a different kind of bet than a mechanism with no spec and universal support, and S1 marks
that difference rather than collapsing it into a single ranking.

---

## Scope Boundaries

**In scope**: mechanisms where the *user's* AI does the work and the *user's* account pays.

**Out of scope**:

- Server-side model selection and API vendor comparison — that is 3.200's category. The
  distinction that matters here is *whose meter runs*, not *which model is better*.
- Agent-to-agent orchestration among servers the page operator controls — 2.075 (A2A) and
  2.083 (AI Agent Gateways). Those move work between machines that already trust each other;
  this category crosses a trust and billing boundary into the user's own client.
- Prompt content quality. This survey covers the *envelope*, not what goes in it.
- On-device model APIs that run a browser-bundled model (Chrome's built-in Prompt API and
  equivalents). These are adjacent and worth an explicit note in S2, but the model there is
  the *browser vendor's*, not one the user chose and pays for — a different trust and
  capability profile from the category defined above.

---

## A Terminology Warning

**"BYOAI" is already taken.** In 2026 the term overwhelmingly denotes an enterprise
security topic: employees using personal ChatGPT/Claude/Gemini accounts for work outside IT
governance — the successor framing to BYOD, discussed in terms of shadow IT, data leakage,
and acceptable-use policy. A second, weaker sense appears in vendor marketing: letting a
customer plug their own model or API key into a SaaS product ("bring your own key").

Neither sense is the mechanism category surveyed here. Searching "BYOAI" returns governance
and risk-management material, not handoff transports. This matters for two reasons:

1. **Prior-art searches under "BYOAI" will return false negatives.** Anyone concluding
   "nobody has worked on this" from a BYOAI search has searched the wrong term. The
   relevant prior art lives under *WebMCP*, *share targets*, *deep links*, *protocol
   handlers*, and *MCP*.
2. **Any public naming of this pattern inherits the collision.** A talk or spec that calls
   this "BYOAI" will be heard by enterprise audiences as a shadow-IT talk.

S1 therefore uses "client-side AI handoff" throughout and treats "BYOAI" as a keyword for
findability only.

# Browser AI Extensions and Agentic Browsers

**Leg**: Both — but ambient, not invoked by the page
**Governance**: None — vendor products
**Status**: Crowded, well funded, and visibly consolidating

---

## What It Is

An AI attached to the browser itself rather than to a tab: an extension or a purpose-built
browser that can read the current page, act on it, and carry context across tabs. An agentic
browser "does not just summarize a webpage; it reads the page, decides the next click,
scrolls, fills a form, opens a new tab, compares prices, and reports back with a result."

For this category, this class matters because it makes the handoff **disappear**. If the
user's AI is already looking at the page, no transport is needed — no link, no clipboard, no
share sheet. The user says "do this with what's on screen" and the agent reads it directly.

## The 2026 Landscape

- **Claude for Chrome** — an extension offering multi-tab automation, connected to Claude
  Code and Cowork. Browser-and-OS control is reported to sit behind Claude Pro accounts,
  i.e. the user's existing subscription pays.
- **Gemini in Chrome** — native Chrome integration with multi-tab context and Google app
  workflows. At Google I/O 2026 Google announced a sweeping Gemini integration across Chrome,
  positioning it as an agentic platform for multi-step tasks. Google has also said Gemini in
  Chrome will be the first agent to consume WebMCP tools (see `webmcp.md`).
- **Perplexity Comet** — reported to offer the strongest autonomous experience where changing
  browsers is acceptable. Perplexity removed Comet's paywall in late 2025; as of mid-2026 it
  is free on macOS, Windows, Android, and iOS with no subscription for core features.
- **OpenAI Atlas** — **discontinued**. OpenAI announced on 9 July 2026 that it is retiring
  the standalone ChatGPT Atlas browser, nine months after its October 2025 launch. **Atlas
  stops working on 9 August 2026.**

The Atlas retirement is the most instructive data point in this profile. A well-resourced
vendor shipped a dedicated agentic browser, ran it for nine months, and withdrew it. Whatever
the internal reasoning, the market signal is that *owning the browser* was not the winning
position — and the survivors are split between an extension on someone else's browser
(Anthropic), the incumbent's own browser (Google), and a free challenger (Perplexity).

## Why This Is Not a Transport

Everything in this class is a **product, not a mechanism**. There is no specification, no
interoperability surface, and nothing a page author can target. A page cannot detect that an
agentic browser is present, cannot invoke it, cannot hand it a structured task, and cannot
receive a result from it.

The page's only lever is **being legible**: clean semantic markup, readable text, and — for
the one standardized affordance in this area — WebMCP tools, which are explicitly designed
for this consumer. As of 2026-09-07 ChatGPT Desktop and Brave's Leo do call them; in
August 2026 nothing did. See `webmcp.md`.

The handoff here is initiated by the **user**, not the page. That places this class outside
the outbound leg entirely, however well it serves the underlying user need.

## The Dependency Nobody Controls

Whether this class eventually absorbs the whole category depends on a decision no page author
influences: **do agentic browsers become the default way people browse, or do they stay a
power-user tool?**

Two forces cut against default status. Agentic browsing raises real security concerns — an
agent reading and acting on arbitrary page content is a prompt-injection surface by
construction, which is the risk WebMCP's browser-mediated model and
`requestUserInteraction()` are designed to contain. And the Atlas withdrawal suggests
appetite for a *separate* agentic browser is thinner than 2025 assumed.

The extension form (Claude for Chrome) and the incumbent-integration form (Gemini in Chrome)
both avoid asking the user to switch browsers, which is likely why they survived.

## Trade-offs

**For it**: zero handoff friction — nothing to copy, click, or connect; the user's own
subscription pays; works on any page including ones with no AI affordance at all; carries
context across tabs; already shipping and free to try.

**Against it**: not a mechanism a page can target; no spec, no detection, no invocation, no
return path; the page cannot participate beyond being readable; vendor products subject to
withdrawal on short notice, as Atlas demonstrates; prompt-injection exposure; user must have
installed and enabled it; page-scraping accuracy remains the failure mode WebMCP exists to
fix.

## Category Position

This class is the **substitute good** for everything else in this survey, and its trajectory
is the main uncertainty in the category's five-year outlook. If agent-attached browsing
becomes ordinary, the outbound leg loses most of its motivation — the page stops needing to
push work anywhere because the AI is already there.

But that future arrives, if at all, through WebMCP-shaped interfaces rather than through any
of these products directly, and the WebMCP consumer gap means it has not started. For now,
this class serves the user who has already opted in, and serves the page not at all.

# Clipboard Handoff and Paste-Back Envelopes

**Leg**: Both — the only mechanism that closes the loop with no setup
**Governance**: **None.** No spec, no draft, no proposal found
**Status**: Universally supported transport; entirely unspecified payload

---

## What It Is

The page renders a prepared task and a copy control. The user copies, switches to whichever
AI they use, pastes, reads the answer, copies the result, returns to the page, and pastes it
back. The page parses what arrives.

Two independent halves, and they are not equally hard:

**Copy-out** is trivial and completely solved. The Async Clipboard API is standardized and
universally supported, permission-gated behind a user gesture, and the page controls the
exact bytes.

**Paste-back** is where the actual design problem lives. The page must accept text produced
by a model it did not prompt, in a client it does not control, and decide whether it is
usable — and it must do this without the model having been told what shape to produce, unless
the copy-out payload said so.

## Governance — the Genuine Void

This is the clearest finding in the survey. **No standards body, working group, community
group, or vendor has published a convention for structuring an AI handoff payload or its
return envelope.** Searches for a markup or envelope convention return generic AI-button UI
components and SEO schema advice, not a proposal.

Compare the neighbors: prefill URLs are ungoverned but at least *documented per vendor*. Web
Share is a W3C standard. `registerProtocolHandler` is in the HTML Standard. WebMCP is a W3C
Community Group draft. MCP has dated revisions and a SEP process. The clipboard lane has
**nothing at any level** — which is notable given it is the lane most widely used in
practice, because it is the fallback whenever a deep link is stripped, a share sheet is
absent, or a user has not connected anything.

## What an Envelope Would Need

Nothing here is invented by this survey; these are the requirements any working
implementation must satisfy, and the fact that they are *stable and small* is itself a
finding:

- **A version marker**, so a page can reject or migrate payloads from an older format.
- **A task/kind discriminator**, so the page knows what it is receiving.
- **A correlation identifier**, so a returned answer can be matched to the request that
  produced it — the thing Web Share and prefill URLs structurally cannot provide.
- **Tolerant parsing.** This is the hard requirement. Models wrap output in prose, add code
  fences, apologise, and reformat. A parser that demands exact bytes will fail constantly, so
  the envelope must be recognisable inside surrounding chatter and the parser must extract
  rather than validate-or-reject.
- **A human-legible surface.** Unlike every other mechanism here, the payload passes through
  a human's screen twice. An envelope that looks like machine noise will be edited, truncated,
  or distrusted by the person carrying it.

The last two constraints are what make this different from ordinary serialization
and why borrowing an existing format does not settle the question. JSON in a fenced block is
the obvious candidate and it is *fine*; the unsolved part is the recognition and extraction
discipline around it, not the encoding.

## Why It Survives Despite Being the Worst-Looking Option

It is easy to dismiss clipboard handoff as a stopgap. The evidence in this survey says
otherwise, because it is the **only** mechanism with all four of these properties:

1. **Vendor-neutral.** Works with any AI, including ones that did not exist when the page was
   written, including local models, including a colleague reading the text.
2. **Zero setup.** No install, no OAuth, no connection, no permission beyond the copy gesture.
   It is the only mechanism that works for an anonymous first-time visitor *and* returns a
   result.
3. **No payload ceiling.** Not bounded by URL length, which is the hard wall on every
   scheme-based mechanism in this survey.
4. **Universally available.** It is the documented fallback when custom schemes are stripped
   by renderers, and it is unaffected by browser engine support, origin trials, or spec churn.

Every alternative gives up at least one of these. Prefill URLs give up the return leg and the
payload ceiling. Web Share gives up the return leg entirely. MCP gives up zero-setup. WebMCP
gives up every visitor without a consuming agent — currently almost all of them.

## Trade-offs

**For it**: universal; vendor-neutral; no setup; no size limit; closes both legs; degrades
gracefully; the user sees exactly what is sent and what returns, which is a real consent and
auditability property as well as a limitation.

**Against it**: manual — several user actions per round trip, and the user can lose the
thread at any step; no automation and no background operation; return parsing is
best-effort against unconstrained model output; no correlation unless the envelope supplies
it; feels unpolished next to a one-click integration; and it is entirely unspecified, so
every implementation invents its own format and none interoperate.

## Category Position

Clipboard handoff is the **floor of the category** — the thing that works when nothing else
does — and simultaneously the **only lane with no governance whatsoever**.

That combination is the most defensible answer to the survey's "what is actually missing"
question. The transports are largely solved and standardized; what is unspecified is the
**payload convention** that would let a page and an arbitrary AI agree on what a task and its
result look like as text a human can carry. That is a small, tractable specification — closer
in size to a microformat than to a protocol — and nobody has written it.

Whether it *should* be written, and by whom, is an S4 question. S1's finding is only that it
does not exist.

# Discovery Conventions — `llms.txt`, `.well-known`, schema.org, Agent Cards

**Leg**: Discovery — how a page advertises what it offers an AI
**Governance**: Mixed — from none (`llms.txt`) to fully governed (schema.org, A2A)
**Status**: Several live conventions; none designed for handoff

---

## Why Discovery Is a Separate Problem

Every transport in this survey assumes the two ends already know about each other. Discovery
is the prior question: **how does an AI client learn that this page supports a handoff, and
what shape it accepts?**

Four conventions bear on it, with sharply different governance and sharply different
evidence of working.

---

## `llms.txt`

A plain-text file at a site's root offering an AI-oriented map of the site's content, by
analogy with `robots.txt`.

**Governance: none.** It is an individual proposal that gained community traction. No
standards body has chartered it, and there is no normative specification.

**Adoption**, as of mid-2026, is real but modest: **8.7% of the top 1,000 websites** publish
one (June 2026); another study puts adoption at **10.13%**.

**Evidence of effect is poor, and this is the important part.** SE Ranking trained an XGBoost
model to test whether `llms.txt` presence correlates with AI citation frequency and found
that **removing the `llms.txt` variable improved the model's prediction accuracy** — the file
added noise rather than signal. A separate analysis found the file present on **fewer than 1%
of 120 sites that were actually cited** in AI search results. The mid-2026 position is
that the proposal is technically coherent but practically unproven for the use case its
advocates care about, following a familiar hype curve from mid-2024 enthusiasm to
disappointing data.

**Relevance here is limited anyway.** `llms.txt` describes *content for crawling*, not
*capabilities for invoking*. It is an SEO-adjacent artifact aimed at retrieval and citation.
Nothing in it advertises "this page can hand you a task." Its main value to this survey is as
a cautionary case: a low-cost, plausible convention with visible adoption and no measurable
effect.

---

## schema.org Actions and `potentialAction`

The strongest *governed* prior art for declaring machine-callable capability on a page.

schema.org's Actions vocabulary and the `potentialAction` property let a page declare, in
structured data, which actions are possible on a resource — buy, book, reserve, subscribe,
contact — each with a `target` naming the URL that handles the action. `potentialAction`
"indicates a potential Action, which describes an idealized action in which this thing would
play an 'object' role."

**Governance**: schema.org is a long-established, multi-search-engine-backed vocabulary with
a public extension process — governed, and vastly more mature than anything
AI-specific.

**Relevance is direct and underappreciated.** The described current behavior of agents
encountering `potentialAction` is that they "call the target URL and route the user there
with the relevant context pre-filled," with the user completing the final step — which is
*the outbound handoff shape this survey is about*, expressed in a vocabulary that
already exists. The stated trajectory is that agents will eventually call the target
programmatically and complete the action without the user.

Anyone designing a markup convention to advertise a handoff affordance must reckon with
`potentialAction` first. The vocabulary is extensible, adopted, and already understood by the
crawlers that feed AI systems. Inventing a parallel vocabulary would require justifying why
an extension would not do.

**Limits**: `potentialAction` declares that an action exists at a URL. It does not describe
prompt payloads, does not model a return leg, and does not specify how an answer comes back.
It solves advertisement, not exchange.

---

## `.well-known` and A2A Agent Cards

The `.well-known` URI convention (IETF RFC 8615) is the web's standard place to put
machine-discoverable metadata, and it is the pattern the AI ecosystem has converged on.

**A2A agent cards** are the fully worked example: JSON metadata published at
`/.well-known/agent.json` describing an agent's identity, skills, capabilities, and accepted
authentication schemes. A2A is governed by the Linux Foundation's **Agentic AI Foundation**,
reached v1.0 in early 2026 with **signed agent cards** — cryptographic proof that a card was
issued by the domain owner — and is at v1.2 with 150+ organizations reported in production.
Full treatment is in survey 2.075.

**MCP** also uses `.well-known`, though narrowly: the 2026-07-28 revision adds OpenID Connect
discovery for authorization servers (SEP-2351). A broader "Server Cards" capability-discovery
mechanism was claimed by a secondary source but **could not be confirmed** against the
official release post — see `remote-mcp-oauth.md`.

**Relevance**: agent cards prove the discovery half is a solved design problem with a working
governed instance, including the identity and trust properties (signing) that any handoff
convention would eventually need. What A2A discovers, however, is *another agent* — a peer
service that speaks A2A. It does not discover *a page's affordance for the human's own AI*,
and it presumes both ends are agents. The pattern transfers; the artifact does not.

---

## What Discovery Tells Us About the Category

The four conventions divide cleanly:

| Convention | Governance | Discovers | Evidence of working |
|---|---|---|---|
| `llms.txt` | None | Content to read | Poor — measured as noise |
| schema.org `potentialAction` | Strong (schema.org) | Actions at a URL | Established, agent-relevant |
| A2A agent cards | Strong (Linux Foundation) | Peer agents | Strong, 150+ orgs |
| MCP `.well-known` | Moderate (SEP process) | Authorization servers | Live, narrow scope |

The pattern is consistent with the transports: **the web already has governed machinery for
advertising machine-callable capability, and none of it was designed for a page handing work
to the human's own assistant.** The gap is not tooling. It is that no one has defined the
*semantics* of this particular exchange in any of the several vocabularies that could
comfortably carry it.

# S1 Synthesis and Verdict

**Survey**: 2.078 — Client-Side AI Handoff
**Pass**: S1 Rapid Discovery
**Verified**: 2026-08-04

---

## The Answer to the Central Question

**There is no governed standard for handing a task from a web page to the end user's own AI
and getting the answer back.**

But the far more useful finding is *why*, and it is not the answer the slot was set up to
expect. The gap is **not** an absent transport. The web has an unusual surplus of them:

| Mechanism | Governance | Outbound | Return leg | Setup required |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vendor prefill URLs / custom schemes | None (per-vendor) | ✅ | ❌ | Client installed |
| Web Share / Share Target | **W3C standard** | ✅ | ❌ | Installed target |
| `registerProtocolHandler` + `web+` | **WHATWG HTML Standard** | ✅ | ❌ | Vendor must register |
| Browser AI agents | None (products) | n/a — user-initiated | n/a | Extension/browser |
| WebMCP `document.modelContext` | W3C CG draft (non-normative) | ❌ wrong direction | ✅ | Consuming agent |
| Remote MCP + OAuth 2.1 | MCP SEP process (dated revs) | ✅ | ✅ | **OAuth connect** |
| Clipboard envelope | **None at any level** | ✅ | ✅ | **None** |

Read down the governance column: three standardized mechanisms, one credible draft,
one well-run vendor-originated spec. Read down the last two columns: the only mechanism that
closes both legs with **zero setup** is the one with **no specification of any kind**.

That is the finding. **The transports are solved and largely standardized; the payload
semantics are unspecified.** Nobody has defined what a task and its result look like as text
a human can carry between a page and an arbitrary AI.

## Verdict on `qualifies_as_2xxx`

**No — and the slot should say so.**

This category does not contain a governed standard the way 2.071 (gRPC), 2.072 (GraphQL),
2.074 (MCP), or 2.075 (A2A) do. It is a **convention landscape**: several real standards
being used off-label, several vendor conventions with no coordination, and one genuine void.

The classification that fits is a **survey of a category with no standard at its center**, which
is a legitimate 2.xxx entry provided it is labeled as such. `metadata.yaml` should be
updated from `undetermined` to record this explicitly, with the caveat that WebMCP could
change the answer for the *inbound* direction if consuming clients ever appear.

## Prior Art — the Claim That Must Not Be Made

The metadata for this slot notes that establishing prior art matters before any public claim
that nobody has defined this. S1's answer is unambiguous:

**Do not claim the space is unexamined.** As of August 2026 there is substantial, named,
multi-vendor standards activity on page-to-AI communication:

- **WebMCP** — W3C Web Machine Learning Community Group draft, announced 10 February 2026,
  authored by Microsoft and Google with named editors, shipping in a Chrome origin trial
  (149–156), with Firefox and Safari engaged in the discussion.
- **MCP 2026-07-28** — the largest revision since launch, with a stateless core, MCP Apps,
  Tasks, and six authorization-hardening SEPs.
- **A2A** — Linux Foundation governance, signed agent cards, v1.2, 150+ organizations
  reported in production.
- **schema.org `potentialAction`** — an established governed vocabulary whose current agent
  behavior is *exactly* the pre-filled outbound handoff shape.

A claim that "nobody has defined this" is defensible **only** in one narrow, specific form:
*no one has specified the payload convention for a text-carried task and its return envelope
across arbitrary AI clients.* That claim survives the searches run in this pass. The broad
version does not survive contact with WebMCP.

Note also the **terminology trap** documented in `approach.md`: "BYOAI" in 2026 means
enterprise shadow-IT governance, not handoff transports. A prior-art search under that term
returns false negatives, and any public naming inherits the collision.

## What Is Actually Missing

Three gaps, in descending order of how tractable they are:

1. **A payload/envelope convention (unclaimed).** Version marker, kind
   discriminator, correlation ID, tolerant parsing, human-legible surface. This is
   microformat-sized, not protocol-sized. It has no owner, no draft, and no competing
   proposal. It would immediately improve the clipboard lane *and* give Web Share's `text`
   field something meaningful to carry — turning a solved-but-semantically-empty W3C
   transport into a usable handoff.

2. **A destination registry with an incentive behind it.** The share sheet solves "which AI
   does this user use" on mobile; `registerProtocolHandler` could solve it on desktop and
   sits unused because no vendor claims a shared scheme. This is a **coordination problem,
   not a technical one**, and no specification fixes it.

3. **A correlation mechanism for one-way transports.** Prefill URLs and Web Share both hand
   work off with no way to learn the outcome. An envelope with a correlation ID (gap 1) is
   the cheapest available patch, since the human carries the ID back by pasting.

## Mechanism Selection by Situation

Category-neutral guidance; specific personas are S3's job.

- **Anonymous first-time visitor, must work today** → clipboard handoff, with prefill links
  as a per-vendor accelerator where the vendor is known. Nothing else functions without setup.
- **Payload above a few thousand characters** → clipboard. Every scheme-based mechanism hits
  the URL wall.
- **Committed, authenticated, returning user** → remote MCP with OAuth 2.1. The connect step
  is affordable exactly once the relationship justifies it.
- **Machine-readable results required** → remote MCP. Nothing else returns typed data.
- **Mobile, and the destination should be the user's choice** → Web Share, accepting that no
  result comes back.
- **Agent-attached visitors** → WebMCP tools, understood as a forward-looking bet with no
  consumers yet, not a working feature.

The most robust posture is **layered**: clipboard as the floor that always works, prefill
links as the fast path for known vendors, MCP as the upgrade for committed users, WebMCP as a
cheap option on a future that may not arrive.

## Confidence and Volatility

**High confidence**: WebMCP's status, governance, and consumer gap; MCP 2026-07-28 contents;
Anthropic deep-link parameters and limits; `llms.txt` adoption and weak effect evidence;
Atlas retirement (9 July 2026 announcement, service ends 9 August 2026); the absence of any
payload-envelope specification.

**Low confidence, flagged in place**: ChatGPT and Gemini prefill parameters and ceilings
(secondary sources only); whether `claude.ai/new?q=` still works on the web; MCP "Server
Cards" (**unconfirmed** — contradicted by the official release post); which AI vendors
register as OS share targets.

**Volatility warning.** This category is moving faster than any other 2.xxx neighbor. Within
the six months before this pass: WebMCP was announced, shipped, renamed, and partially
deprecated; MCP landed its largest-ever revision; and a major vendor launched and killed an
agentic browser. **A six-month refresh interval is too long here — this survey should be
flagged S-tier on the next freshness sweep regardless of age.**

## S2 Priorities

1. Verify every vendor prefill parameter against primary documentation; resolve the
   `claude.ai/new?q=` question and the ChatGPT/Gemini submit semantics — auto-submit versus
   prefill is a user-safety difference, not a detail.
2. Confirm or kill the MCP "Server Cards" claim against the final 2026-07-28 specification.
3. Determine which AI clients register as OS share targets, and by which platform mechanism.
4. Assess whether schema.org `potentialAction` could carry a handoff affordance as an
   extension, versus a new vocabulary — gap 1's cheapest possible form.
5. Examine on-device browser model APIs (Chrome's built-in Prompt API and equivalents) as the
   adjacent case excluded from S1 scope by design.
6. Track whether any mainstream agent client has begun consuming `document.modelContext`.
   This single fact determines the category's shape more than any other.

# `registerProtocolHandler()` and `web+` Schemes

**Leg**: Outbound — routing, not payload
**Governance**: WHATWG HTML Standard (a living standard, normatively specified)
**Status**: Long-standing; **not Baseline**; uneven engine support; little used

---

## What It Is

`navigator.registerProtocolHandler()` lets a site register itself as the handler for a URL
scheme. Once registered and approved by the user, links in that scheme open at the
registering site. The canonical uses are a webmail site claiming `mailto:` and a VoIP site
claiming `tel:`.

Custom schemes must carry a **`web+` prefix** (`web+ai:`, `web+task:`), which is the spec's
mechanism for keeping site-defined schemes out of the IANA-registered namespace.

For this category the appeal is narrow and clear: it would let a **web-based** AI client claim a
scheme, so that `web+ai:` links resolve to whichever AI the user chose — the same
destination-discovery win the share sheet offers, but working from an ordinary anchor on
desktop, with no installed native app and no OS involvement.

## Governance

Standardized in the WHATWG HTML Standard — normative prose, defined algorithms, a
registration ceremony, and security requirements. On paper this is better governance than any
AI-specific mechanism in this survey.

It is also, in practice, the least exercised. Standardisation and adoption have diverged
sharply here, and the divergence is old.

## Implementation Reality

- **Secure context only** — HTTPS required.
- **Chrome** supports it across current versions.
- **Not Baseline** — it "does not work in some of the most widely-used browsers." Safari does
  not support it, which caps the mechanism's reach at a fraction of desktop users and
  effectively all of iOS.
- **Edge** exposes enterprise policy (`RegisteredProtocolHandlers`) for administering handler
  registration, which is a signal that managed deployments are the realistic use case rather
  than the open web.

The mechanism has been available for well over a decade and has been the subject of
deprecation discussion since at least 2012 without being removed. It is stable, not growing.

## The Registration Problem

The decisive constraint is **who registers**, and it is not the page that wants the handoff.

`registerProtocolHandler()` is called by the *destination* — the AI client — to claim a
scheme. A page emitting `web+ai:` links is betting that some AI vendor has already called
that method in the user's browser. No vendor has any incentive to claim a generic scheme it
does not control, and no vendor has done so.

That is a **coordination problem, not a technical one**, and it is the same problem in a
different shape as vendor prefill URLs. Prefill URLs fail because every vendor picks its own
parameter; protocol handlers fail because no vendor picks a shared scheme at all. The
transport is standardized and the namespace is empty.

Registration is additionally gated behind a user permission prompt — appropriate, but another
step in a mechanism that has no users to lose.

## Payload Limits

A registered handler receives the full URL, so payload sits under the same practical URL
length constraints described in `vendor-prefill-urls.md`: a few thousand characters before
middleboxes and renderers start truncating. No advantage over prefill links on capacity.

And like every scheme-based approach, `web+` links are **stripped by renderers that allow
only `http`/`https`** — the same GitHub-style limitation that constrains custom-scheme deep
links generally.

## Trade-offs

**For it**: real WHATWG standardisation; solves destination discovery on desktop without the
OS; user-controlled and permission-gated; works from a plain anchor; the `web+` prefix keeps
the namespace tidy; enterprise-administrable.

**Against it**: not Baseline, no Safari; registration must come from AI vendors who have no
reason to do it; empty namespace after a decade of availability; same payload ceiling as
prefill URLs; still outbound-only with no return leg; stripped by strict renderers; long
history of deprecation discussion.

## Category Position

This is the survey's clearest example of **a standard that exists and did not get used**.
It is documented here for that reason: anyone proposing a new scheme-based
convention for AI handoff is proposing something the web platform already supports and that
nobody adopted.

The lesson generalises. The gap in this category is repeatedly **not the absence of a
transport** but the absence of anyone with an incentive to be the destination. A new spec
does not fix that; a vendor deciding to claim the namespace would.

# Remote MCP Servers with OAuth 2.1

**Leg**: Both — the only fully bidirectional structured mechanism in the category
**Governance**: Model Context Protocol working group (Anthropic-originated, SEP process)
**Status**: Mature and shipping; spec revision **2026-07-28** is the largest since launch

---

## What It Is

Instead of the page pushing a task out to the AI, the page (or its operator) runs an **MCP
server** that the user's AI client connects to once. Thereafter the AI can read from and
write to the service directly, on the user's behalf, with the user's authorisation.

The handoff inverts: rather than moving a prompt to the AI, you give the AI a durable,
authenticated door into the service. Work flows both ways for as long as the connection
lasts.

## Governance

MCP is the most substantively governed mechanism in this survey, though not by a neutral
standards body. It originated at Anthropic (launched November 2024) and evolves through a
public **SEP** (Specification Enhancement Proposal) process with a working group and dated
spec revisions.

The current revision is **2026-07-28**, with the release candidate locked 21 May 2026 — the
**largest revision since MCP launched**. Dated revisions, numbered enhancement proposals, and
a public RC period put this closer to real standards practice than anything else in the
category, while falling short of W3C/IETF-style multi-stakeholder governance.

## What Changed in 2026-07-28

**Stateless core.** The `initialize`/`initialized` handshake and the `Mcp-Session-Id` header
are gone. Servers handle requests independently, with `Mcp-Method` and `Mcp-Name` as required
headers and the protocol version and client capabilities carried in `_meta` rather than in a
header. Any request can land on any server instance, so sticky
routing and shared session stores are no longer required. A server that previously needed
sticky sessions, a shared session store, and deep packet inspection at the gateway "can now
run behind a plain round-robin load balancer."

For this survey that is an **operating-cost** change, and a large one: it removes the main
reason hosting an MCP endpoint was heavier than hosting a normal API.

**MCP Apps** (SEP-1865). Servers can ship interactive HTML interfaces that hosts render in a
sandboxed iframe. UIs are declared ahead of time so they can be cached and security-reviewed,
and they communicate back over the same JSON-RPC protocol as direct tool calls. This puts
*service-rendered UI inside the AI client* — the mirror image of putting AI inside the page,
and directly relevant to what "getting the answer back" can mean.

**Tasks.** Long-running work graduated from experimental core to an extension. `tools/call`
returns a task handle; clients drive progress with `tasks/get`, `tasks/update`, and
`tasks/cancel`. `tasks/list` was removed. This matters for any handoff whose unit of work
outlives a single request.

## Authorisation — the Friction, and the Hardening

Since the **November 2025** revision, any MCP server reachable over the internet **must
implement OAuth 2.1 with PKCE** (S256) — stated without exception. The 2026-07-28 revision
hardened this further across six SEPs, including:

- **SEP-2468** — clients must validate the `iss` parameter per RFC 9207.
- **SEP-837** — clients declare an OpenID Connect `application_type` during Dynamic Client
  Registration.
- **SEP-2352** — credentials bind to the issuing authorization server's `issuer`.
- **SEP-2351** — OpenID Connect discovery for authorization servers via `.well-known`.

Managed providers now cover the full surface — Dynamic Client Registration, PKCE, Resource
Indicators, Protected Resource Metadata — so that connecting a server can reduce to a single
Google or GitHub login with no JSON configuration.

**This is the mechanism's decisive trade-off.** OAuth is what makes the return leg
trustworthy, and it is also the reason the mechanism cannot serve a first-time anonymous
visitor. Somebody must connect the server to their client, once and on purpose, before
anything works. However smooth vendors make that step, it is a step — and it sits before any
value is delivered, which is the worst possible position for a conversion funnel.

A page cannot present a remote MCP server to a passing reader the way it presents a link.

**Unverified claim, flagged:** a secondary source described "Server Cards" for capability
discovery via a `.well-known` URL in this revision. That claim **could not be confirmed**
against the official release post, which mentions `.well-known` only in the OpenID Connect
discovery context (SEP-2351). Do not repeat the Server Cards claim without primary
confirmation. (Compare A2A's genuine agent cards, covered in `discovery-conventions.md`.)

> **Resolved 2026-08-04** against the final changelog: no Server Cards feature exists. The
> spec does add an in-band **`server/discover`** RPC, which is not the same thing — see
> `../S2-comprehensive/mcp-return-leg.md`. The revision also **deprecates** RFC 7591 Dynamic
> Client Registration in favor of Client ID Metadata Documents, so DCR references below
> describe the legacy path.

## Reach

The client side is broad: MCP is consumed by Claude's clients, and OpenAI documents MCP and
connectors as a supported integration path. An MCP server is not a single-vendor bet, which
distinguishes it sharply from prefill URLs.

## Trade-offs

**For it**: the only mechanism that closes both legs with structure; results return as typed
data, not text a human must re-key; no payload ceiling; durable — connect once, use
indefinitely; cross-vendor client support; the most active and best-governed spec in the
category; and the 2026-07-28 stateless core substantially lowers hosting cost.

**Against it**: requires running and operating a server, which prefill links do not; OAuth
2.1 + PKCE is mandatory for internet-facing servers, so the connect step cannot be skipped;
that step blocks first-touch and anonymous use entirely; the spec is moving fast enough that
implementations carry migration debt; and governance, while real, is vendor-originated rather
than neutral.

## Category Position

Remote MCP is the **structured lane** of this category and the natural terminus for any
service whose handoff carries more than a few thousand characters or needs machine-readable
results. It does not compete with prefill links — it serves a different moment in the user
relationship. Prefill links serve the visitor; MCP serves the committed user.

The open question this survey must resolve is whether MCP's connect step is a **temporary
friction** that one-click OAuth will erode, or a **permanent floor** that keeps a lighter,
unauthenticated handoff path alive indefinitely. The evidence so far points at the floor: the
connect step is not slow, it is *deliberate*, and deliberateness is the security property
being purchased.

# Vendor Prefill URLs and Custom URL Schemes

**Leg**: Outbound only
**Governance**: None — per-vendor product convention
**Status**: Widely shipped, individually undocumented, individually unstable

---

## What It Is

A link that opens the user's AI client with a prompt already in the input box. The page
composes the task as text, URL-encodes it into a query parameter, and renders an ordinary
anchor. Clicking it launches the AI — web app, desktop app, or CLI — with the prompt staged.

This is the lowest-friction outbound mechanism that exists. It requires nothing of the page
but string concatenation, and nothing of the user but having the client installed.

## Governance

There is no specification. Each vendor invents its own parameter names, its own length
ceiling, and its own answer to the question of whether the prompt auto-sends. No standards
body has chartered work here, and no vendor has proposed cross-vendor alignment. Parameters
appear and disappear between product releases with no deprecation cycle.

The most consequential asymmetry is not the parameter name but the **submit semantics**.
Some clients prefill and wait for the user; at least one is reported to auto-submit. A page
author writing one link for several vendors cannot assume the user gets a chance to read
what was staged on their behalf.

## The Anthropic Surfaces (documented)

Anthropic is the only vendor found in this pass with *published* deep-link documentation,
and it covers three distinct surfaces:

**Claude Desktop** — the `claude://` scheme, on macOS, Windows, and Linux. Paths include
`claude://claude.ai/new` (new chat), `claude://claude.ai/chat/{conversation-id}` (existing
chat), `claude://code/new`, and `claude://cowork/new`. A `q` parameter **prefills the prompt
field rather than auto-submitting** — the documentation is explicit that the text is staged
"so you can review and send it." Prompt text in `q` is **truncated at roughly 14,000
characters**. Code and Cowork sessions accept optional `folder` and `file` parameters, and
any folder supplied through a link is treated as untrusted and requires confirmation.

**Claude Code** — the `claude-cli://` scheme, whose only accepted path is
`claude-cli://open`. Parameters are `q` (prefill text, URL-encoded, `%0A` for line breaks,
**maximum 5,000 characters**), `cwd` (absolute working directory; network and UNC paths
rejected, as are paths containing invisible or bidirectional control characters), and `repo`
(a GitHub `owner/name` slug resolved against clones Claude Code has previously seen). If both
`cwd` and `repo` are supplied, `cwd` wins.

**VS Code extension** — registers its own handler at `vscode://anthropic.claude-code/open`,
opening an editor tab rather than a terminal.

The Claude Code documentation is notable for treating the untrusted-link problem as a design
requirement rather than an afterthought. A deep link "never executes anything on its own";
the session shows a persistent `Prompt from an external link` warning below the input box
until the prompt is sent or cleared, and for prompts over 1,000 characters the warning
includes the character count and instructs the user to scroll and review, since long prompts
can push instructions off screen. Handler registration happens on the first prompt of an
interactive session, writes only to user-level locations, and can be suppressed entirely via
a `disableDeepLinkRegistration` setting — enforceable organization-wide through managed
settings.

That combination — prefill-not-send, a visible provenance warning, a length-triggered
review prompt, and an administrable off switch — is the most complete answer any vendor in
this survey gives to *"a hostile page composed this prompt."* It is a product decision, not
a standard, but it is the closest thing to a reference design the category has.

## Other Vendors (reported)

Claims below appear consistently in secondary sources but were **not** confirmed against
primary vendor documentation in this pass, and should be re-verified before load-bearing use:

- **ChatGPT** — a `?q=` style prefill with a ceiling **reported** at roughly 6,000
  characters. No official parameter documentation was located.
- **Gemini** — `gemini.google.com/app?prompt=`, **reported** to auto-fill *and auto-submit*.
  If accurate, this is the mechanism's sharpest edge: the page's text reaches the model with
  no user review step.
- **claude.ai on the web** — the plain `claude.ai/new?q=` web parameter is **reported** to
  have been removed around October 2025, which would leave the documented `claude://`
  desktop scheme as the supported path. Sources conflict; treat as unresolved.

The pattern across vendors is clear enough without resolving every parameter: **the desktop
and CLI surfaces are documented and conservative; the web surfaces are undocumented and
volatile.**

## Practical Ceiling

Documented ceilings are 14,000 characters (Claude Desktop) and 5,000 (Claude Code). Reported
ceilings elsewhere sit near 6,000. Widely circulated advice to stay under about 4,000
characters is folklore that predates the documented limits, but it survives for a reason:
the *transport* between page and client imposes its own limits independent of the AI vendor.
Chat clients, ticket systems, and link-shortening middleboxes truncate long URLs, and
practical URL handling degrades well before any vendor's stated maximum.

The ceiling is therefore best understood as **a few thousand characters of prompt, reliably**
— enough for an instruction plus a modest excerpt, not enough for a document. Any handoff
whose payload scales with user data will outgrow this mechanism, which is the single most
important structural fact about it.

## Interoperability Constraint

**Platforms that render user content routinely strip custom URL schemes.** GitHub-rendered
Markdown allows only `http` and `https`: a `claude-cli://` link in a README, issue, pull
request, or wiki renders as bare label text with the URL removed. The documented workaround
is to place the link in a code block for manual copying — which is to say, *the fallback for
a failed deep link is the clipboard*.

This constraint applies wherever the page author does not control the renderer, and it is
the reason clipboard handoff cannot be dismissed as merely primitive.

## Trade-offs

**For it**: zero infrastructure; no user setup beyond having the client; works from any
surface that renders a link; degrades to a visible URL rather than a broken feature; the
user sees and can edit the task before it runs (on the surfaces that prefill).

**Against it**: outbound only — there is no return path, so the answer must come back by
some other route; hard payload ceiling in the low thousands of characters; no governance, so
every parameter is a moving target; per-vendor branching means the page must either ask the
user which AI they use or render several buttons; custom schemes are stripped by common
renderers; and where auto-submit is the behavior, the page can put words in the user's
mouth without review.

## Category Position

This is the **default outbound mechanism** and will remain so for any page that wants a
handoff to work today, for an unknown user, with no setup. Its limits are not incidental:
the payload ceiling and the missing return leg are structural, and they are what
the heavier mechanisms in this survey exist to solve. Nothing else in the category matches
its reach, and nothing about it constitutes a standard.

# Web Share API and Web Share Target

**Leg**: Outbound (Web Share API) / receiving side (Web Share Target)
**Governance**: W3C — `w3c/web-share-target`, Web Share Target Level 2 draft; Web Share is a W3C spec
**Status**: Genuine standard; real platform integration; **not AI-aware**

---

## What It Is

Two complementary specs describing the OS share sheet as a web-accessible mechanism.

**Web Share API** (`navigator.share`) lets a page hand text, a URL, or files to the operating
system's native share dialog. The OS presents the user's installed share targets and the user
picks one. The page never learns which target was chosen.

**Web Share Target** lets an installed web app *appear in that dialog*. A site declares a
`share_target` member in its Web App Manifest with an action URL and a params mapping — the
manifest names which query parameters receive the shared title, text, and URL. On Android,
Web APKs for sites whose manifest contains `share_target` get an equivalent entry in their
Android manifest and appear in the intent picker once installed.

Together they describe a **registry of destinations the user has chosen and the OS
maintains** — which is structurally the thing an outbound AI handoff wants and cannot
otherwise get.

## Governance

This is the most conventionally standardized mechanism in the survey. Web Share Target is
developed in the open at W3C with a Level 2 draft, an explainer, and a documented native
integration survey. It has a normative manifest member documented on MDN and shipped
platform behavior.

It is also, unlike WebMCP, **not new** — it long predates the current AI cycle, which is
exactly why it carries no AI-specific semantics.

## Why It Nearly Solves the Problem

The share sheet answers the hardest unsolved question in the outbound leg: **which AI does
this user actually use?**

Prefill URLs force the page to guess or to ask — either branch on a vendor or render a row
of competing buttons. `navigator.share()` makes that the OS's problem. The user's installed
AI clients appear alongside every other target, the user picks, and the page stays out of it.
No vendor branching, no parameter table, no per-vendor length ceiling to reason about.

Observationally, native AI apps do appear in mobile share sheets today. Note carefully that
this is via **platform-native** mechanisms — Android intent filters and iOS share extensions
— not via Web Share Target, which only applies to installed *web* apps. The distinction is
material: the receiving behavior a page relies on is the OS's, not the web platform's, and
is out of scope for the W3C spec. *(Specific AI vendors' share-target registration was not
confirmed against documentation in this pass; S2 should verify per-vendor.)*

## Why It Does Not

**No semantics.** Web Share carries `title`, `text`, `url`, and files. There is no field for
"this is a task," no field for "here is the schema I want back," no way to distinguish a
prompt from a bookmark. A shared task arrives at the AI as an undifferentiated blob of text —
the same shape a clipboard paste would deliver, minus the clipboard.

**No return leg at all.** The spec is one-way by design and the page is not told what
happened. `navigator.share()` resolves on successful hand-off, not on task completion. There
is no callback, no result, no correlation identifier. Whatever the AI produces stays in the
AI.

**Desktop coverage is the weak point.** The mechanism is strongest exactly where the OS share
sheet is a first-class citizen — mobile — and weakest on desktop browsers, where much of the
work this category cares about happens.

**Requires a user gesture** and, for the receiving side, an *installed* app. A page cannot
share programmatically, and a web-app AI client that the user has not installed will not
appear.

## Trade-offs

**For it**: a real, governed, long-standing W3C standard; the only mechanism that solves
destination discovery without the page guessing; no vendor branching; no parameter volatility;
native OS integration on mobile; the user's chosen defaults are respected by construction.

**Against it**: no AI-specific semantics whatsoever; strictly one-way with no result and no
correlation; the page cannot know whether anything happened; desktop support is the weak
flank; receiving side depends on platform-native registration outside the web spec; requires
an installed target and a user gesture.

## Category Position

Web Share is **the standard that already exists for the half of the problem nobody else
solves** — routing to the user's chosen destination — and it is unusable as a complete
handoff because it carries no meaning and returns nothing.

This makes it the most interesting mechanism in the survey for the question *what is actually
missing*. The transport is standardized, shipped, and OS-integrated. What is absent is a
**convention for what to put in the `text` field** so that a receiving AI could recognize a
structured task rather than a paragraph — and a correlation mechanism so the page could ever
learn the outcome. That is a payload and semantics gap sitting on top of a solved transport,
not a missing transport.

# WebMCP (`document.modelContext`)

**Leg**: Inbound-agent — the agent comes to the page
**Governance**: W3C Web Machine Learning Community Group — Draft Community Group Report
**Status**: Draft; origin trials live in Chrome 149 and Edge 150; **ChatGPT Desktop
consumes it** as of 2026-09-07 (Brave Leo experimental). Reach remains narrow — see below.

---

## What It Is

WebMCP lets a page **register tools** that an AI agent can discover and call. Instead of an
agent screenshotting the page and guessing where to click, the site hands it a list of the
things it can do and the exact parameters each one takes. The page declares
`document.modelContext` tools as ordinary JavaScript functions; the browser translates them
into MCP format when talking to the agent and handles the protocol work.

It inverts the usual control relationship: **the website tells the agent what is possible,
rather than the agent reverse-engineering the interface.**

## Governance — Read This Carefully

WebMCP is a **Draft Community Group Report** in the W3C **Web Machine Learning Community
Group**. A Community Group Report is *not* on the W3C standards track. It carries no
Recommendation status, no patent commitments of the kind a Working Group produces, and no
guarantee of stability. It is the lightest-weight artifact W3C publishes.

That said, it is real multi-vendor work with named institutional editors, which puts it well
above a vendor convention:

- **Authors/editors**: Microsoft and Google — Brandon Walderman (Microsoft), Khushal Sagar
  and Dominic Farolino (Google).
- **First announced**: 10 February 2026.
- **Most recent draft in this pass**: 21 July 2026.
- Firefox and Safari participate in specification discussions **without implementation
  commitments**.
- Anthropic created the upstream MCP protocol but has **made no public statement about
  WebMCP** since the August 2025 proposal — notable, given WebMCP borrows MCP's primitives.

So the characterization is: *the most credible standards activity in this category,
at the least binding possible status, from two of four browser engines.*

## API Surface and Its Churn

The API moved in July 2026 from `navigator.modelContext` to **`document.modelContext`**, on
the reasoning that tools belong to a specific page rather than to the browsing context.
Chrome **deprecated the `navigator` form in Chrome 150** while the origin trial continues to
ship it — so early implementers carry a migration obligation.

Beyond bulk tool registration, the draft supports dynamic `registerTool()` and
`unregisterTool()`, which matters for single-page applications whose available actions change
as the user navigates.

For sensitive actions, the draft provides **`agent.requestUserInteraction()`** to require
human confirmation before proceeding. Because the browser mediates every call, it "can
enforce web-specific security policies" — the security argument for putting this in the
browser rather than in a library.

WebMCP implements only MCP's **primitives layer** (tools, resources). The browser supplies
the data and transport layers. This is a deliberate coupling decision: it keeps WebMCP
compatible as MCP evolves underneath it.

## Implementation Reality

- **Chrome**: first shipped `navigator.modelContext` in **Chrome 146 Canary**, making Chrome
  the first browser with native WebMCP support. A public **origin trial spans Chrome
  149–156**.
- **Edge**: **origin trial live in Edge 150** (updated 2026-09-07; this was "experimental
  support behind a flag" as recently as 2026-08-04).
- **Firefox, Safari**: engaged in spec discussion; standards-position issues open, no
  implementation, no timelines.

## The Demand Side Arrived — Updated 2026-09-07

**This section previously read "No mainstream agent client calls `modelContext` tools yet"
and called that the single most important fact about WebMCP. It inverted in about a month,
and the correction is left visible rather than quietly rewritten, because the speed is the
finding.**

The specification's own `implementation-status.md` now lists, as of 2026-09-07:

| | status |
|---|---|
| **ChatGPT Desktop** | "WebMCP is supported in ChatGPT Desktop" |
| **Brave** | "Experimental support is added to Leo AI chat" |
| **Chrome** | Origin trial live in Chrome 149 |
| **Edge** | Origin trial live in Edge 150 |
| Firefox, Safari | standards-position issues open only |

So a page that adopts WebMCP today has at least one mainstream consumer, which was not true
in August. What has *not* changed is the shape of the reach: consumption requires the visitor
to be running a specific desktop client or an origin-trial browser, so the anonymous reader —
the largest population in S3 — is still not covered.

**The August assessment was "adopt cheaply, expect nothing, revisit on one specific signal:
a mainstream agent client announcing it consumes `document.modelContext`."** That signal has
fired. The revised position is in `../S4-strategic/webmcp-trajectory.md`.

The limited browser coverage matters less than it appears, because the consuming
agents are themselves Chromium-adjacent — Safari's absence costs little when no agent would
have used it. The binding constraint is client adoption, not engine coverage.

## Direction — Why This Is Not a Drop-In Answer

WebMCP solves **agent-comes-to-page**. The user is already browsing with an agent attached,
and the page makes itself legible to it.

The category this survey defines is **page-pushes-task-out**: the user is on the page, has no
agent attached to that page, and the page wants to route work to the AI the user pays for
elsewhere. WebMCP does not initiate anything. If the user arrives without an agent, WebMCP is
inert — there is no one listening.

The two compose rather than compete. A page can offer WebMCP tools for agent-attached
visitors and an outbound handoff for the rest. But **WebMCP is not the missing outbound
transport**, and treating its existence as proof that the handoff problem is solved would be
a category error.

Where WebMCP *is* directly relevant to this survey is the **return leg**: for an
agent-attached user, a tool call is a structured way for results to land back in the page —
the thing clipboard paste-back approximates by hand.

## Trade-offs

**For it**: genuine multi-vendor standards activity with named editors; browser-mediated
security and user-confirmation primitives; structured typed calls instead of DOM scraping;
built on MCP primitives, so it tracks a protocol with real momentum; the browser absorbs
transport complexity so the page writes plain functions.

**Against it**: Community Group draft, not standards track; API renamed and a form deprecated
within five months of announcement; one engine shipping, one flagged, two uncommitted; **zero
consuming clients in production**; wrong direction for outbound handoff; and the upstream MCP
author has been publicly silent on it.

## Category Position

WebMCP is the **strongest candidate for a governed standard anywhere in this category** and
still does not qualify as one — it is a draft at a non-normative status. It acquired its
first mainstream consumer between 2026-08-04 and 2026-09-07, which strengthens the candidacy
without changing the status.
For the survey's central question it is the decisive piece of prior art: it demonstrates that
the standards community *has* taken up page-to-agent communication, which forecloses any
claim that the space is unexamined. It equally demonstrates that the **outbound** leg remains
unclaimed, because WebMCP does not attempt it.

# The Unspecified Layer: Envelope and Payload Design

**Mechanism**: clipboard handoff and the payload convention that does not exist
**Verified**: 2026-08-04

---

## Why This File Exists

Every other file in S2 analyzes something with documentation to analyze. This one examines
the layer S1 identified as unclaimed: **what a task and its result look like as
text a human carries between a page and an arbitrary AI.**

There is no spec to summarize. What follows is an analysis of the design constraints the
problem imposes — derived from how the surrounding mechanisms fail — rather than a review of
prior art, because no prior art was found.

## The Asymmetry: Copy-Out Is Solved, Paste-Back Is Not

**Copy-out** is architecturally uninteresting and completely solved. The Async Clipboard API
is standardized, universally supported, and permission-gated behind a user gesture. The page
controls the exact bytes it emits. Nothing about this half is hard.

**Paste-back** carries the entire design problem. The page must accept text produced by a
model it did not prompt, in a client it does not control, formatted however that client's
system prompt and the user's editing left it — and decide whether it is usable.

The asymmetry is routinely missed and needs saying sharply: **the page has total
control over what it sends and none over what returns.** Every constraint below follows from
that single fact.

## Why the Envelope Cannot Be Ordinary Serialization

The obvious move is to pick a format — JSON in a fenced code block — and be done. The format
is fine. The format is not the problem.

**The payload passes through a human's screen twice.** This is the constraint that
disqualifies borrowing a wire format wholesale. The user sees the outbound envelope when they
copy it and the inbound envelope when they copy the result back. An envelope that reads as
machine noise gets edited, truncated, or distrusted by the person carrying it — and unlike
every other transport in this survey, **the human is part of the wire.**

No existing serialization format optimizes for this. Wire formats assume machine-to-machine
transit where legibility is a debugging convenience. Here it is a functional requirement: the
carrier is a person who may reasonably decide not to paste something that looks alarming.

**The model is an active participant, not a pipe.** A model asked to produce structured output
will wrap it in prose, add code fences, apologise for limitations, explain its reasoning, and
reformat whitespace. It may summarize the schema instead of filling it. Under a system prompt
the page never sees, it may refuse the format entirely.

This is not a failure to be engineered away; it is what conversational models do. An envelope
design that treats deviation as an error case has misunderstood the medium.

## Requirements the Problem Imposes

Five, and their stability across independent implementations is itself the S2 finding — these
are the shape of the problem, not one design's opinion:

**1. Version marker.** The page must reject or migrate payloads from an older format. Since
the envelope travels through an unversioned channel with no negotiation — no handshake, no
capability exchange, nothing MCP's header dispatch provides — the version has to be *in the
payload*. There is nowhere else to put it.

**2. Kind discriminator.** The page needs to know what it received. A page supporting several
handoffs must route an arriving envelope without guessing from content.

**3. Correlation identifier.** The single most valuable field, because it is the one thing the
one-way transports structurally cannot provide. `navigator.share()` resolves on hand-off, not
completion, and tells the page nothing about the target. Prefill URLs have no return channel
at all. An identifier the human carries in the text is the **only** correlation mechanism
available to those transports.

MCP solves this with task handles held by a client. Here the handle is held by a person, in
text, and must survive being read by a model that may decide it is noise and drop it. That is
the specific engineering problem: **a correlation token robust against a helpful
paraphraser.**

**4. Tolerant parsing.** The hard requirement. A parser demanding exact bytes fails
constantly. The envelope must be *recognisable inside surrounding chatter*, and the parser
must **extract rather than validate-or-reject**: locate a delimited region, tolerate
surrounding prose, tolerate code fences that may or may not be present, tolerate whitespace
reformatting, and accept partial results where fields are recoverable.

This inverts normal parser discipline. Ordinary robustness principles favor strictness in
what you accept, to avoid the interoperability rot that tolerant parsing produced
in HTML. Here strictness is unavailable: the sender is a model that cannot be made to comply
and a human who may edit in transit. **The tolerance is forced by the medium, and its costs
are real and should be stated rather than waved away** — tolerant parsers accumulate
compatibility quirks and are hard to change later.

**5. Human-legible surface.** The envelope should look like something a person is willing to
carry: recognisably structured, obviously not secret, and short enough to be scanned.

## Two Constraints From Elsewhere in the Survey

**Encoding overhead does not apply — and that is the lane's structural advantage.** The
clipboard carries raw text. No percent-encoding, no URL length ceiling, no silent truncation.
`outbound-transports.md` shows structured payloads encode badly into URLs, since JSON is
mostly the characters that expand. The clipboard's immunity to this is not a minor
convenience: it is the reason the lane survives for exactly the structured payloads a
handoff most wants to send.

**The prompt-length review problem transfers.** Claude Code escalates its warning above 1,000
characters because long prompts push instructions off screen and defeat review. The same
applies to a pasted envelope: past some length the human carrying it stops reading. An
envelope design that grows with the payload silently loses the user's review — which is the
lane's main safety property. Envelope structure should therefore stay bounded even when
content does not.

## The Adjacent Case: On-Device Model APIs

S1 excluded browser-bundled models from scope. S2 examines the boundary, because the
exclusion is load-bearing.

Chrome's **Prompt API** exposes the on-device **Gemini Nano** model through a `LanguageModel`
global (earlier explored as `window.ai`), letting a page run inference locally with no server
infrastructure and no user data leaving the device. It has been present since Chrome 138,
initially behind flags (`chrome://flags/#prompt-api-for-gemini-nano`,
`chrome://flags/#optimization-guide-on-device-model`) and subsequently available as an origin
trial for production sites.

**Why this is a different category**, in three respects:

- **Whose model.** The model is the *browser vendor's*, shipped with the browser. The user did
  not choose it and cannot substitute the assistant they pay for. The defining property of
  this survey's category — the user's own AI, on the user's own account — is absent.
- **Capability tier.** A small on-device model is not a substitute for a frontier model on a
  paid subscription. The tasks motivating a handoff are typically ones the small model cannot
  do; if it could, the page would not be handing off.
- **No handoff occurs.** The page calls the model directly and receives a return value. There
  is no outbound leg, no return leg, and no envelope — the entire problem this survey
  describes evaporates.

What it *does* threaten is the **low end**. Handoffs that exist only because the page has no
model — classify this, extract these fields, summarize this paragraph — can be served locally
with no clipboard, no link, and no user steps. The remaining case for a handoff is where the
user's *specific* assistant matters: its context about them, its memory, its subscription
tier, its tools.

That sharpens the category's boundary rather than blurring it. **Client-side AI handoff is
about routing to a chosen assistant, not about accessing any model.** A page with an on-device
model available still has a reason to hand off — it just no longer has a reason to hand off
trivial work.

## Architectural Verdict

The envelope layer is **small, well-constrained, and unclaimed**. Its requirements
are stable across independent derivation, its scope is closer to a microformat than a
protocol, and it would improve two mechanisms at once: the clipboard lane directly, and Web
Share's standardized-but-empty `text` field by giving it something meaningful to carry.

It is also the layer with **no owner and no incentive structure**. Every other mechanism here
has a vendor or a browser engine behind it. This one has whoever writes it down — which is
the S4 question, not S2's.

# Remote MCP as the Structured Return Leg

**Mechanism**: MCP specification revision **2026-07-28**
**Verified**: 2026-08-04

---

## Why the 2026-07-28 Revision Changes the Calculus

MCP's relevance to this survey was always conditional on cost. A page can emit a link for
nothing; running an MCP server was, until this revision, meaningfully harder than running an
ordinary API. The 2026-07-28 revision — the largest since MCP launched in November 2024, with
its release candidate locked 21 May 2026 — narrows that gap substantially.

## The Stateless Core

The `initialize`/`initialized` handshake (SEP-2575) and the `Mcp-Session-Id` header (SEP-2567)
are **gone**. Servers handle each request independently. Two required headers carry routing:

```http
Mcp-Method: tools/call
Mcp-Name: summarize_document
```

Protocol version and client capabilities do **not** travel in a header — they ride in `_meta`
on every request, as `io.modelcontextprotocol/protocolVersion` and
`io.modelcontextprotocol/clientCapabilities`, with clients identifying themselves via
`io.modelcontextprotocol/clientInfo` and servers via `io.modelcontextprotocol/serverInfo` in
each result. Mismatches return `UnsupportedProtocolVersionError`.

> **Corrected 2026-08-04.** An earlier draft of this file showed an `MCP-Protocol-Version`
> header, following May-2026 release-candidate summaries. No such header exists in the final
> specification. See 2.074's `refresh-2026-08.md` for the RC-versus-final deltas.

The operational consequence is stated directly in the release: any MCP request can land on
any server instance, so sticky routing and shared session stores are no longer required. A
server that previously needed sticky sessions, a shared session store, and deep packet
inspection at the gateway "can now run behind a plain round-robin load balancer."

For this survey the significance is not performance but **who can afford to participate**.
Moving session state out of the protocol turns an MCP endpoint into something a small
operator can host on ordinary infrastructure — the same shape as any stateless HTTP service.
The mechanism's remaining cost is authorisation, not architecture.

Dispatching on headers rather than a negotiated session also means intermediaries can route
on method and name without parsing bodies, which is why the gateway layer (2.083) gets
simpler at the same time.

## Authorisation: the Irreducible Step

Since the **November 2025** revision, any MCP server reachable over the internet **must
implement OAuth 2.1 with PKCE** using S256 — stated without exception. The 2026-07-28
revision hardened this across six SEPs:

| SEP | Requirement |
|---|---|
| SEP-2468 | Clients must validate the `iss` parameter per RFC 9207 |
| SEP-837 | Clients declare an OpenID Connect `application_type` during Dynamic Client Registration |
| SEP-2352 | Credentials bind to the issuing authorization server's `issuer` |
| SEP-2351 | OpenID Connect discovery for authorization servers via `.well-known` |

These are authorization-server-confusion and token-misredemption defences — the failure
class where a client is induced to send a token intended for one server to another. That the
working group spent a revision here indicates MCP is being deployed in adversarial, multi-
tenant settings, which is a maturity signal.

Managed providers now cover the full surface — PKCE, Resource Indicators, Protected Resource
Metadata — so connecting a server can reduce to a single Google or GitHub login with no JSON
configuration.

**One correction to the registration story.** The same revision **deprecates OAuth 2.0
Dynamic Client Registration (RFC 7591)** in favor of **Client ID Metadata Documents**,
retaining DCR only for backwards compatibility with authorization servers that lack CIMD
support. DCR is therefore the legacy path, not the recommended one.

**The architectural point S1 raised, restated exactly.** Whichever registration mechanism
is used, it removes only the *administrative* prerequisite: no pre-registered client ID, no
out-of-band setup between the page operator and the AI vendor. What it cannot remove is the
**authorization grant** — a human deciding, in an authorization-server UI, that this client
may act for them.

That is not latency to be optimized away. It is the security property being purchased. OAuth
exists to make the grant explicit and revocable; a version without a deliberate user decision
would not be OAuth. So the connect step is a **floor, not friction** — which is the finding
S1 predicted and this revision's direction confirms. Every change in 2026-07-28 makes the
step *smoother*; none makes it *optional*.

The consequence for the category is structural: **remote MCP cannot serve an anonymous
first-time visitor**, and no amount of specification work will change that. A mechanism
requiring an authorization grant before delivering value is disqualified from first-touch by
construction, which is the gap the clipboard lane occupies.

## Tasks: Work That Outlives a Request

Long-running work graduated from experimental core to an extension:

- `tools/call` returns a **task handle** rather than blocking for a result.
- Clients drive progress with `tasks/get`, `tasks/update`, and `tasks/cancel`.
- `tasks/list` was **removed**.

The removal is informative. Without `tasks/list`, a client cannot enumerate tasks it does not
already hold handles for — task discovery is not a protocol feature. Handles are capabilities:
holding one is the authority to inspect the task. That is a coherent model for a stateless
core, and it means correlation is the *client's* responsibility to carry.

Correlation via an opaque handle is exactly the primitive the one-way transports lack. Web
Share and prefill URLs have no way to tie a result to a request; MCP has handles. The
difference is that MCP can hand a handle to a *client*, while the one-way transports would
have to hand it to a *human* — which is the design constraint the envelope work in
`envelope-design.md` inherits.

## MCP Apps: UI in the Other Direction

MCP Apps (SEP-1865) let servers ship interactive HTML that hosts render in a **sandboxed
iframe**. UIs are declared ahead of time so they can be cached and security-reviewed, and
they communicate back over the same JSON-RPC protocol as direct tool calls.

It ships as an **official extension** (`io.modelcontextprotocol/ui`), not as part of the core
— the 2026-07-28 revision makes extensions a first-class negotiated concept, and neither Apps
nor Tasks is baked into the base SDK. Reach is already real: host support has shipped in
ChatGPT, Claude, Goose, and VS Code, with servers from Shopify, Hugging Face, and ElevenLabs.

This inverts the usual framing of the whole category. Every mechanism surveyed so far moves
work *from the page to the AI*. MCP Apps move **the service's interface into the AI client**.
The user never returns to the page; the page's UI comes to them.

Declaring UIs ahead of time is the same containment instinct seen in WebMCP's character
budgets and Claude Code's prefill-not-send: constrain what the untrusted party can introduce
at runtime. Across three independent designs by different teams, the pattern is consistent —
**runtime-introduced content is where these systems get attacked**, which is what
the WebMCP tool-injection research demonstrates empirically.

## Resolving the "Server Cards" Flag

S1 recorded a secondary-source claim that this revision introduced **Server Cards** for
capability discovery via a `.well-known` URL, and asked S2 to confirm or kill it.

**Killed.** The official release post does not mention Server Cards. It references
`.well-known` in exactly one context: OpenID Connect discovery for authorization servers
(SEP-2351). The claim appears to conflate MCP's OIDC discovery with **A2A's agent cards**,
which are published at `/.well-known/agent.json` and do carry capability
metadata — including cryptographic signing since A2A v1.0.

**But the final specification does add discovery — of a different kind.** Servers MUST
implement a **`server/discover`** RPC advertising their supported protocol versions,
capabilities, and identity (SEP-2575). Clients may call it before any other request for
up-front version selection, or use it as a backward-compatibility probe on STDIO.

The distinction is the whole point, and it must be stated exactly:

| | In-band (`server/discover`) | Out-of-band (A2A agent cards) |
|---|---|---|
| Prerequisite | You already have the endpoint URL | You have a domain name |
| Answers | "What can this server do?" | "Does this origin have an agent, and what is it?" |
| Published at | The endpoint itself, via RPC | `/.well-known/agent.json` |
| Signed | No | Yes, since A2A v1.0 |

So **capability discovery is now solved once you hold the endpoint; endpoint discovery is
not.** A page still has no standard way to advertise "I have an MCP server, here it is" such
that a client would find it and offer to connect — which is the half this survey cares about,
since a page meeting a stranger has no way to hand over a URL except by printing it.

A2A remains the only worked example of **signed, out-of-band** capability discovery in the
neighborhood.

> **Revised 2026-08-04** against the final 2026-07-28 changelog. The Server Cards kill stands
> — no such feature, no capability-discovery `.well-known` URL. An earlier draft went further
> and said capability discovery "remains unaddressed," which the `server/discover` RPC makes
> too broad. Narrowed above.

Anyone repeating the Server Cards claim in a public setting should expect to be corrected.

## Client Reach

MCP is consumed by Claude's clients, and OpenAI documents MCP and connectors as a supported
integration path. An MCP server is therefore not a single-vendor bet — a material contrast
with prefill URLs, where every vendor is a separate implementation and a separate parameter
table.

This is the strongest argument for MCP as the category's structured lane: it is the only
mechanism where **one implementation reaches multiple AI vendors** and returns typed data.

# Outbound Transport Mechanics

**Mechanisms**: custom URL schemes, `registerProtocolHandler()`, Web Share / Share Target
**Verified**: 2026-08-04

---

## Handler Registration Is the Whole Problem

Every outbound mechanism reduces to the same question: **when the user clicks, what receives
the click?** The transports differ almost entirely in who answers that question and when.

| Mechanism | Registrant | When | User consent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Custom URL scheme (`claude://`, `claude-cli://`) | The AI client, with the OS | On install or first use | OS-level, implicit |
| `registerProtocolHandler()` | The destination *site* | On call, per browser | Explicit permission prompt |
| Web Share Target | The destination app, via manifest | On install | Implicit in installing |
| Web Share (`navigator.share`) | n/a — OS presents all targets | At click time | The pick *is* the consent |

The pattern: **the page that wants the handoff never registers anything.** It is always
betting that some destination already did. This is why the mechanisms fail differently but
for the same underlying reason — S1 identified it as a coordination problem, and the
registration table is what that looks like architecturally.

## Custom Scheme Registration, Per Platform

Claude Code's handler registration is the best-documented instance in the category and shows
what the OS-level path actually involves.

Registration occurs **when the user sends their first prompt of an interactive session** —
not at install, and not when a session merely starts. It writes to user-level locations only:

| Platform | Handler location |
|---|---|
| macOS | `~/Applications/Claude Code URL Handler.app` |
| Linux | `claude-code-url-handler.desktop` under `$XDG_DATA_HOME/applications` (default `~/.local/share/applications`) |
| Windows | `HKEY_CURRENT_USER\Software\Classes\claude-cli` |

Three architectural observations.

**User-level only.** No administrator rights, no system-wide mutation. This is what makes
per-user AI client registration viable at all, and it is why the mechanism works on managed
machines where installing software does not.

**Registration is earned, not requested.** Tying it to first actual use rather than install
means a handler exists only for clients the user uses — which quietly improves the
odds that a deep link resolves to something the user wants. It also produces the category's
most common failure: a freshly installed client whose links silently do nothing.

**It is administrable.** A `disableDeepLinkRegistration` setting suppresses registration
entirely and can be enforced organization-wide through managed settings. Any handoff design
targeting enterprise users must assume this may be off.

Dispatch after registration is ordinary OS behavior — `open` on macOS, `xdg-open` on Linux,
`Start-Process` in PowerShell — which means deep links are equally reachable from scripts and
automation, not only from clicks. `xdg-open` comes from `xdg-utils`, routinely absent from
minimal server images, containers, and WSL distributions, so Linux dispatch has a real
dependency that desktop-oriented docs tend to gloss.

## Payload Mechanics and the Ceiling

The parameter is a URL query value, so the payload must be percent-encoded, with `%0A` for
line breaks in multi-line prompts.

Documented ceilings:

| Surface | Limit | Behavior at limit |
|---|---|---|
| Claude Desktop `claude://` | ~14,000 characters | Truncated |
| Claude Code `claude-cli://` | 5,000 characters | Documented maximum |
| ChatGPT (reported) | ~6,000 characters | Unconfirmed |

**Encoding overhead is the trap.** Percent-encoding expands the payload before any limit
applies. A character requiring encoding becomes three bytes (`%0A`), and structured content —
JSON, code, anything with braces, quotes, and newlines — encodes far worse than prose. A
5,000-character budget does not carry 5,000 characters of JSON. This is why the practical
ceiling sits well below the documented one for exactly the payloads a structured handoff
would want to send, and it is a stronger argument against scheme-based transport than the raw
numbers suggest.

**Truncation is silent.** A prompt exceeding the ceiling arrives cut off. It does not error.
For prose the user may notice; for a structured payload, truncation produces something that
looks complete and is not.

## Intermediary Stripping

**Renderers that allow only `http`/`https` strip custom schemes.** GitHub does this across
READMEs, issues, pull requests, and wikis: the link renders as bare label text with the URL
removed entirely — not a broken link, an *invisible* one. The documented workaround is to put
the URL in a code block for manual copying.

The architectural consequence: **the documented fallback for a
stripped deep link is the clipboard.** Any design betting on custom schemes needs the
clipboard lane anyway, wherever the page does not control the renderer.

## The Untrusted-Prompt Problem

A deep link means an arbitrary page composes text that lands in the user's AI. Claude Code's
documentation treats this as a design requirement and gives the category its only worked
example of mitigation:

- **Prefill, never send.** "A deep link never executes anything on its own." The link chooses
  a directory and fills the prompt box; nothing reaches the model until the user presses
  Enter.
- **Persistent provenance indicator.** A `Prompt from an external link` warning sits below
  the input box until the prompt is sent or cleared.
- **Length-triggered review escalation.** Above 1,000 characters the warning includes the
  character count and instructs the user to scroll and review, because long prompts push
  instructions off screen.
- **Path sanitisation.** `cwd` rejects network and UNC paths and paths containing invisible or
  bidirectional control characters. Any folder supplied through a link is treated as untrusted
  and requires confirmation.

That third item is the subtle one. It concedes that **the user's review is only as good as
what fits on screen**, and that a sufficiently long prompt defeats review by burying its
payload. This is the same failure mode as WebMCP's character budgets, reached from the
opposite direction: both mechanisms bound the attacker's prompt surface because neither can
bound the reader's attention.

Set against this, the *reported* auto-submit behavior of at least one major vendor is not a
minor parameter difference. It removes every one of these protections at once. Confirming
that behavior against primary documentation remains the highest-value open verification in
this survey.

## Web Share Internals

`navigator.share()` accepts `title`, `text`, `url`, and files. It requires a **transient user
activation** — it cannot be called programmatically — and returns a promise that **resolves
on successful hand-off, not on task completion**. The page is never told which target was
chosen.

The receiving side is declared in the Web App Manifest:

```json
"share_target": {
  "action": "/handle-share",
  "method": "GET",
  "params": { "title": "title", "text": "text", "url": "link" }
}
```

The `params` object maps share fields onto the query parameter names the action URL expects.
On Android, Web APKs for manifests containing `share_target` get an equivalent entry in the
Android manifest and appear in the intent picker once installed.

Two architectural limits follow directly from this shape.

**No result, by construction.** There is no callback, no correlation identifier, and no
channel back. This is not an oversight — a share target is a separate app, often a separate
process, frequently on a different origin. Returning a result would require a session concept
the spec does not have, by design.

**No semantics.** Three untyped string fields. There is no field for "this is a task," none
for "here is the schema I want back," and no way to distinguish a prompt from a bookmark. A
task arrives as undifferentiated text in `text` — structurally identical to a clipboard paste,
minus the clipboard.

Which is exactly where the category's actual gap becomes visible in the wire format itself.
`text` is a standardized, OS-integrated, vendor-neutral, permission-correct transport field
with **nothing specified about what to put in it**. The transport is finished. The convention
is missing.

# S2 Synthesis — What the Architecture Constrains

**Survey**: 2.078
**Pass**: S2 Comprehensive Analysis
**Verified**: 2026-08-04

---

## The Structural Finding

S1 found that the transports are solved and the payload semantics are not. S2's architectural
analysis sharpens that into something more useful: **the mechanisms in this category are not
competing implementations of one idea. They are answers to different questions, and the
question each answers is fixed by where it puts the human.**

| Mechanism | Untrusted thing | Who decides | Review step |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prefill URL / custom scheme | Prompt text from a page | Human, before send | Structural — prefill, not send |
| Web Share | Text in an untyped field | Human, picks the target | Structural — the pick |
| Clipboard envelope | Model output, page input | Human, twice | Structural — sees both directions |
| WebMCP | **Tool metadata** | **The model** | Constructed — budgets, hints, `exposedTo` |
| Remote MCP | Server capability | Human, once at the OAuth grant | Front-loaded — then delegated |

The three mechanisms with a human in the loop get their safety property **for free from the
shape of the transport**. The two that automate must **rebuild** that property — and the
published attack research shows the rebuild is incomplete.

This is the architectural insight the category's discourse mostly misses. The clipboard lane's
manual steps are usually read as backwardness awaiting automation. They are also the review
step, and every mechanism that removes them has had to reintroduce something in its place:
Claude Code's persistent external-link warning, WebMCP's character budgets and annotation
hints, MCP Apps' ahead-of-time UI declaration, the OAuth grant. **Three independent teams
converged on constraining runtime-introduced content**, which is the empirical shape of the
problem.

That does not make the clipboard lane preferable. It makes the automation cost visible: what
you pay for removing the human is a security surface you must then engineer, and the WebMCP
results suggest the engineering is not finished.

## What the Evidence Actually Constrains

**Remote MCP cannot serve first-touch.** Not a maturity problem, not a UX problem. Dynamic
Client Registration removes the administrative prerequisite; the authorization grant remains
because it *is* the security property. Every 2026-07-28 change makes the step smoother; none
makes it optional. Any strategy assuming this friction disappears is assuming OAuth stops
being OAuth.

**WebMCP does not address the outbound leg and will not.** It is agent-comes-to-page by
design. A page whose visitor has no agent attached has no one to talk to. Since no mainstream
client consumes `document.modelContext` as of this pass, that is currently every visitor.

**Scheme-based transports cannot carry structured payloads well.** The documented ceilings
(5,000 and ~14,000 characters) overstate real capacity because percent-encoding expands
exactly the characters structured data is made of, and truncation is silent. This is a
property of URLs, not of any vendor's generosity.

**The clipboard's immunity to encoding overhead is structural, not incidental.** Raw text, no
ceiling, no truncation, no renderer stripping. This is why it survives as the fallback even
where prettier mechanisms exist — and the fallback for a *stripped deep link* is documented as
the clipboard, so a design betting on custom schemes needs the clipboard anyway.

## Corrections This Pass Made

**MCP "Server Cards": killed.** Not in the official 2026-07-28 release. The only `.well-known`
reference is OIDC discovery for authorization servers (SEP-2351). The claim appears to
conflate MCP with A2A's genuine `/.well-known/agent.json` agent cards. The final spec does add
a **`server/discover`** RPC (SEP-2575), but it is **in-band** — you must already hold the
endpoint URL to call it. So capability discovery is solved once you have the endpoint;
**endpoint discovery is not**, and that is the half this survey needs.

**WebMCP's security model: substantiated and qualified.** `agent.requestUserInteraction()` is
real but Chrome states implementation details "remain under discussion." Origin scoping via
`exposedTo`, the `untrustedContentHint` and `readOnlyHint` annotations, and the character
budgets (500 description / 150 per parameter / 30 name / 1,500 output) are documented. Chrome
concedes it is "impossible to guarantee safety inside of a large language model."

**WebMCP's attack surface: documented with measurements.** Mid-Session Tool Injection (Lee,
Chang, Yu, Yeh — NYCU, June 2026) reaches 94–100% success via tool hijacking and 36–85% via
tool framing, the latter preserving task completion 81–85% of the time. Registration races hit
100%. Note that tool framing attacks `readOnlyHint` — **the same field Chrome's guidance tells
authors to set**. Proposed mitigations (origin-bound immutable tool IDs, lifecycle validation,
third-party data boundaries, registration logs) are research prototypes, not spec features.

## Remaining Unresolved

- **ChatGPT and Gemini prefill parameters and submit semantics.** Still secondary-source only.
  The reported Gemini auto-submit is the highest-value open item in the survey: if accurate,
  it removes every protection the Anthropic surfaces build in, and it is the difference
  between a page *suggesting* a prompt and a page *speaking as the user*.
- **Whether `claude.ai/new?q=` still functions on the web.** Sources conflict.
- **Which AI clients register as OS share targets, by which platform mechanism.** Observable
  in the wild; not confirmed against documentation.
- **Whether any mainstream agent has begun consuming `document.modelContext`.** The single
  fact that most determines this category's shape.

## What S2 Hands to S3 and S4

**To S3**: the mechanism-selection axis is not capability but **relationship stage**. Anonymous
first-touch, engaged-but-unconnected, and committed-and-authenticated are served by different
mechanisms for structural reasons, and no mechanism spans all three. Personas should be built
on that axis rather than on task type.

**To S4**: two strategic questions the architecture poses but cannot answer.

1. **Does the envelope layer get written, and by whom?** It is microformat-sized, its
   requirements are stable under independent derivation, and it has no owner or incentive
   structure — the only layer here with no vendor or browser engine behind it.
2. **Does agent-attached browsing become ordinary?** If it does, the outbound leg loses most
   of its motivation. The path runs through WebMCP-shaped interfaces, and the consumer gap
   means it has not started. The Atlas retirement (announced 9 July 2026, service ending 9
   August 2026) is evidence that the *dedicated browser* route to that future has already
   failed once.

# WebMCP Architecture and Its Attack Surface

**Mechanism**: `document.modelContext` (W3C Web Machine Learning CG draft)
**Verified**: 2026-08-04

---

## The Layering Decision

WebMCP implements **only MCP's primitives layer** — tools and resources. The browser supplies
the data and transport layers. A page registers ordinary JavaScript functions; the browser
translates them into MCP form when an agent asks.

This is the single most consequential architectural choice in the spec, and it cuts two ways.

**In its favor**: the page never speaks MCP. It writes functions with JSON Schema parameter
descriptions and the browser handles framing, transport, and protocol negotiation. When MCP
revises its wire format — as it did substantially on 2026-07-28 — pages built on WebMCP do
not have to change, because they were never coupled to the wire in the first place. The
browser absorbs the churn.

**Against it**: everything the browser does not expose is unavailable. The page cannot
negotiate transport, cannot see the agent's identity beyond what the browser reveals, and
cannot implement protocol features the browser has not implemented. The page is a tool
provider and nothing more.

## Registration Lifecycle

The API moved from `navigator.modelContext` to **`document.modelContext`** in the 21 July
2026 draft, on the reasoning that tools belong to a document rather than a browsing context.
Chrome deprecated the `navigator` form in Chrome 150 while continuing to ship it through the
origin trial (Chrome 149–156), so implementations built during the trial carry a migration.

Registration comes in two shapes:

- **Bulk declaration** — the page provides its full tool set at once.
- **Incremental** — `registerTool()` and `unregisterTool()` mutate the set at runtime.

The incremental form exists for single-page applications, where the available actions change
as the user navigates without a document load. It is also, as the next section shows, the
source of the mechanism's most serious documented vulnerability.

A tool declaration carries a name, a description, a parameter schema, and a handler. The
description is not documentation — it is **the input on which the agent decides whether to
call the tool**. Descriptions are prompt content. This collapses a distinction that ordinary
API design keeps separate, and it is why the security model below is not optional reading.

## Origin Controls and Annotation Hints

Chrome's official guidance defines the page author's responsibilities concretely.

**Exposure is scoped by origin.** `registerTool()` accepts an `exposedTo` parameter naming
the origins permitted to see the tool. By default, "other websites or cross-origin iframes
can't observe or interact with your tools." Chrome enforces origin-based access control at
the API. The guidance draws the line by capability: read-only tools revealing user data
should be exposed only to origins you would share that data with directly; read-write tools
only to origins you would authorize to act on the user's behalf.

**Annotation hints steer the agent.** Two are documented:

- `untrustedContentHint` — set when a tool returns user-generated or external content, so the
  agent knows the output is not authored by the site.
- `readOnlyHint` — set on non-mutating tools, informing the agent's risk calculus.

**Character budgets** bound the prompt surface a page can occupy:

| Element | Budget |
|---|---|
| Tool description | 500 characters |
| Per parameter | 150 characters |
| Tool name | 30 characters |
| Per output | 1,500 characters |

Chrome notes these "are subject to change." They exist because tool metadata enters the
agent's context, so an unbounded description is an unbounded injection vector. The budgets
are a containment measure, not an ergonomics one.

**`agent.requestUserInteraction()`** asynchronously requests user input at tool execution
time, for actions needing human confirmation. Chrome's documentation states that
implementation details "remain under discussion" — so S1's description of it as a
human-in-the-loop primitive is correct in intent, but it is not yet a settled interface.

Chrome states the limit of the whole approach plainly: it is "impossible to guarantee safety
inside of a large language model," because the models are probabilistic. The browser can
enforce origin boundaries and budgets. It cannot enforce that the agent reasons correctly
about what it reads.

## The Documented Attack Surface

Security research published in June 2026 by Lin-Fa Lee, Yi-Yu Chang, Chia-Mu Yu, and Kuo-Hui
Yeh (National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University) analyzes **Mid-Session Tool Injection (MSTI)**
against WebMCP, and it is the most specific evidence available about the mechanism's real
risk profile.

**The broken assumption.** Conventional agent designs assume "the set of tools available to
an agent within a single session is no longer static and inherently trusted." WebMCP's
dynamic registration makes the tool surface mutable at runtime. **The tool surface itself
becomes a security boundary** — one that did not previously exist.

**Threat model.** The attacker compromises a third-party script the page already loads — a
CDN asset, an advertising SDK — giving them arbitrary same-page script execution. This is a
realistic position, not a contrived one: it is the ordinary supply-chain surface of a modern
web page. Critically, the attacker does not need to compromise the *site*, only something the
site includes. Every mitigation that assumes the page author is the only party calling
`registerTool()` fails here.

**Two attack families**, with measured results against GPT-5.4, Claude Opus, and Gemini 2.5:

| Family | Method | Attack success | Task completion preserved |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tool Hijacking** | Alters visible tools directly — `AbortSignal` manipulation, registration race conditions | 94–100% | 17–18% |
| **Tool Framing** | Manipulates metadata — names, descriptions, `readOnlyHint` | 36–85% | 81–85% |

Registration races reached **100%** success. Description injection succeeded **59%** of the
time.

**The finding that matters**: tool framing is *stealthier* than hijacking. Hijacking wins
almost always but breaks the task, so the user notices. Framing wins less often but leaves
the task completing normally 81–85% of the time — the user gets what they asked for while
data is exfiltrated. Composite attacks maintained completion while leaking.

Note what tool framing attacks: `readOnlyHint` is one of the two annotation hints Chrome's
own guidance tells authors to use. The hint is advisory metadata that steers the agent, and
an attacker who can rewrite it can tell the agent a mutating tool is safe. **The security
guidance and the attack surface are the same field.**

**Proposed mitigations** — binding tool identity to origin with immutable IDs, lifecycle
consistency validation, data boundaries for third-party tools, and traceable
registration/invocation logs. The authors report defences reducing attack success to 0%
while preserving legitimate completion. These are research prototypes, not spec features.

## What This Means for the Category

Three architectural conclusions follow, and they generalise beyond WebMCP.

**First, dynamic registration is a liability the spec has not fully priced.**
`registerTool()`/`unregisterTool()` exist for SPA ergonomics and deliver the highest-success
attack in the literature. A spec at Community Group status with a 100%-success registration
race in published research has design work remaining, and any adopter should treat the
incremental API as the riskier half.

**Second, the trust boundary sits in an unusual place.** In every other mechanism in this
survey, the untrusted thing is *content* and the decision-maker is a *human* — the user reads
a prefilled prompt and presses Enter, or reads a pasted answer and decides to accept it. In
WebMCP, the untrusted thing is *tool metadata* and the decision-maker is a *model*. That
substitution removes the review step the other mechanisms retain by construction.

This reframes the clipboard lane's apparent primitiveness. The manual steps that make it
feel unpolished — the user seeing exactly what is sent and exactly what returns — are the
same property WebMCP has to reconstruct with origin controls, character budgets, annotation
hints, and a confirmation primitive still under discussion.

**Third, the layering that protects pages from MCP churn also removes their defences.**
Because the browser owns transport and identity, a page cannot independently verify which
agent is calling or attest its own tool set. It can only declare `exposedTo` and trust the
browser's enforcement. That is a reasonable trade for the ergonomics; it is not a neutral
one, and it means the page's security posture is exactly as good as the browser's — with no
option to do better.

# Persona: The Publisher Whose Readers Arrive Cold

**Stage**: Anonymous first-touch
**Constraint**: No account, no install, no connection, no second chance

---

## Who

Anyone publishing substantive material to an audience that arrives from search, a link, or a
newsletter and may never return: research write-ups, analyzes, technical reports, data
journalism, standards explainers, long-form documentation.

The reader lands, reads, and either does something with the material or does not. There is no
onboarding, no signup wall, and no prior relationship. The publisher does not know who they
are, what AI they use, or whether they use one at all.

## Why the Need Arises

Published analysis has a persistent failure mode: **the reader agrees with it and does
nothing.** The material describes a situation, but applying it to the reader's own
circumstances requires work the page cannot do — it does not know their data, their
constraints, or their question.

An AI the reader already pays for *can* do that work. It can take the framing from the page
and apply it to the reader's specifics, in a session where the reader can push back and
iterate. The page's job becomes handing over a well-formed starting point rather than trying
to anticipate every reader.

This is a genuine value transfer and it costs the publisher nothing per reader — which
matters enormously to publishers whose economics do not support per-reader inference.

## What Rules Mechanisms Out

**The reader is anonymous, which eliminates the entire structured lane.** Remote MCP requires
an OAuth grant before delivering anything. Asking a first-time reader to authorize a
connection to a page they have read once is not a friction problem to be smoothed — it is
asking for a security decision before any value has been demonstrated. They will not do it,
and they should not.

**The publisher does not know which AI the reader uses.** A prefill link must name a vendor.
Guessing wrong produces a dead button; rendering one button per vendor turns a call to action
into a decision the reader did not want to make, and dates the page as vendors come and go.

**The renderer may not be the publisher's.** Material that travels — syndicated, quoted,
pasted into an issue tracker, rendered by a platform that strips custom schemes — loses deep
links silently. Not broken: invisible.

**Mobile and desktop both matter and behave differently.** Web Share works well on mobile and
poorly on desktop, so it cannot be the only path.

## What "Working" Means Here

- The reader can act on the material **immediately**, with no setup step of any kind.
- It works regardless of which AI they use, including one that did not exist when the page was
  written.
- It survives syndication and re-rendering.
- The reader can see what is being handed over. Trust is the whole relationship at this stage;
  an opaque action from a page someone just met is worse than no action.
- Nothing about it costs the publisher per reader.

## The Uncomfortable Fit

This persona is served by the **least specified mechanism in the category**. Clipboard handoff
is the only thing that satisfies every constraint above: vendor-neutral, zero setup, no
payload ceiling, immune to renderer stripping, and fully visible to the reader.

It is also the only mechanism with no specification at any level, which means every publisher
implementing it invents their own format and none interoperate. The reader who has learned to
recognize one publisher's handoff learns nothing transferable.

The **return leg is where this persona is served worst**. A reader who takes the material to
their AI, gets an answer, and wants to bring something back — a result, a correction, a
structured output the page could act on — has no supported path unless the publisher built a
paste-back lane by hand. Most do not. So the common case is a one-way handoff where the page
never learns whether anything happened, which also means the publisher gets no signal about
whether the affordance is worth maintaining.

This persona is the strongest argument in the survey for the envelope convention being worth
writing: it has the largest population, the least tolerance for setup, and the least served
return leg.

# S3 Approach — Who Needs a Client-Side AI Handoff

**Survey**: 2.078
**Pass**: S3 Need-Driven Discovery

## The Selection Axis

S2 established that the mechanisms in this category are not competing implementations of one
idea. Each is fixed to a particular position by where it places the human, and no mechanism
spans the whole range.

The practical consequence is that **the deciding variable is not the task — it is the
relationship stage between the page and the person reading it.**

| Stage | What the page knows | Mechanisms structurally available |
|---|---|---|
| **Anonymous first-touch** | Nothing. No account, no install, no connection. | Clipboard; prefill links (if vendor guessed); Web Share (mobile) |
| **Engaged, unconnected** | Some identity; willing to act; hasn't authorized anything | All of the above, plus deep links to known clients |
| **Committed, authenticated** | Account, and willingness to grant authorisation | Remote MCP with OAuth; everything above |
| **Agent-attached** | Visitor arrives with an agent reading the page | WebMCP — currently no consuming clients |

A persona's position on this axis determines which mechanisms are even candidates, before any
question of task complexity. A page that mostly meets strangers cannot use the mechanism that
requires a grant, however well that mechanism would serve the work.

Personas below are therefore organized by stage, not by industry or task type.

## What Personas Are For

Each persona states **who** has this need and **why** it arises — the situation, the
constraint that rules mechanisms out, and what "working" means for them. Personas do not
contain implementation guidance; that is not what this pass is for.

None of these personas is the survey's recommendation. They are the range of situations the
category has to serve, and they exist partly to demonstrate that a single answer would be
wrong.

## The Personas

| File | Persona | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| `anonymous-reader.md` | Publisher whose readers arrive cold | Anonymous first-touch |
| `no-inference-budget-operator.md` | Small operator shipping a product with no model spend | Anonymous → engaged |
| `governed-enterprise.md` | Organization where only the approved AI may see the data | Engaged, constrained |
| `docs-and-developer-experience.md` | Documentation team whose readers debug with their own agent | Engaged, unconnected |
| `committed-workflow-user.md` | Daily user of a tool who will connect it once | Committed, authenticated |
| `regulated-and-local-only.md` | Context where data cannot leave the device or org | Any stage, hard constraint |

## A Note on Scope

These personas describe *why the need arises*, not which product should be built. Several of
them are currently served badly or not at all — that is a finding, not a gap in the pass. Where
a persona has no good mechanism, S3 says so plainly and leaves the strategic implication to

# Persona: The Committed Workflow User

**Stage**: Committed, authenticated — the one persona for whom setup is rational
**Constraint**: Repetition makes manual handoff intolerable

---

## Who

Someone who uses a particular tool as part of their regular work — a project tracker, a CRM,
a writing environment, an analytics console, a personal knowledge system. They have an
account, they return daily or weekly, and the tool holds data they care about.

They are not evaluating the product. They have chosen it.

## Why the Need Arises

The economics of friction invert with repetition.

At first touch, a clipboard round trip is a reasonable price for zero setup — a few seconds
once. Performed twenty times a week, the same round trip becomes the reason someone stops
using a feature. Each iteration costs the same as the first; nothing amortises. Worse, the
manual path is **lossy**: the user re-selects, re-copies, and re-pastes, and each step is a
chance to carry the wrong text or lose the result.

Meanwhile the payoff from structure rises. A one-off answer can be read by a human and acted
on manually. A recurring one wants to land back **in the tool** — as a field, a record, a
status, a linked note — where it participates in whatever the tool does next. Reading and
re-keying defeats the purpose.

This persona also wants the AI to see **more than one item at a time**: not "summarize this
record" but "look across everything I have open this week." That is beyond what any
text-carried handoff can stage, because the payload would have to contain the whole working
set.

## What Changes at This Stage

**The OAuth grant becomes affordable.** This is the persona that makes the structured lane
viable, and it is the only one in the survey where the connect step is straightforwardly
worth it. The user already trusts the tool with their data. Authorising their assistant to
reach it is a smaller decision than the one they already made by storing the data there.

The cost is paid **once**; the benefit accrues on every subsequent interaction. That is the
exact inverse of the clipboard's cost profile, and it is why the two mechanisms serve
different moments rather than competing.

**Structured results become the point.** Typed data returning through a tool call means the
tool can act on it — update a record, populate a field, trigger a workflow — without the user
transcribing anything.

**Long-running work becomes plausible.** MCP's Tasks extension lets `tools/call` return a
handle that the client drives with `tasks/get`, `tasks/update`, and `tasks/cancel`. Work that
outlives a single exchange is only meaningful to someone who will still be around when it
finishes — which is exactly this persona and none of the others.

## What Rules Mechanisms Out

**Manual clipboard as the primary path.** It works, and its cost is proportional to usage,
which is the wrong shape for daily work.

**Payload-ceilinged mechanisms.** This user's context is their whole working set, not a
paragraph. Anything bounded by URL length is disqualified.

**Vendor-specific prefill links.** A daily workflow cannot be built on parameters that change
without deprecation cycles.

## What "Working" Means Here

- Connect once; never think about it again.
- The assistant reaches current data without the user staging it.
- Results land **in the tool**, structured, not in a chat window to be transcribed.
- Revocable and inspectable — the user can see what is connected and withdraw it.
- Reaches more than one AI vendor, so choosing an assistant does not mean re-choosing tools.

## The Fit, and Its One Weakness

Remote MCP with OAuth 2.1 fits this persona nearly perfectly, and it is the only mechanism in
the survey that does. Cross-vendor client support means one server implementation reaches
multiple assistants — the property prefill links conspicuously lack.

The weakness is not in the mechanism but in **getting people to this stage.** No one arrives
here. They arrive anonymous, and something has to carry them from first touch to commitment
before the OAuth grant is a reasonable ask. That path runs through the mechanisms this persona
has outgrown.

A tool that offers only the MCP lane is asking strangers for an authorisation grant, which
they will decline. A tool that offers only the clipboard lane never gives this persona a
reason to deepen. **The mechanisms are stages of one relationship, not alternatives** — and
this persona is the destination, not the entry point.

## What Is Still Missing Here

**Discovery.** Even a committed user must find out that the tool offers an MCP endpoint and
connect it manually. There is no standard way for a page to advertise "I have an MCP server,
here it is" that an AI client would recognize and offer to connect.

S2 confirmed this gap is real rather than an oversight: the "Server Cards" capability
discovery claimed for the 2026-07-28 revision does not exist in the specification. The spec's
actual addition, a `server/discover` RPC, is in-band — it answers "what can this server do"
only once the client already holds the URL, which is the step this persona is
missing. A2A publishes signed agent cards at `/.well-known/agent.json`; MCP endpoints have no
out-of-band equivalent.
So the last mile of the best-fitting mechanism in the category is a copy-pasted URL and a
support article — which is a discovery problem, not a protocol one, and the one place this
otherwise well-served persona still relies on the clipboard.

# Persona: The Documentation and Developer-Experience Team

**Stage**: Engaged, unconnected — high intent, no relationship
**Constraint**: The reader is mid-problem and every extra step loses them

---

## Who

The team responsible for a technical product's documentation, error pages, dashboards, and
runbooks. Their readers are developers and operators who arrive **already stuck** — a build
failed, an API returned something unexpected, an alert fired.

They are the most receptive audience in this survey and the least patient. They have a
specific problem, a strong intent to solve it, and no interest whatsoever in the documentation
site as a destination.

## Why the Need Arises

Documentation has always had a translation gap: it describes the general case, and the reader
has a specific one. The reader must map the doc onto their actual repository, their actual
error, their actual configuration — and that mapping is where readers give up.

The reader's own coding agent can close that gap, because it has the context the docs never
will: their code, their logs, their environment. It cannot, however, easily get the docs'
context — the correct procedure, the right flags, the current version's semantics.

**A handoff moves the doc's knowledge to where the reader's context already is.** Rather than
the reader carrying their situation to the documentation, the documentation carries its
procedure to the reader's agent.

The population served is unusually well matched to this category: developers have the highest
rate of paid AI subscriptions and CLI agent installation of any audience. The destination
almost certainly exists.

## Why This Persona Is Distinctive

It is the only persona where **the outbound leg alone is sufficient.**

Everywhere else in this survey, the missing return path is the central complaint. Here it is
not. The reader takes the prompt to their agent, and the work happens *in their repository* —
which is where they wanted it. Nothing needs to come back to the documentation site. The doc
page is not tracking anything, not storing a result, and not continuing a session.

This makes the persona the best-served in the entire category, and it explains why deep links
are documented as a first-class feature for exactly this use case rather than for any of the
others.

## The Mechanism Fit

Deep links to CLI agents are purpose-built here, and the documented use cases name this
persona directly: an incident runbook step that opens the affected service's repo with a
diagnostic prompt; a monitoring alert linking to an investigation prompt for a specific
metric; a README that opens the project with an onboarding prompt; a CI failure notification
pre-filling the failing job's name.

Two documented details matter especially for this reader:

- **The `repo` parameter.** A link can name a GitHub `owner/name` slug and resolve to whichever
  local clone the reader used most recently, rather than requiring an absolute path everyone
  shares. This is what makes a *shared* runbook link work across a team whose clones live in
  different places — the difference between a link that works for its author and one that
  works in every case.
- **Prefill, never send.** The session opens with the prompt staged and a persistent `Prompt
  from an external link` warning. For a reader clicking from an alert or a third-party
  runbook, that review step is the appropriate default.

## What Rules Mechanisms Out

**Anything requiring setup.** The reader is mid-incident. An OAuth flow at that moment is
absurd.

**Anything mobile-first.** This work happens at a workstation with a terminal. Web Share is
irrelevant.

**Anything requiring the docs team to run infrastructure.** Documentation sites are static
builds. An MCP server is a service to operate, which is a different team and a different
budget — an organizational barrier rather than a technical one.

## The Constraint That Bites

**Custom schemes are stripped exactly where developers read.** GitHub renders only `http` and
`https` in READMEs, issues, pull requests, and wikis — a `claude-cli://` link appears as bare
label text with the URL removed entirely.

This is the sharpest instance of the stripping problem in the whole survey, because GitHub is
where a large share of this persona's material lives. The documented workaround is to put the
URL in a code block for the reader to copy manually — which is to say that **for the audience
best served by deep links, the fallback on the most important surface is the clipboard.**

The persona also inherits the 5,000-character ceiling, which bites here specifically:
percent-encoding expands code, stack traces, and configuration far worse than prose, so a
prompt carrying a real error payload hits the wall early and truncates silently. The
documented mitigation is indirect but effective — store the long procedure as a repository
skill and let the link's prompt merely name it, keeping the payload small by moving the
content to where the agent already looks.

## What "Working" Means Here

- One click from stuck to a staged, context-loaded session.
- Correct repository resolution without the reader configuring anything.
- Survives the platforms where technical documentation actually lives.
- The reader sees the prompt before it runs — this audience will not tolerate a page silently
  instructing their agent, and is well equipped to notice.
- No infrastructure for the docs team.

# Persona: The Governed Enterprise

**Stage**: Engaged, but constrained by policy rather than willingness
**Constraint**: Only the sanctioned AI may see the data, and IT must be able to prove it

---

## Who

An organization that has selected and contracted an AI provider — with a data processing
agreement, an approved tenancy, logging, and an acceptable-use policy — and must ensure that
work involving company data goes there and nowhere else.

The people inside it are ordinary employees using web tools during their work. The person
with the requirement is not the employee; it is the security, compliance, or IT function that
must answer for where company data went.

## Why the Need Arises

This is the persona the term "BYOAI" actually denotes in 2026 — the enterprise successor to
BYOD. The documented pattern is consistent: **employees adopt personal AI tools because they
are useful, easy to access, and available long before an official programme exists.** Creating
a personal account takes minutes; getting a company tool approved may take weeks or never
happen.

The result is that AI use is already happening, outside governance, on personal accounts. The
organization's choice is not whether employees use AI but whether the usage is visible and
directed.

For this survey, the relevant consequence is precise. When a third-party web tool offers an AI
affordance, the enterprise needs it to route to **their** sanctioned assistant — not to the
tool's server-side model (which means company data reaches a vendor with no agreement), and
not to whatever personal account the employee happens to be signed into.

**A handoff mechanism is therefore a governance instrument, not a convenience.** It is the
difference between data going somewhere approved and data going somewhere unknown.

## What Rules Mechanisms Out

**Server-side inference by the tool vendor.** The data leaves to an unvetted processor. This
is the thing the persona exists to prevent, and it disqualifies the default architecture of
most AI products outright.

**Anything the organization cannot administer or audit.** Mechanisms with no enterprise
control surface cannot be sanctioned, because the security function cannot make a statement
about them.

**Anything that silently picks a destination.** The organization must know where the data
went. A mechanism that hands to "whatever is installed" is unauditable by construction, which
is an unusual mark against the OS share sheet — the destination-discovery property that helps
every other persona is a liability here, because the user's choice is exactly what policy
needs to constrain.

**Uncontrolled agentic browsing.** An agent reading and acting on arbitrary page content is a
prompt-injection surface, and published research through 2026 has repeatedly demonstrated
agent hijacking via injected instructions in ordinary content. A security function evaluating
this class is evaluating a documented attack surface, not a hypothetical one.

## What "Working" Means Here

- The destination is **the sanctioned assistant**, deterministically.
- IT can **administer** the mechanism — enable, disable, or constrain it centrally.
- Usage is **attributable**: who handed what, to where.
- Employees are not driven back to personal accounts by a policy that makes the sanctioned
  path harder than the unsanctioned one. This is the persona's real failure mode — governance
  that forbids without providing produces exactly the shadow usage it was meant to stop.

## Where the Category Serves This Persona Well

Better than one might expect, and for a specific reason: **the mechanisms with the most
administrative surface are the ones built by vendors selling to enterprises.**

- **Deep link registration is administrable.** Claude Code's handler registration writes only
  to user-level locations, and `disableDeepLinkRegistration` can be set in managed settings so
  users cannot re-enable it. An organization can decide centrally whether custom-scheme
  handoff exists at all.
- **Protocol handler registration is administrable.** Edge exposes a
  `RegisteredProtocolHandlers` policy — which is, tellingly, the main realistic use case for
  a mechanism the open web never adopted.
- **Remote MCP is the strongest fit.** OAuth 2.1 with mandatory PKCE means the connection is
  explicit, scoped, attributable, and revocable — the properties a governance function
  actually needs. The 2026-07-28 hardening (issuer validation per RFC 9207, issuer binding,
  `application_type` declaration at Dynamic Client Registration) is the work an
  enterprise security review would otherwise raise as findings.

**This is the one persona for whom the OAuth connect step is a feature rather than a cost.**
Every other persona in this survey experiences the grant as friction to be minimized. Here the
deliberateness is the point: an explicit, logged, revocable authorisation is what makes the
usage governable.

## Where It Is Served Badly

**Clipboard handoff is ungovernable and unstoppable.** It requires no registration, no
permission beyond a copy gesture, and leaves no administrable surface. An organization cannot
disable it, cannot see it, and cannot direct it. The most universal mechanism in the category
is the one least compatible with this persona's requirements — and it is the one employees
will use if the sanctioned path is harder.

That tension is unresolvable by mechanism choice. It is resolved, if at all, by making the
sanctioned path easier, which puts this persona's fate in the hands of how smooth
the MCP connect step becomes.

**WebMCP offers no enterprise story yet.** Origin scoping via `exposedTo` is a page-author
control, not an organizational one, and there is no administrative policy surface for which
agents may call which tools. Given the published tool-injection results, a security function
evaluating WebMCP today would find measured attack success rates and research-prototype
mitigations — not a posture it can sanction.

# Persona: The Operator With No Inference Budget

**Stage**: Anonymous → engaged
**Constraint**: Every unit of model work must run on someone else's meter

---

## Who

An individual or very small team running a hosted product whose value depends on AI-shaped
work — drafting, analysis, structuring, critique — but whose economics cannot absorb
per-user inference cost.

This includes indie SaaS, tools built by consultants alongside client work, products serving
a niche too small for venture-scale spend, and non-commercial or community tools with no
revenue at all. The common thread is that **the marginal cost of a user must be
approximately zero**, because there is no funding to make it anything else.

## Why the Need Arises

The default architecture for an AI product is to call a model API server-side and bill the
user enough to cover it. That architecture imposes two things a small operator may be unable
to accept.

**The unit economics.** A free tier becomes a metered liability. A generous user costs real
money. Pricing must exceed inference cost plus margin, which sets a floor that may be above
what the niche will pay. The operator is forced into either a paywall the market will not
bear or a burn rate they cannot fund.

**The vendor relationship.** Server-side inference means an API account, key management,
provider rate limits, and exposure to pricing changes decided elsewhere. A pricing change or
a deprecated model becomes an operational emergency for a one-person team with no leverage.

Meanwhile the users **already pay for a frontier model** — often a better one than the
operator could afford to call. The subscription exists; it is idle while the user is on the
operator's page. Routing the work there is not a workaround; it is noticing that the capacity
is already bought and paid for.

There is a secondary effect worth naming: a product with no inference dependency has no model
version to pin, no provider outage to absorb, and no per-user cost curve. **Its operating
profile is that of a static site**, which is what makes it survivable as a side project.

## What Rules Mechanisms Out

**Anything metered.** By definition. This includes on-device browser models only partially:
they are free, but they are the browser vendor's small model, not the frontier model the
user's subscription buys, and they cannot do the work that motivated the product.

**Anything requiring per-vendor negotiation or partnership.** The operator has no leverage to
get listed, integrated, or featured by an AI vendor, and no capacity to maintain several
vendor-specific integrations as parameters drift.

**Anything requiring setup before first value.** This persona's users are frequently arriving
cold, and a product with no marketing budget cannot spend its one moment of user attention on
an authorisation flow.

## What "Working" Means Here

- Marginal cost per user stays at zero, at any scale, including a traffic spike.
- No model API account, no key management, no provider dependency in the critical path.
- Works with whatever AI the user has, and keeps working when they switch.
- Maintainable by one person with no ongoing per-vendor work.
- Degrades to something usable rather than breaking when a vendor changes something.

## The Trajectory This Persona Actually Follows

This is the persona whose needs change most as the relationship deepens, and it is the
clearest case for treating mechanisms as **layered rather than chosen**:

- **First-touch** — clipboard handoff, because nothing else functions for a stranger. This is
  the floor and it must always work.
- **Known vendor** — a prefill deep link once the user has indicated what they use, saving
  the paste step but adding nothing structural.
- **Returning, committed user** — remote MCP with OAuth. The connect step is affordable
  exactly once the relationship justifies it, and it buys the structured return leg the
  clipboard approximates by hand. The 2026-07-28 stateless core matters here specifically:
  it means a small operator can host an MCP endpoint on ordinary infrastructure without
  sticky sessions or shared session stores.

The operator who builds only the MCP lane serves nobody at first touch. The operator who
builds only the clipboard lane never gives committed users anything better. **Neither
mechanism is a substitute for the other**, and this persona is where that is most visible
because a single product spans the whole axis.

## What This Persona Is Missing

The **format churn tax**. With no specification for the handoff payload, this operator
invents an envelope, and when their users' AI clients change behavior — a new system prompt,
a different default output format — the parser breaks with no upstream to track and no
community fix to adopt. A shared convention would convert a private maintenance burden into
someone else's problem, which is worth more to a one-person team than to anyone else in this
survey.

# S3 Synthesis — What the Personas Establish

## The Coverage Map

| Persona | Best-fit mechanism | Return leg served? |
|---|---|---|
| Publisher, cold readers | Clipboard | ❌ Badly — usually one-way |
| Operator with no inference budget | Clipboard → deep link → MCP, layered | ⚠️ Only after commitment |
| Governed enterprise | Remote MCP + OAuth | ✅ Well |
| Docs / developer experience | CLI deep links | ✅ **Not needed** — work lands in the repo |
| Committed workflow user | Remote MCP + OAuth | ✅ Well, but discovery is manual |
| Regulated / local-only | Clipboard, by elimination | ✅ By human, as required |

Three patterns fall out of this, and none of them is "pick a mechanism."

## 1. Relationship Stage Beats Task Type

The personas differ far more by **when in the relationship the handoff happens** than by what
the AI is asked to do. A publisher and a workflow tool might want the same analysis performed;
they cannot use the same mechanism, because one is talking to a stranger and the other to a
committed user.

This validates the axis S2 predicted. It also means **mechanism selection is not a
product decision made once** — a single product spanning first touch to daily use spans the
whole axis, which is why the no-inference-budget operator needs three mechanisms layered
rather than one chosen.

## 2. The Clipboard's Constituency Is Structural, Not Transitional

Three of six personas land on clipboard handoff, and they arrive by **different routes**:

- The publisher, because the reader is anonymous and no other mechanism functions.
- The operator, because it is the only zero-marginal-cost floor that always works.
- The regulated practitioner, because every automated mechanism is disqualified by the
  constraint the persona exists to satisfy.

Only the first of these is a *waiting* problem. The regulated persona cannot be automated away
at all — the automation is the prohibited thing — and the anonymous-first-touch problem is
permanent for as long as strangers exist. **The lane with no specification has the broadest
and most durable constituency in the category**, which is the S3 finding that most sharpens
S1's conclusion.

## 3. The Return Leg Is Where Coverage Fails

Read the right-hand column. The return leg is served well only for personas who have already
paid an authorisation cost, or — in the documentation case — for whom no return is needed
because the work lands in the user's own repository.

For the largest population, the anonymous reader, the return leg is essentially unserved. The
handoff is one-way, the page never learns whether anything happened, and the publisher gets no
signal about whether the affordance is worth maintaining. That absence is self-reinforcing:
without a return signal there is no evidence of value, and without evidence of value there is
no investment in the return leg.

## What This Pass Contributes to the Central Question

S1 concluded that the payload/envelope convention is the unclaimed layer. S3 tests
that against real needs and **strengthens it**:

- The personas with the **largest populations** (anonymous readers) and the **hardest
  constraints** (regulated) are both served by the unspecified lane.
- The personas served well by governed mechanisms are the ones who have **already committed** —
  a minority at any moment, and reached only by traversing the unspecified lane first.
- The one persona that needs no envelope (documentation) is the exception that explains the
  rule: it does not need a return leg, because the work lands where the user already is.

The convention would therefore serve the majority of the category's population at the moment
they are least served — which is the strongest need-side argument available for writing it.

## A Constraint S3 Adds That S2 Did Not

**Human inspectability should be a design requirement of the envelope, not an accident of the
medium.**

S2 derived this weakly, observing that the payload passes a human's screen twice so it must be
legible. S3 makes it a hard requirement from the regulated persona, for whom per-item human
review is a *control* rather than a cost — and notes it costs the other personas little, since
S2 found human review is where the manual mechanisms' safety properties come from in the first
place.

An envelope optimized purely for machine parsing would fail the persona with the least
flexibility and gain little from the others.

## What S3 Hands to S4

1. **The category cannot consolidate onto one mechanism.** Six personas, four distinct
   best-fits, and structural reasons — not maturity gaps — behind each. Any strategic
   prediction of convergence has to explain away the regulated and anonymous constituencies.
2. **Layering is the deliverable.** The question a builder faces is not "which mechanism"
   but "which mechanisms, in what order, as the relationship deepens." That is a design
   pattern, and it has no name or documented reference form.
3. **Endpoint discovery is the unglamorous gap.** Even the best-served persona connects their
   MCP endpoint by copy-pasting a URL from a support article. S2 killed the "Server Cards"
   claim; the spec's `server/discover` RPC helps only once a client already holds the URL.
   A2A's signed agent cards remain the only worked example of **out-of-band** capability
   discovery in the neighborhood. This is a smaller and more tractable gap than the envelope,
   and nobody is working on it either.

# Persona: Regulated, Air-Gapped, and Local-Only

**Stage**: Any — the constraint overrides the relationship
**Constraint**: The data cannot leave, and no vendor may be in the path

---

## Who

Contexts where the material being worked on cannot be sent to a third party under any
commercial arrangement: clinical records, legal privilege, classified or export-controlled
material, unpublished financial data, journalism protecting a source, research under a data
use agreement, and any jurisdiction whose data residency rules the available vendors do not
satisfy.

Also, in a quieter and much larger form: individuals who simply decline to send their material
to a cloud model, and run a local one instead.

## Why the Need Arises

This persona's requirement is the inverse of every other persona's. Others want the handoff to reach
the user's assistant conveniently. This one requires that **the handoff cannot reach anything
the user did not choose** — and needs to be able to demonstrate that, not merely believe it.

The pressure to use AI is identical to everywhere else. The material is complex, the work is
suited to a model, and the practitioner would benefit. What differs is that the ordinary
architecture — a hosted product calling a hosted model — is unavailable regardless of price
or quality.

Local models have become capable enough that this is no longer a hypothetical
trade. The gap between a well-run local model and a frontier model is real but no longer
disqualifying for structured, bounded tasks — which is the shape of task a handoff
carries.

## What Rules Mechanisms Out

**Server-side inference.** Immediately and absolutely.

**Remote MCP as commonly deployed.** Not the protocol — the topology. MCP over the internet
means a network path to a hosted assistant, which is the thing prohibited. The protocol is
fine locally; the usual deployment is not.

**Agentic browsers and cloud AI extensions.** An assistant reading page content and sending it
to a vendor is exactly the prohibited flow, executed automatically and without a per-item
decision.

**Vendor prefill links.** They name a vendor's endpoint. The mechanism *is* the data path to a
third party.

**Anything whose destination is chosen by the OS.** The share sheet's helpfulness is
disqualifying here for the same reason it is for the governed enterprise: the user's
convenience choice is exactly what the constraint must remove.

## What Remains

Very little, which is the finding.

**Clipboard handoff is the mechanism this persona is left with**, and for once its properties
are ideal rather than merely adequate:

- It is **destination-agnostic**. The text goes wherever the user pastes it — a local model, an
  on-premises deployment, an air-gapped workstation, a colleague. The page has no idea and no
  influence.
- It involves **no network path from the page to any AI**. There is nothing to audit because
  there is no connection.
- It is **fully inspectable**. The user reads exactly what is being handed over before it goes
  anywhere. For material under a legal or ethical duty, per-item human review is not friction
  — it is the required control.
- It works with **models that have no integration story at all**, including a locally served
  open-weights model behind no API a page could call.

**On-device browser models are a partial fit with a sharp limit.** Chrome's Prompt API runs
Gemini Nano locally with no data leaving the device, which satisfies the letter of the
constraint. But the model is the browser vendor's, not one the organization vetted or
controls, and a compliance function cannot make assurances about a model it did not select and
cannot version-pin. For individuals it is a real option; for regulated institutions it usually
is not.

## What "Working" Means Here

- **Demonstrably** no data path to an unapproved processor — provable, not asserted.
- The destination is the user's own model, chosen by them, possibly with no network at all.
- Per-item human review is preserved rather than automated away.
- No dependency on any vendor's continued existence, pricing, or terms.

## Why This Persona Matters to the Category

It is small in population and disproportionately important to the survey's conclusions, for
two reasons.

**It is the persona that cannot be automated away.** Every other persona in this survey might
eventually be served by agent-attached browsing, if that future arrives. This one cannot be:
the automation is the prohibited thing. **Whatever else happens to the category, the manual,
inspectable, destination-agnostic lane retains a constituency that will not migrate.**

**It reframes the clipboard's weaknesses as its specification.** Elsewhere the manual steps
read as an unfinished implementation. Here they are the control: human review per item, no
network path, no vendor in the loop, no automatic destination. The mechanism the category
treats as its floor is this persona's ceiling.

That is a strong argument that the envelope convention should be designed to preserve human
inspection rather than to optimize it away — a design constraint that serves this persona
directly and costs the others little, since S2 found the human review step is where the other
mechanisms' safety properties come from too.

# S4 Approach — Strategic Viability

**Survey**: 2.078
**Pass**: S4 Strategic Selection
**Date**: 2026-08-04

## The Question S4 Answers

S1 mapped the mechanisms, S2 analyzed their architecture, S3 established who needs them. S4
asks the question a builder actually faces: **which of these is safe to build on for the next
three to five years, and which will strand you?**

This category makes that question unusually sharp, for a reason that belongs up front.

## Why Normal Viability Analysis Underperforms Here

The usual signals — stars, release cadence, corporate backing, adoption curves — are weak
predictors in this category, because the category's history is short and violent. In the six
months preceding this pass:

- WebMCP was **announced, shipped, renamed, and partially deprecated** (February to July 2026).
- MCP landed its **largest revision since launch**, removing the session model entirely.
- A major vendor **launched and killed** a dedicated agentic browser — Atlas, announced
  October 2025, retired 9 July 2026, service ending 9 August 2026.

Corporate backing did not save Atlas. Standards-body participation did not stop WebMCP's API
from being renamed within five months. A dated specification did not stop MCP from removing
its own handshake.

So S4 evaluates on **structural** grounds instead: does the mechanism's position depend on a
decision someone else has yet to make, and what happens to it if that decision goes the other
way? A mechanism whose survival requires a specific actor to do a specific thing is a bet on
that actor. A mechanism whose properties follow from the medium is not.

## The Evaluation Frame

Each trajectory file asks four questions:

1. **What must be true** for this mechanism to matter in three years?
2. **Who decides** that, and do they have an incentive?
3. **What is the failure mode** — does it degrade, or strand you?
4. **What does adopting it cost if the bet loses?**

The fourth question does most of the work. In a category moving this fast, the dominant
consideration is not which mechanism wins but **how much you lose by being wrong**, and the
mechanisms differ enormously on that axis while looking superficially comparable.

# Strategic Outlook: The Clipboard Lane and the Envelope Convention

**The unspecified floor** — most durable mechanism, least likely to be standardized

---

## Two Different Bets

This file covers a mechanism and a proposal, and they have opposite risk profiles:

- **The clipboard lane** — will it still matter in three years? This is a low-risk assessment.
- **The envelope convention** — will anyone write it, and does it succeed if written? This is
  the uncertain question in the survey.

## The Clipboard Lane Is Structurally Durable

Its position does not depend on any actor's decision, which is unique in this category. It
rests on properties of the medium:

- **Vendor-neutral.** Works with any AI, including ones not yet released and local models with
  no integration surface.
- **Zero setup.** The only mechanism serving an anonymous first-time visitor *and* returning a
  result.
- **No payload ceiling.** Raw text — immune to the percent-encoding expansion and silent
  truncation that bound every scheme-based mechanism, and immune for the structured
  payloads a handoff most wants to carry.
- **Universally available.** Unaffected by engine support, origin trials, or spec churn. It is
  the documented fallback when renderers strip custom schemes.

S3 found three of six personas landing here by three *different* routes — anonymity,
zero-marginal-cost, and regulatory prohibition. Only the first is a waiting problem. **The
regulated persona cannot be automated away, because the automation is the prohibited thing**,
and anonymous first touch is permanent for as long as strangers exist.

Even the maximal agentic-browsing future does not close this. An agent-attached user is served
by WebMCP-shaped interfaces; every other visitor still needs a floor.

**Three-year outlook: the lane persists, unquestionably.** The interesting question is whether
it stays unspecified.

## Will the Envelope Convention Get Written?

The requirements are stable and small — version marker, kind discriminator, correlation
identifier, tolerant parsing, human-legible surface. S2 derived them from the medium's
constraints; S3 confirmed them against real needs and added human inspectability as a hard
requirement. This is microformat-sized work, not protocol-sized.

**The case for it being written:**

- The requirements are stable under independent derivation, which is what a ready problem
  looks like.
- It would improve two mechanisms at once — the clipboard lane directly, and Web Share's
  standardized-but-empty `text` field by giving it something meaningful to carry. Upgrading a
  W3C transport by specifying its payload is a elegant position.
- It serves the largest population (anonymous readers) at the moment they are least served.
- The barrier to writing it is a document, not an implementation. No browser has to ship
  anything.

**The case against — and it is strong:**

**Nobody owns it, and nobody's incentives point at it.** Every other mechanism here has a
vendor or a browser engine behind it. This one has whoever writes it down. AI vendors want
handoffs to *their* client, not a vendor-neutral envelope that makes assistants
interchangeable. Browser vendors are occupied with WebMCP. Page authors each want their own
handoff working, not an interoperable one — the benefit of a shared format accrues to the
ecosystem while the cost falls on the first adopter.

**Conventions with no adoption incentive do not spread**, and this survey contains two cautionary
cases sitting right next to each other:

- **`registerProtocolHandler`** — standardized in the WHATWG HTML Standard, available
  for over a decade, and the `web+` namespace is *still empty* because no vendor had a reason to
  claim it. Specification quality was never the obstacle.
- **`llms.txt`** — an individual proposal that gained real traction (8.7% of the top 1,000
  sites by June 2026, ~10% in another study) and still shows no measurable effect. SE Ranking's
  XGBoost analysis found that *removing* the `llms.txt` variable **improved** prediction
  accuracy — the file added noise rather than signal — and fewer than 1% of sites actually cited
  in AI results had one.

`llms.txt` is the sharper warning of the two, because it *succeeded* at the thing an envelope
convention would aim for: it got written, publicised, and adopted by thousands of sites. It
still did not work, because adoption by publishers does not create consumption by AI clients.

**An envelope convention faces the identical structure.** A page can emit a well-formed
envelope; whether the model on the other side respects it depends on that vendor's system
prompt and training, which no convention controls. This is the specific reason tolerant parsing
is a requirement rather than a nicety — the convention cannot compel the far end, only make
recognition likely.

## What Success Would Actually Require

Not a specification. **A consuming implementation on the AI side** — at least one major client
recognising the envelope and honouring the requested return shape. That is the same demand-side
dependency that has WebMCP stalled at zero consumers with two browser vendors behind it.

The realistic path is narrower and more promising than a general standard: a convention that
works **without** far-end cooperation, by being robust to models that ignore it. Tolerant
parsing, a correlation token that survives paraphrase, and a structure legible enough that a
human carrying it will not mangle it. That is achievable unilaterally, which is exactly why it
is the version worth building.

## Failure Mode and Cost of Being Wrong

**The cheapest work in the survey, and the least losable.**

An operator who defines an envelope for their own handoff gets working paste-back regardless of
whether anyone else adopts it. If the convention spreads, they were early. If it does not, they
still have a functioning feature — the format was never load-bearing on adoption.

Contrast MCP (expensive to build, cheap to be wrong about) and WebMCP (cheap to build, useless
if consumers never arrive). The envelope is cheap to build **and** useful alone. That asymmetry
is the strongest strategic argument for doing the work, and it holds whether or not it is ever
published as a convention.

## Three-Year Outlook

**Highly likely**: the clipboard lane persists as the category's floor, and implementations
remain mutually incompatible.

**Possible (≈30%)**: a convention emerges from a builder or small group, gets documented, and
achieves partial adoption among page authors — `llms.txt`-shaped success, meaning visible
adoption with unproven effect.

**Unlikely (≈10%)**: a body charters it, or a major AI vendor commits to honouring it. Either
would change the picture completely; neither has any current advocate.

## Strategic Position

**Build the envelope for yourself. Publish it if you like. Do not expect the ecosystem to
adopt it, and do not build anything that depends on adoption.**

The lane is durable, the work is cheap, the benefit is immediate and unilateral, and the
convention's spread is a nice-to-have rather than a prerequisite. That combination is rare
enough in this category to be worth acting on — and it is the opposite of the posture the
surrounding mechanisms reward.

## A Note on Prior-Art Claims

Anyone documenting or publicising a convention here should state its novelty narrowly. The
defensible claim is that **no one has specified the payload convention for a text-carried task
and its return envelope across arbitrary AI clients** — which survives the searches in this
survey. The broad claim that page-to-AI communication is unexamined does not survive contact
with WebMCP, MCP, A2A, or schema.org's `potentialAction`, all of which are named, governed, and
active. See the S1 recommendation for the full prior-art record.

# Strategic Outlook: Remote MCP

**The structured lane** — most likely to persist, permanently bounded reach

---

## What Must Be True

Almost nothing. This is the most secure position in the category, and the reasons are already
established rather than pending.

MCP has cross-vendor client consumption today — Claude's clients consume it, and OpenAI
documents MCP and connectors as a supported integration path. It has a dated revision history,
a public SEP process, a working group, and a release-candidate period. The 2026-07-28 revision
was the largest since launch and shipped on schedule after an RC locked 21 May 2026.

Nothing about MCP's near-term relevance depends on a decision someone has yet to make. That
alone distinguishes it from every other mechanism surveyed.

## The Direction of Travel Is Legible

The 2026-07-28 changes point consistently in one direction: **making MCP cheaper to operate
and safer to expose.**

The stateless core removed the `initialize`/`initialized` handshake and `Mcp-Session-Id`,
replacing session negotiation with header-based dispatch. Servers that needed sticky sessions,
shared session stores, and deep packet inspection at the gateway now run behind a plain
round-robin load balancer.

Strategically this is a **participation** change, not a performance one. It lowers the cost of
hosting an MCP endpoint to roughly that of a normal stateless HTTP service, which brings small
operators into the addressable set — the group that previously could not justify the
operational overhead.

The authorization hardening points the same way. Six SEPs on issuer validation (RFC 9207),
`application_type` declaration at Dynamic Client Registration, and issuer binding are
authorization-server-confusion defences. Specs get that work when they are deployed in
adversarial multi-tenant settings, which is a maturity signal that cannot be faked.

MCP Apps and Tasks extend what the lane can carry — server-rendered UI inside the client, and
work that outlives a request. Both are the moves of a protocol expanding into production
workloads rather than one searching for a use case.

## The Governance Caveat

MCP is **vendor-originated**. It came from Anthropic and evolves through a process Anthropic
convenes. The SEP process is public, the working group is real, and the revisions are dated —
this is better practice than most vendor specs — but it is not multi-stakeholder governance in
the W3C or IETF sense.

For a builder this matters less than it might, because the practical risk is not capture but
**pace**. MCP revises fast and removes things: `tasks/list` was deleted, the session model was
deleted. Implementations carry migration debt as a matter of course. Budget for it.

## The Permanent Boundary

**Remote MCP cannot serve an anonymous first-time visitor**, and no amount of specification
work will change that.

Dynamic Client Registration removed the administrative prerequisite. What remains is the
**authorization grant** — a human deciding, in an authorization-server UI, that this client may
act for them. That is not latency; it is the security property being purchased. Every
2026-07-28 change makes the step smoother and none makes it optional, because a version without
a deliberate user decision would not be OAuth.

S1 framed this as the survey's open question — temporary friction or permanent floor. **S4's
answer is floor.** The evidence is the direction of the spec's own work: it optimizes the grant
relentlessly and never questions it.

The strategic consequence is that MCP's addressable population is **committed users only** —
S3's governed enterprise and committed workflow personas. That is a valuable population and a
bounded one, and it is reached only by traversing mechanisms MCP does not provide.

## Failure Mode and Cost of Being Wrong

**Expensive to adopt, cheap to be wrong about.** An unusual and favorable combination.

Adoption costs real work: a server to build, host, and operate, plus OAuth 2.1 with PKCE, plus
tracking a fast-moving spec. This is not a weekend addition, and the stateless core reduced but
did not eliminate it.

But the bet is nearly unlosable in the relevant sense. MCP already has multi-vendor
consumption, so its value does not depend on a future adoption event. If MCP were somehow
displaced, the work — a documented, authenticated, structured API over your own service — is
the most portable asset in this survey. It would be re-skinned to whatever replaced it, not
discarded.

Compare the alternatives: prefill links strand you when a parameter changes; WebMCP strands you
if consumers never arrive. MCP's failure mode is *rework*, not *loss*.

## The Gap That Persists

**Endpoint discovery** — and note the narrowing, because the final spec partly closed the
adjacent gap. S2 killed the "Server Cards" claim, and the 2026-07-28 release introduces no
discovery at a well-known URL, referencing `.well-known` only for OIDC discovery of
authorization servers (SEP-2351). It *does* add a **`server/discover`** RPC (SEP-2575), so a
client holding an endpoint URL can now interrogate its versions, capabilities, and identity
up front.

What remains missing is the step before that: **how a client comes to hold the URL at all.**
A committed user must still learn the endpoint exists and connect it manually, typically by
copy-pasting from a support article. A2A publishes signed agent cards at
`/.well-known/agent.json` and has done since v1.0; MCP has no out-of-band equivalent.

This is a smaller and more tractable gap than the envelope convention, it has an obvious
existing model to copy in A2A, and nobody is working on it. It is the most likely place for the
next meaningful improvement in this lane, and worth watching in future revisions.

## Three-Year Outlook

**Highly likely**: MCP remains the structured integration standard for AI clients, continues
revising quickly, and gains capability discovery in some form. Reach stays bounded to
authenticated relationships.

**Possible**: governance broadens to a foundation, following A2A's move to the Linux
Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation. This would strengthen the position without changing the
technical picture.

**Unlikely**: displacement. Cross-vendor consumption plus an active spec process is a strong
incumbency, and no competing structured protocol has comparable client reach.

## Strategic Position

**Build it when you have committed users; do not expect it to reach anyone else.**

Remote MCP is the correct terminus of a layered strategy and the wrong entry point. The
operator who builds only this lane is asking strangers for an authorisation grant they will
decline — and will conclude, wrongly, that the demand is not there.

# S4 Synthesis — Strategic Recommendation

**Survey**: 2.078 — Client-Side AI Handoff
**Pass**: S4 Strategic Selection
**Verified**: 2026-08-04

---

## The Risk Matrix

The mechanisms differ far more in **cost of being wrong** than in capability, and that is the
axis a builder should actually decide on:

| Mechanism | Cost to adopt | Cost if the bet loses | Depends on someone else's decision? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clipboard + envelope | Low | **Near zero** — works alone regardless of adoption | **No** |
| Web Share | Very low | Near zero — degrades to nothing | No |
| Prefill deep links | Low | Low but recurring; fails **silently** | Yes — per-vendor, ungoverned |
| WebMCP | Low | Near zero — unused registration code | **Yes** — no consuming clients exist |
| Remote MCP | **High** | Low — the work is portable | No — already multi-vendor |
| Agentic browsers | n/a | n/a — nothing to build | Entirely |

Two rows do the strategic work.

**The clipboard lane is the only mechanism that is both durable and unilateral.** Its value does
not depend on any vendor, standards body, or adoption event. Everything else here is either
someone else's decision or expensive.

**Remote MCP inverts the usual pattern**: expensive to adopt, cheap to be wrong about. The work
— a documented, authenticated, structured API over your own service — is the most portable asset
in the survey. Its failure mode is rework, not loss.

## The Recommendation: Layer, Do Not Choose

No mechanism spans the relationship axis, and S3 confirmed this is structural rather than a
maturity gap. The strategic answer is a **stack ordered by relationship stage**, built from the
bottom:

**1. Clipboard handoff with a defined envelope — the floor.**
Build first, always. It is the only mechanism serving an anonymous visitor *and* returning a
result, the only one immune to payload ceilings and renderer stripping, and the documented
fallback when custom schemes are stripped. Define the envelope — version marker, kind
discriminator, correlation identifier, tolerant parsing, human-legible surface — for your own
use, without waiting for a convention. Design for **human inspectability**, which S3 established
as a hard requirement from the regulated persona and which S2 showed is where the manual
mechanisms' safety properties come from anyway.

**2. Prefill deep links — the accelerator.**
Add once the user's vendor is known. Saves a paste step; adds nothing structural. Never the only
path, because parameters change without deprecation and failures are silent. Re-verify
periodically. Prefer surfaces that prefill rather than auto-submit.

**3. Web Share on mobile — the destination-discovery win.**
A few lines behind a capability check. Solves "which AI does this user use" by delegating to the
OS. Accept that nothing comes back.

**4. WebMCP read-only tools — the cheap option on an uncertain future.**
Register read-only tools with narrow `exposedTo` scoping. Near-zero downside, largest upside in
the category. Do not architect around it, do not describe it to users as working, and treat the
incremental `registerTool()`/`unregisterTool()` API as the riskier half given the published
100%-success registration races.

**5. Remote MCP with OAuth 2.1 — the terminus.**
Build when you have committed users. The connect step is a floor, not friction: it is the
security property being purchased, and every 2026-07-28 change makes it smoother while none
makes it optional. Do not expect it to reach anyone at first touch.

**The ordering is the recommendation.** A builder starting at step 5 asks strangers for an
authorisation grant, gets declined, and concludes wrongly that the demand is not there. A builder
who stops at step 1 never gives committed users anything better.

## What the Category's Own History Teaches

Three lessons, each with a concrete case in this survey:

**Specification quality does not produce adoption.** `registerProtocolHandler` has been in the
WHATWG HTML Standard for over a decade with an empty `web+` namespace, because the registrant
must be a destination and no AI vendor has a reason to claim a generic scheme. The recurring gap
in this category is **not the absence of a transport but the absence of anyone with an incentive
to be the destination.**

**Publisher adoption is not consumption.** `llms.txt` reached 8.7–10% of top sites and still
measures as noise rather than signal — SE Ranking's analysis found that removing it *improved*
prediction accuracy. Any convention here faces the same structure: a page can emit it; whether
the far end honours it is not the convention's to decide.

**Corporate backing does not confer durability.** Atlas launched October 2025, was retired 9 July
2026, and stops working 9 August 2026. WebMCP was announced, shipped, renamed, and partially
deprecated inside five months. Evaluate on structure, not on who is behind it.

## Signals to Monitor

In descending order of how much each would change the picture:

1. **A mainstream agent client announcing it consumes `document.modelContext`.** Single most
   consequential event available. Currently zero.
2. **Confirmation of auto-submit behavior in any major vendor's prefill parameter.** A
   user-safety difference, not a detail — the difference between a page suggesting a prompt and
   a page speaking as the user.
3. **MCP gaining capability discovery at a well-known URL.** Would close the last-mile gap for
   the best-served persona. S2 confirmed it is absent; A2A's signed agent cards are the obvious
   model.
4. **Any AI vendor claiming a shared `web+` scheme, or committing to honour an envelope
   convention.** Either would resolve a coordination failure a decade old.
5. **WebMCP tool-injection mitigations entering the specification** — origin-bound immutable
   tool IDs, lifecycle validation, registration logs. Currently research prototypes; likely a
   precondition for consumer adoption.

## Refresh Posture

**This survey should be flagged S-tier on the next freshness sweep regardless of age.** The
category moved further in the six months before this pass than most 2.xxx neighbors move in
three years, and several load-bearing facts have short half-lives: the WebMCP origin trial ends
at Chrome 156, Atlas service ends 9 August 2026, and vendor prefill parameters change without
deprecation cycles.

Four claims remain **unresolved** and should lead the next pass: ChatGPT and Gemini prefill
parameters and submit semantics; whether `claude.ai/new?q=` still functions on the web; which AI
clients register as OS share targets and by which platform mechanism; and whether any mainstream
agent has begun consuming `document.modelContext`.

## Final Position

**The transports are solved; the payload semantics are not.**

Every mechanism surveyed here — vendor prefill URLs, the clipboard, Web Share Target,
`registerProtocolHandler`, remote MCP, WebMCP — answers the question of *how bytes move*.
None of them answers *what shape the bytes are in*. There is no convention for how a page
states a task so that an arbitrary model returns something the page can parse back, and no
body in this category has taken that layer up.

That has a consequence for anyone choosing a mechanism today: **the envelope is yours to
define whichever transport you pick**, and defining it well is independent of every pending
decision above it. A convention that is defined, tolerantly parsed and human-legible works
under the clipboard lane now and converges toward a standard later if one appears.

The second durable finding is the asymmetry between the legs. The **inbound** leg — an agent
already attached to the browser, calling tools a page exposes — acquired its first mainstream
consumer during this survey's lifetime and is being actively built by browser and model
vendors. The **outbound** leg — a page handing work to an agent the visitor holds, where the
page has no agent attached to begin with — is served by no mechanism here, and WebMCP's own
explainer does not attempt it. For the anonymous reader, the largest population in S3, the
return leg is essentially unserved, and the absence is self-reinforcing: without a return
signal there is no evidence of value, and without evidence of value there is no investment in
the return leg.

Those two facts — an unclaimed payload layer and an unserved outbound leg — are what a reader
should carry out of this survey. What to *do* about them depends on what you are building,
and this survey does not presume to know.

# Strategic Outlook: Vendor Conventions and Ambient Agents

**Prefill links, share sheets, protocol handlers, agentic browsers** — the mechanisms you do
not control

---

## Prefill URLs and Custom Schemes

**What must be true**: that vendors keep shipping deep-link parameters. They currently do, and
the desktop and CLI surfaces are documented and conservative.

**The structural weakness**: no governance, no deprecation cycle, no cross-vendor alignment.
Parameters appear and disappear between product releases. The web surfaces are the volatile
ones — the reported removal of `claude.ai/new?q=` around October 2025 is the pattern, whether
or not that specific report is accurate.

**Direction of travel is favorable but narrow.** The documented Anthropic surfaces have moved
toward *more* safety, not less: prefill-never-send, a persistent `Prompt from an external link`
warning, review escalation above 1,000 characters, path sanitisation, user-level-only handler
registration, and an administrable `disableDeepLinkRegistration` switch. That is a vendor
treating untrusted-page-composed prompts as a design problem, and it is the closest thing to a
reference design the category has.

**But it is one vendor's product decision, not a norm.** The *reported* auto-submit behavior
elsewhere is the counter-example, and if accurate it removes every one of those protections at
once — the difference between a page suggesting a prompt and a page speaking as the user.
Confirming that remains the highest-value open verification in this survey.

**Cost of being wrong**: low but recurring. A changed parameter breaks a button. The fix is
cheap; the *detection* is not, because a stripped or dead deep link fails silently. Budget for
periodic re-verification, and never make a prefill link the only path.

**Three-year outlook**: persists, stays ungoverned, stays per-vendor. No vendor has an
incentive to standardize a mechanism whose whole purpose is routing users to their own client.
Treat as a **convenience layer over a working fallback**, never as infrastructure.

---

## Web Share and Share Targets

**The most under-exploited standard in the category.** A genuine W3C specification, OS-integrated,
long predating the AI cycle, that solves the hardest unsolved problem in the outbound leg —
*which AI does this user actually use* — by making it the operating system's problem instead of
the page's.

**What must be true**: nothing. It already works. It is stable, standardized, and not subject to
the churn afflicting everything else here.

**Why it is not enough**: it carries no semantics — three untyped string fields, with no way to
say "this is a task" or "here is the shape I want back" — and it is one-way by construction.
`navigator.share()` resolves on hand-off, not completion, and the page never learns which target
was chosen. Desktop coverage is the weak flank.

**The strategic observation**: Web Share is a finished transport with an unspecified payload.
The envelope convention and Web Share are **complementary**, not alternatives — specifying what
goes in `text` would upgrade a standardized, OS-integrated, vendor-neutral transport at zero
implementation cost to browsers. Nothing else in this survey offers that leverage, and it is the
strongest technical argument for the envelope work.

**Cost of being wrong**: near zero. It is a few lines behind a capability check, degrading to
nothing on unsupported platforms.

**Three-year outlook**: stable and unchanged. Web Share will not gain AI-specific semantics —
it is a general-purpose sharing spec and there is no constituency for narrowing it.

---

## `registerProtocolHandler` and `web+`

**The category's cautionary tale, and it should be read as one.**

Genuinely standardized in the WHATWG HTML Standard, available for well over a decade, secure-
context-gated, permission-prompted, enterprise-administrable via Edge's `RegisteredProtocolHandlers`
policy. Not Baseline — Safari does not support it — but the technical picture was never the
obstacle.

**Why it failed**: the registrant must be the *destination*, and no AI vendor has any incentive
to claim a generic `web+` scheme it does not control. A decade of availability produced an
**empty namespace**.

**The strategic lesson generalises to everything else here**: the recurring gap in this category
is **not the absence of a transport but the absence of anyone with an incentive to be the
destination.** A new specification does not fix that. A vendor deciding to claim the namespace
would, and none has.

Anyone proposing a new scheme-based convention for AI handoff is proposing something the web
platform has supported for ten years and nobody adopted.

**Three-year outlook**: unchanged. Not worth building on outside managed enterprise deployments,
where the administrative policy surface is the actual use case.

---

## Agentic Browsers and Extensions

**The substitute good** — and the category's main long-term uncertainty.

If agent-attached browsing becomes ordinary, the outbound leg loses most of its motivation: the
page stops needing to push work anywhere because the AI is already reading the page.

**What must be true**: that this becomes how people browse by default, rather than a power-user
tool. Two forces cut against it.

**The Atlas retirement is hard evidence.** OpenAI announced on 9 July 2026 that it is retiring
the standalone ChatGPT Atlas browser — nine months after its October 2025 launch — with service
ending 9 August 2026. A well-resourced vendor built a dedicated agentic browser, ran it for nine
months, and withdrew it.

The survivors avoided asking users to switch browsers: an extension on someone else's browser
(Claude for Chrome, behind existing Pro subscriptions), the incumbent's own integration (Gemini
in Chrome, announced sweepingly at Google I/O 2026), and a free challenger (Perplexity Comet,
paywall removed in late 2025, free across macOS, Windows, Android, and iOS). **The
browser-replacement route has already failed once**; the extension and incumbent-integration
routes are the live ones.

**Security is the second brake.** An agent reading and acting on arbitrary page content is a
prompt-injection surface by construction — the risk WebMCP's browser mediation, character
budgets, and confirmation primitive exist to contain, and which published research has repeatedly
demonstrated against production agents.

**The strategic point for a builder**: none of this is a mechanism you can target. There is no
spec, no detection, no invocation, no return path. A page cannot tell an agentic browser is
present and cannot hand it a structured task. The page's only lever is **being legible** — clean
semantics, readable content, and WebMCP tools that only a narrow set of agents currently calls.

**Cost of being wrong**: zero, because there is nothing to build. This is a scenario to monitor,
not an option to adopt.

**Three-year outlook**: growing but not default. The extension and incumbent-integration forms
persist; dedicated agentic browsers stay niche after Atlas. Even in the strong-growth case, the
transition runs through WebMCP-shaped interfaces — and the consumer gap means it has not started.

---

## Cross-Cutting Conclusion

None of these four is a foundation.

Prefill links are a convenience over a fallback. Web Share is a transport waiting for a payload
convention. Protocol handlers are a decade-old lesson in incentives. Agentic browsers are a
scenario, not a mechanism.

What they share is that **the page does not control any of them.** Each depends on a vendor, an
OS, or a user decision made elsewhere. That is why the layered posture in the S4
recommendation puts the mechanisms the page *does* control — the clipboard lane and its envelope
— at the bottom of the stack, holding everything else up.

# Strategic Outlook: WebMCP

**The standards bet** — highest ceiling in the category, entirely unresolved demand side

---

## What Must Be True

WebMCP matters in three years if and only if **mainstream agent clients consume
`document.modelContext`**.

Everything else about it is on track. Two browser vendors author it, one ships it in an origin
trial (Chrome 149–156), a second supports it behind a flag, the other two engines participate
in the discussion, and there is a growing body of implementation tutorials and security
research. The supply side is functioning.

The demand side was at zero when this pass was written. As of 2026-08-04, **no mainstream
agent client called `modelContext` tools** — Claude, ChatGPT Agent, Perplexity and Gemini all
read pages the old way, and Google had said Gemini in Chrome would be the first consuming
agent with no timeline attached.

> **Superseded 2026-09-07.** ChatGPT Desktop now consumes WebMCP, per the specification's own
> `implementation-status.md`, and Brave's Leo has experimental support. The analysis below is
> preserved as written because its reasoning about *why* the demand side lagged is what makes
> the reversal legible — but read it knowing the premise expired in about a month. The revised
> position is at the end of this file.

This is an unusual failure shape. Web platform features normally fail because implementers
do not ship them. Here the implementation shipped and **the consumers have not arrived** — a
supply-demand mismatch rather than an adoption curve, and the two look identical from the
outside for a while.

## Who Decides, and Do They Have an Incentive?

**Google decides**, more than anyone. It authors the spec, ships the implementation, and owns
the agent most likely to consume it first. If Gemini in Chrome consumes WebMCP tools, the
mechanism has a demand side and the calculus changes across the category.

The incentive is real but not overwhelming. Agents that read pages by scraping work *acceptably*
today — imperfectly, expensively, and fragilely, but they work, and they work on the entire web
rather than on the small fraction of sites that would register tools. An agent vendor gains
reliability on adopting sites and gains nothing everywhere else. That is a real but bounded
payoff, which is consistent with a shipped spec and no announced consumer.

**Anthropic's silence is the signal to watch.** It created the upstream MCP protocol and has
made no public statement about WebMCP since the August 2025 proposal, despite WebMCP borrowing
MCP's primitives. Anthropic ships an agentic browser extension — a natural WebMCP consumer —
and has said nothing. Whatever the reason, a second major agent vendor committing to consume
would move this from a Google bet to an ecosystem one.

## The Security Question Is Not Settled

The published Mid-Session Tool Injection research (NYCU, June 2026) is the most serious
obstacle to consumer adoption, and it is easy to underrate as an academic finding.

Tool hijacking reaches **94–100%** success; registration races reach **100%**. Tool framing
reaches 36–85% while **preserving task completion 81–85% of the time** — the user gets what
they asked for while data leaves. The threat model is an ordinary compromised third-party
script: a CDN asset or an ad SDK, which is the standard supply-chain surface of a normal web
page, not an exotic scenario.

An agent vendor deciding whether to consume WebMCP is deciding whether to let arbitrary pages
inject tool definitions into their agent's context. The published numbers say that decision
currently carries a measured, high-success attack surface whose mitigations are research
prototypes rather than spec features. **That is a coherent reason to wait**, and it may
explain the silence better than indifference does.

The mitigating direction is real too: origin-bound immutable tool IDs, lifecycle validation,
and registration logs reportedly drove attack success to zero without harming legitimate
completion. The fixes are known. They are not yet in the specification.

## Failure Mode and Cost of Being Wrong

**This is the cheapest bet in the category to lose**, which is the single most important
strategic fact about it.

Adopting WebMCP means declaring tools the page already implements as functions. If no agent
ever calls them, the page has some unused registration code and loses nothing — no
infrastructure, no ongoing cost, no user-facing commitment, no migration debt beyond the
already-required `navigator` → `document` move.

There is no lock-in either. The tools are the page's own functions; only the registration
surface is WebMCP-specific.

The genuine risk is not wasted effort but **premature exposure**: registering tools that
mutate state, on a page loading third-party scripts, before the injection mitigations exist.
That risk is avoidable by scope — expose read-only tools, set `exposedTo` narrowly, and treat
the incremental `registerTool()`/`unregisterTool()` API as the riskier half.

## Three-Year Outlook

**Most likely (≈50%)**: Gemini in Chrome consumes WebMCP; adoption becomes real but
concentrated in Google's ecosystem; other vendors follow slowly or partially; the spec stays
at Community Group status for some time yet.

**Plausible (≈30%)**: the demand side never materialises meaningfully. WebMCP joins
`registerProtocolHandler` as a well-specified capability the ecosystem declined to use — and
this survey has that precedent sitting right next to it, which is why the probability is not
lower.

**Less likely (≈20%)**: broad multi-vendor consumption, standards-track promotion, and WebMCP
becomes the default way pages talk to agents. This requires the security work to land first.

## Strategic Position — revised 2026-09-07

**The August position was: adopt cheaply, expect nothing, revisit on one specific signal —
"a mainstream agent client announcing it consumes `document.modelContext`."**

**That signal fired, about a month later.** The specification's own
`implementation-status.md` now records WebMCP support in **ChatGPT Desktop**, experimental
support in **Brave's Leo**, and live origin trials in **Chrome 149 and Edge 150**. The
August text — "every WebMCP implementation guide describes a capability with no consumers" —
is no longer true.

The original wording is preserved above rather than overwritten, because a strategic call
that named its own falsifier and was falsified inside a month is more useful to a reader
than a clean revision would be. It is also the strongest available argument for this
survey's `decay_class: fast`.

**Revised: adopt read-only tools, expect narrow reach, and re-check the reach rather than
the existence.** The question is no longer *whether* anything consumes WebMCP but *how many
of your visitors* are running something that does — which today means a specific desktop
client or an origin-trial browser build. For an audience of developers that is a real
number; for a general audience it is close to zero.

The next signal worth monitoring has moved accordingly: **a shipping, non-trial browser
enabling it by default**, or **Firefox or Safari moving off a standards-position issue**.

Critically, **WebMCP does not address the outbound leg** and never will. Even complete success
leaves the category's central gap untouched, because a page whose visitor has no agent
attached still has no one to hand work to.
